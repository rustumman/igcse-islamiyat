2.2 · The Compilation of the Qur’an under the Khalifas

632 – 656 CE · Madinah · from memorised recitation to a single standard book

The Qur’an was complete — every verse revealed — the moment the Prophet died in 11 AH. It was not, however, a book. It lived in the memories of hundreds of companions and on scattered fragments of bone, bark and parchment. Two Caliphs, a generation apart, turned that living memory into the single fixed text every printed Qur’an in the world today still matches, letter for letter.

□ Predict before you read

Two separate compilation efforts happened within about fifteen years of each other — one under Abu Bakr, one under ’Uthman. If the Qur’an had already been written down once under Abu Bakr, why would ’Uthman need to do the job again? Write a guess. The answer is not “the first copy was lost or wrong” — hold that thought.

Preserved twice over, from the very first day

During the Prophet's own lifetime the Qur’an was already being preserved through two parallel, deliberate methods, not left to chance.

The first was memorisation. Companions known as the huffaz (those who had memorised the entire text) numbered in the hundreds by the time of the Prophet's death, and the Prophet himself reviewed the whole revealed Qur’an with Jibreel once every Ramadan — and, significantly, twice in the final Ramadan of his life, which companions later understood as a sign his death was near.

The second was writing. The Prophet appointed dedicated Scribes of the Revelation (Kuttab al-Wahy) — among them a young Ansari companion named Zayd ibn Thabit, who will matter enormously later in this lesson — to record each verse as it was revealed, on whatever material was to hand: palm-leaf stalks, thin flat stones, shoulder blades of animals, pieces of leather and parchment. What did not yet exist was a single bound volume gathering every verse in one place, in a fixed order, between two covers. There was a simple reason for that: while the Prophet was alive, revelation was still arriving, and verses could still be abrogated or reordered by divine instruction (naskh) — binding it into one finished book before revelation itself was finished would have made no sense.

The first compilation — Abu Bakr, 12 AH

Within a year of the Prophet's death, the new Muslim state faced a crisis. Several Arab tribes rebelled against Madinah and rallied around a rival claimant to prophethood, Musaylimah. Caliph Abu Bakr sent an army to confront him at the Battle of Yamama (12 AH / 633 CE). The rebellion was crushed — but a large number of the Qur’an's huffaz, reportedly around seventy, were among the Muslim dead.

1

’Umar raises the alarm

’Umar ibn al-Khattab went to Abu Bakr, warning that if huffaz kept dying in battle at this rate across further campaigns, portions of the Qur’an itself could be lost forever unless it was gathered into one authoritative written copy.

2

Abu Bakr hesitates, then agrees

His first response was caution: “How can I do something the Messenger of Allah did not do?” ’Umar kept pressing until, by Abu Bakr's own account, “Allah opened my chest for that which He had opened the chest of ’Umar.”

3

Zayd ibn Thabit is chosen — and hesitates too

Abu Bakr summoned Zayd, by then a respected scribe who had personally heard the Prophet's own final recitation. Zayd's first reaction mirrored Abu Bakr's own: he called it a task heavier than moving a mountain. He accepted on the same reasoning — that refusing to safeguard the Qur’an would be the greater risk.

4

A rigorous method, not a shortcut

Zayd did not simply write from his own memory. He collected the Qur’an “from date-palm stalks, thin white stones, and the hearts of men,” accepting each verse only where it was independently confirmed — matched against both a written record from the Prophet's own lifetime and corroborating testimony that it had been heard directly from the Prophet.

5

One finished mus-haf

The completed single volume — called a mus-haf — was kept with Abu Bakr, then passed to ’Umar when he became Caliph, and on ’Umar's death passed to his daughter Hafsa bint ’Umar, one of the Prophet's widows, for safekeeping. It would matter again within twenty years.

The second compilation — ’Uthman, c. 25 AH

By the time of the third Caliph, ’Uthman ibn ’Affan, the Muslim empire had spread far beyond Arabia. That created a new problem — not a risk of losing the text, but a risk of the text fragmenting.

The trigger came from a battlefield far from Madinah. The companion Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman, serving on campaigns in Armenia and Azerbaijan, watched soldiers from Iraq and Syria argue over whose recitation of the Qur’an was correct — each side had learned authentic but differently-voiced oral traditions, and each was now accusing the other of getting it wrong. Alarmed, Hudhayfah hurried back to Madinah and warned ’Uthman directly:

“Save this nation before they differ about the Book as the Jews and Christians differed about their Books before them.” Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman, to Caliph ’Uthman ibn ’Affan
1

’Uthman borrows Hafsa's mus-haf

He sent for the original compilation kept by Hafsa bint ’Umar, to be used as the base copy — not started from scratch.

2

A four-man committee, led again by Zayd ibn Thabit

Alongside Zayd sat three Qurayshi companions: ’Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr, Sa’id ibn al-’As, and ’Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Harith ibn Hisham. Their task was not to simply copy — they independently re-verified the text against written material and living witnesses, exactly as Zayd had done under Abu Bakr.

3

One rule for resolving disagreement

’Uthman instructed the three Qurayshi members: if they and Zayd (an Ansari, from Madinah) disagreed on how a word should be written, they were to write it in the dialect of Quraysh — “for the Qur’an was revealed in their tongue.” This gave the project one clear, principled standard rather than an arbitrary compromise.

4

Several copies, sent to the empire's centres

Standard copies were produced and dispatched to the major garrison cities — Kufa, Basra, Damascus — and Makkah, with one retained in Madinah. Every Muslim community now had access to an identical, authoritative text.

5

Other copies withdrawn

’Uthman then ordered that other partial or variant personal copies in circulation be gathered in and destroyed, removing the material basis for further dispute. Hafsa's original was returned to her.

Abu Bakr's compilation · 12 AH

  • Problem it solved: risk of losing verses as huffaz died in battle.
  • Result: one single master copy, for safekeeping.
  • Character: defensive — preserving what already existed.

’Uthman's compilation · c. 25 AH

  • Problem it solved: risk of the empire splitting into rival regional recitations.
  • Result: several identical copies, distributed and enforced as standard.
  • Character: unifying — standardising for a growing, diverse ummah.

□ Elaborate — test your prediction

Go back to the guess you wrote at the start of this lesson. The real distinction examiners reward is not “the first attempt was flawed” — both compilations used the same rigorous method and produced the same accurate text. The distinction is purpose: Abu Bakr's was about preventing loss; ’Uthman's was about preventing division. State that distinction in your own sentence before moving on — this is one of the most commonly tested AO2 ideas in this whole topic.

Why this matters — the AO2 layer

For Muslims, the human effort behind both compilations is not separate from divine protection — it is understood as the very mechanism through which God's own promise was fulfilled: “Indeed, it is We who sent down the Qur’an, and indeed, We will be its guardian” (Qur’an 15:9). ’Uthman's standard text, the Uthmanic mus-haf, is the direct ancestor of every printed Qur’an in circulation today, in every country, in every language edition — which is why a reciter in Jakarta and a reciter in Lagos are reading, letter for letter, the same verses. It is also why the companion ’Ali ibn Abi Talib, who might otherwise have contested ’Uthman's authority to order the destruction of other copies, is recorded defending the decision fully: “Do not speak ill of ’Uthman… he did not do what he did with the mus-hafs except with our full agreement.”

PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · PAPER 1, Q2

(a) Describe the roles of Abu Bakr, ’Umar and ’Uthman in compiling and preserving the Qur’an. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Abu Bakr: authorised the first compilation after ’Umar's warning following the losses at the Battle of Yamama; appointed Zayd ibn Thabit; kept the finished mus-haf in his own safekeeping.
  2. ’Umar: the one who first proposed compilation to Abu Bakr; inherited the mus-haf as Caliph; passed it to his daughter Hafsa for safekeeping before his death.
  3. ’Uthman: responded to Hudhayfah's warning about regional recitation disputes; borrowed Hafsa's mus-haf as the base text; commissioned Zayd's second committee; enforced one standard Quraysh-dialect text across the empire; withdrew variant copies.
  4. Zayd ibn Thabit (credit him even though not named in the question — he is the thread connecting all three Caliphs): led both compilation efforts personally, verifying every verse against written and oral testimony each time.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

Typical prompt: “Significance of the Qur’an being written as a book” or “Benefits of Muslims having a written Qur’an.” Strong answers name concrete benefits: a fixed reference for settling disputes over wording; identical recitation across every Muslim country regardless of language; protection against the gradual drift or embellishment that affected earlier scriptures transmitted only orally or by hand across generations; a stable base text later scholars could safely build tafsir (interpretation) and Islamic law upon.

Examiner pattern: candidates frequently conflate the two compilations into one event, or credit ’Uthman with the huffaz-related trigger that actually belongs to Abu Bakr's. Keep the two timelines and two triggers distinct — the compare table above is designed to fix that specific confusion.
