Preservation Before Compilation

2.2 The Compilation under the Khalifas · how the Qur’an was safeguarded before any single book existed

The Qur’an was complete — every verse revealed — the moment the Prophet died in 11 AH. It was not, however, a book. It lived in the memories of hundreds of companions and on scattered fragments of bone, bark and parchment. Two Caliphs, a generation apart, turned that living memory into the single fixed text every printed Qur’an in the world today still matches, letter for letter.

□ Predict before you read

Two separate compilation efforts happened within about fifteen years of each other — one under Abu Bakr, one under ’Uthman. If the Qur’an had already been written down once under Abu Bakr, why would ’Uthman need to do the job again? Write a guess. The answer is not “the first copy was lost or wrong” — hold that thought.

Preserved twice over, from the very first day

During the Prophet's own lifetime the Qur’an was already being preserved through two parallel, deliberate methods, not left to chance.

The first was memorisation. Companions known as the huffaz (those who had memorised the entire text) numbered in the hundreds by the time of the Prophet's death, and the Prophet himself reviewed the whole revealed Qur’an with Jibreel once every Ramadan — and, significantly, twice in the final Ramadan of his life, which companions later understood as a sign his death was near.

The second was writing. The Prophet appointed dedicated Scribes of the Revelation (Kuttab al-Wahy) — among them a young Ansari companion named Zayd ibn Thabit, who will matter enormously in the next two lessons — to record each verse as it was revealed, on whatever material was to hand: palm-leaf stalks, thin flat stones, shoulder blades of animals, pieces of leather and parchment. What did not yet exist was a single bound volume gathering every verse in one place, in a fixed order, between two covers. There was a simple reason for that: while the Prophet was alive, revelation was still arriving, and verses could still be abrogated or reordered by divine instruction (naskh) — binding it into one finished book before revelation itself was finished would have made no sense.
