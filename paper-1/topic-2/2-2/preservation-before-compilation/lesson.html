Preservation Before Compilation

2.2 The Compilation of the Qur’an under the Khalifas · how the Qur’an was safeguarded before any single book existed

By the time the Prophet (pbuh) died in 11 AH, not one verse of the Qur’an had been lost — yet no single bound copy existed anywhere in Arabia. Before this unit can tell you how Abu Bakr and ’Uthman turned that scattered, living text into the one official book every Muslim reads today, you need to understand exactly what they were working with: two independent safety nets that had already been running side by side for twenty-three years.

□ Predict before you read

Picture the problem this whole unit is going to solve: revelation arrived piece by piece over twenty-three years, there was no printing press, and paper as we know it didn’t exist in Arabia. Write down at least two ways you think the Prophet (pbuh) and his companions might have made sure nothing was ever lost or changed along the way — then, as you read, check which of your guesses turns out to be one of the two real safeguards, and which is missing.

Two tracks, not one

Islamic scholarship on the sciences of the Qur’an (’uloom al-Qur’aan) describes preservation during the Prophet’s lifetime as running on two parallel tracks at once, each acting as an independent check on the other. The first was memorisation: as each portion of revelation arrived, the Prophet recited it aloud to those present, and companions — drawing on a culture that already prized memorising long tribal poetry — committed it to memory on the spot. The second was writing: the Prophet also had verses recorded in real time by a body of companions known as the Scribes of the Revelation (Kuttab al-Wahy), so that the text existed in a fixed physical form as well as a remembered one. Neither track depended on the other for its accuracy — which is exactly the point, and exactly what made the text so difficult to corrupt or lose.

Track one: memorisation

Verses were memorised by companions the moment they were recited, and those who had memorised the complete Qur’an were called the huffaz; by the time of the Prophet’s death, they numbered in the hundreds. The Prophet himself named specific companions as reliable teachers of recitation: “Learn the recitation of the Qur’an from four: ’Abdullah ibn Mas’ud, Salim (the freed slave of Abu Hudhayfah), Mu’adh ibn Jabal, and Ubay ibn Ka’b” (Sahih al-Bukhari). This track had no physical object that could be lost, burned or damaged — but it depended entirely on human memory staying accurate across a growing number of people, which is precisely the vulnerability this whole unit is about to test.

Track two: writing

The Prophet appointed dedicated Scribes of the Revelation, among them a young Ansari companion named Zayd ibn Thabit, to record each verse as it was revealed. Verses were written on whatever material was to hand: palm-leaf stalks, thin flat stones, the shoulder blades of animals, pieces of leather, and parchment. The Prophet personally directed, on Jibreel’s instruction, exactly where each new verse belonged within its surah — arrangement was never left to a scribe’s own judgement. This track produced a fixed physical record that memory alone could never provide — but the pieces were scattered across many hands and many different materials, not yet gathered anywhere in one place. Remember that word “scattered”: it is exactly the problem the next chapter opens with.

Jibreel’s yearly check

A third safeguard ran on top of the other two: an annual review. Every Ramadan, Jibreel came and reviewed with the Prophet everything that had been revealed of the Qur’an so far — companions understood this as a formal, angel-verified confirmation that nothing had drifted or been forgotten. In the very last Ramadan of the Prophet’s life, this review happened twice rather than once, a detail companions only understood in hindsight as a sign that his death was near. Zayd ibn Thabit — the same companion who had served as a scribe throughout the Madani period — is reported to have been present for this final, doubled review, which is part of why, as the next chapter explains, Abu Bakr later turned to him specifically when the text needed to be gathered into one volume.

Why there was still no single book yet

It would be reasonable to ask why, with both memorisation and writing already happening, the Qur’an wasn’t simply bound into one volume during the Prophet’s own lifetime. The answer is structural, not accidental: revelation was still arriving, right up until shortly before his death, and portions could still be abrogated (naskh) or have their place within a surah reassigned by fresh instruction from Jibreel. Binding a finished book before the process of revelation itself had finished would have made no sense — and there was, in any case, a living authority on hand, the Prophet himself, who could confirm the correct wording and order at any moment. That authority is precisely what disappeared at his death — which is exactly where this unit’s story of compilation begins.

The Scribes’ wider job

It’s worth knowing that “Scribes of the Prophet” was a broader title than “Scribes of the Revelation” alone. The same body of literate companions who wrote down verses as they were revealed also handled the Prophet’s wider correspondence: letters inviting rulers and tribal leaders to Islam, the terms of treaties such as Hudaybiyyah, and administrative records connected to zakat and treaties with neighbouring tribes. Several dozen companions are recorded as having served as scribes at one time or another across the Makkan and Madani periods — Zayd ibn Thabit and Ubay ibn Ka’b are the two names that recur most often in connection with the Qur’an specifically.

The near-crisis that proved how fragile even this was

Two safety nets, running for twenty-three years, kept the Qur’an completely intact throughout the Prophet’s lifetime. But “intact” is not the same as “permanently safe” once the Prophet was gone — and it took barely a year after his death for that difference to matter. As the next chapter opens, a single battle would kill a large number of the huffaz who carried the text in memory, and expose, for the first time, exactly how much the whole system had still been relying on living memory alone to fill the gaps between scattered pieces of writing.

Track one — Memorisation (the human safety net)

  • Verses were memorised by companions the moment they were recited, drawing on the strong oral-memory culture of pre-Islamic Arabia.
  • Those who memorised the complete Qur’an were called the huffaz; by the Prophet’s death, they numbered in the hundreds.
  • The Prophet named specific companions as reliable teachers of recitation, including ’Abdullah ibn Mas’ud, Salim, Mu’adh ibn Jabal, and Ubay ibn Ka’b.
  • No physical object could be lost, burned or damaged — but it depended entirely on human memory staying accurate as the number of memorisers grew.

Track two — Writing (the physical safety net)

  • The Prophet appointed dedicated Scribes of the Revelation, among them Zayd ibn Thabit, to record each verse as it was revealed.
  • Verses were written on whatever material was to hand: palm-leaf stalks, thin flat stones, shoulder blades of animals, leather, and parchment.
  • The Prophet personally directed, on Jibreel’s instruction, exactly where each verse belonged within its surah.
  • It produced a fixed physical record memory alone could never provide — but the pieces were scattered across many hands and materials, not yet gathered in one place.

What happened to a single verse, from revelation to safekeeping

1

Revelation arrives

Jibreel delivered the verse to the Prophet, in whichever of the forms revelation took.

2

Recitation

The Prophet recited it aloud immediately to whoever was present.

3

Memorisation begins

Companions in earshot began memorising it there and then.

4

Dictation to a scribe

The Prophet dictated it to an available scribe, who wrote it on whatever material — stone, leather, palm-stalk, parchment — was to hand.

5

Placement fixed

The Prophet specified exactly where the verse belonged within its surah, following Jibreel’s instruction, so the scribe recorded both the wording and its intended place.

6

Annual review

The whole body of revelation so far was reviewed again with Jibreel at the next Ramadan — twice, in the Prophet’s final year.

7

The Prophet dies

Both tracks stopped growing — what existed at that moment, scattered across memory and writing, was all there would ever be.

□ Common mix-up to avoid — during the Prophet’s life vs after his death

This lesson covers only the two safety nets that operated while the Prophet was alive: memorisation and real-time writing, with no single bound book yet because revelation itself hadn’t finished. The rest of this unit — “Compilation under Abu Bakr” and “Compilation under ’Uthman” — picks up the story after his death: Abu Bakr’s compilation following the huffaz losses at the Battle of Yamama, and ’Uthman’s later standardisation. If an exam question asks about Abu Bakr, ’Umar or ’Uthman by name, or mentions the Battle of Yamama, it needs those later chapters, not this one.

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · PAPER 1, Q5 · MAY/JUNE 2022 (J22 S11)

(a) Tasks performed by some Companions as the Prophet’s Scribes. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Writing down verses of the Qur’an as they were revealed, taking dictation directly from the Prophet.
  2. Recording each verse on whatever material was available — palm-leaf stalks, thin flat stones, shoulder blades of animals, leather, or parchment.
  3. Writing the verse in the exact place within its surah that the Prophet specified, following Jibreel’s instruction, rather than choosing an order themselves.
  4. Writing the Prophet’s letters to rulers and tribal leaders inviting them to Islam.
  5. Recording the terms of treaties, such as the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah.
  6. Keeping administrative and financial records, including matters connected to zakat.
  7. Several dozen companions served as scribes at different points; Zayd ibn Thabit and Ubay ibn Ka’b are the two most closely associated with recording the Qur’an itself.

Part (b) — “Benefits of the Qur’an being available to everyone on the internet” (4 marks) — the internet makes the Qur’an instantly accessible worldwide, in the original Arabic and in translation, with audio recitation that helps memorisation and correct pronunciation; it allows searching for specific verses or topics quickly; and countless digital copies make the text effectively impossible to lose or destroy — echoing, in a modern form, exactly the redundancy the Scribes and the huffaz built in fourteen centuries earlier.

Examiner note: Strong answers list several distinct scribal tasks rather than describing Qur’an-writing alone at length — the mark scheme rewards breadth (Qur’an, letters, treaties, records) as much as detail on any one task.
PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · PAPER 1, Q5 · OCT/NOV 2025 (N25 W12)

(a) Main roles played by the Scribes of the Prophet. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. This (a) question covers essentially the same ground as “tasks performed by the Scribes,” worded as “roles” instead — use the same seven points listed in the Exam Lab entry above.

Part (b) — “Is it easier to record Islamic knowledge now than in the Prophet’s time?” (4 marks) — a balanced answer weighs both sides: recording is far faster and more widely accessible now (printing, audio, video, digital storage, instant global reach) than relying on scribes writing by hand on stone or leather; but ease of recording is not the same as guaranteed accuracy, and Islamic knowledge today still depends on the same kind of careful verification (checking sources, authenticity, scholarly oversight) that the Scribes and huffaz provided in the Prophet’s own time.

Examiner note: This is a “to what extent” style (b) question dressed as a yes/no — full marks need both a “yes, it’s easier” point and a “but accuracy still needs safeguarding” point, not a one-sided answer.
