Compilation under ’Uthman

2.2 The Compilation of the Qur’an under the Khalifas · c. 25 AH · one standard text for a growing empire

A decade after Abu Bakr’s single, carefully verified manuscript was completed, a soldier serving far from Madinah overheard something that alarmed him badly enough to ride home and warn the Caliph directly: Muslims from different regions were now arguing over whose recitation of the Qur’an was the correct one. What ’Uthman did next is the reason a printed Qur’an bought today in Jakarta is word-for-word identical to one bought in Makkah.

□ Predict before you read

The previous lesson (“Compilation under Abu Bakr”) ended with a single manuscript — the suhuf — safely kept in the private custody of Hafsa bint ’Umar. That already solved the problem of losing the text from memory. So think about this: if the text already existed safely in writing, what different kind of problem could still arise as Islam spread rapidly out of Arabia into Syria, Iraq, Egypt and beyond — one that a single safely-stored manuscript, on its own, would not fix? Write down your guess before reading on.

A different kind of problem: distance, dialect, and disagreement

By the time ’Uthman ibn ’Affan became the third Caliph, the Muslim state had expanded far beyond Arabia, and with it the Qur’an was being taught, recited and learned by huge numbers of new Muslims who had never heard the Prophet recite it themselves. Arabic itself contained a range of regional dialects, and different companions who had settled as teachers in different garrison towns — Kufa, Basra, Damascus and elsewhere — naturally taught the recitation styles and small pronunciation habits they themselves had learned. None of this was, on its own, a threat to the Qur’an’s content: the underlying meaning and wording were unchanged. But left unaddressed, these small regional differences risked hardening into rival claims about which version was “correct” — a very different danger from the one Abu Bakr’s generation had faced, and one a single safely-guarded manuscript sitting in Hafsa’s home could do nothing to prevent.

Hudhayfah’s warning

The moment this danger became undeniable came from a single companion’s direct experience. Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman, serving on military campaigns in Armenia and Azerbaijan, found himself among soldiers drawn from both Syria and Iraq — and was alarmed to hear them disputing sharply over whose regional recitation of the Qur’an was correct, each side confident the other’s was in error. Troubled by what he had witnessed, Hudhayfah returned to Madinah and went directly to ’Uthman with a warning recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari: “Save this nation before they differ about the Book (the Qur’an) as the Jews and the Christians differed about their Scriptures before.” The comparison was deliberately severe — Hudhayfah was telling ’Uthman that unresolved recitation disputes could fracture the Muslim community’s relationship with its own scripture in exactly the way, as Muslims understood it, earlier religious communities had fractured around theirs.

“O chief of the believers! Save this nation before they differ about the Book (the Qur’an) as the Jews and the Christians differed about their Scriptures before.” Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman to Caliph ’Uthman, Sahih al-Bukhari

’Uthman acts: borrowing Hafsa’s suhuf

’Uthman moved quickly and deliberately. Rather than starting from nothing, he sent to Hafsa bint ’Umar and requested her suhuf — the single verified manuscript produced under Abu Bakr and kept safely in her custody ever since ’Umar’s death. This is the direct link between the two stages of this story: ’Uthman’s committee did not compile a fresh text from scattered fragments the way Zayd had done the first time; it worked from an already-completed, already-verified source, using it as the base for a new kind of project — not verification this time, but standardisation and multiplication.

The four-man committee and the Quraysh rule

’Uthman appointed a committee of four to carry out the work, headed once again by Zayd ibn Thabit — the same companion who had led Abu Bakr’s original compilation a decade earlier — alongside three companions from the tribe of Quraysh: ’Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr, Sa’id ibn al-’As, and ’Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Harith ibn Hisham. ’Uthman gave the committee one specific, decisive instruction for handling any disagreement that arose over how a word should be written down: wherever Zayd and the three Qurashi members disagreed on wording, they were to write it in the dialect of Quraysh — because the Qur’an had first been revealed in that dialect, and it was, not coincidentally, the dialect three of the four committee members themselves spoke natively. This rule gave the committee a clear, principled tie-breaker rather than leaving disputed wording to majority vote or personal preference.

“Whenever you differ with Zayd ibn Thabit on any point in the Qur’an, then write it in the dialect of Quraysh, as the Qur’an was revealed in their tongue.” Caliph ’Uthman’s instruction to the three Qurashi committee members, Sahih al-Bukhari

Multiple copies, sent to a growing empire

Where Abu Bakr’s project had produced one manuscript for safekeeping, ’Uthman’s committee produced several official standard copies — tradition gives the number as somewhere between four and seven — which were then sent out to the major centres of the Muslim world: accounts vary in the exact list, but they generally include Makkah, Syria (Damascus), Kufa and Basra, with one copy retained in Madinah itself as the reference text, sometimes called the Mushaf al-Imam. This was, in effect, the opposite instinct to Abu Bakr’s: instead of a single safeguarded manuscript held quietly by one custodian, ’Uthman deliberately multiplied and distributed the text as widely as possible, so that every major Muslim community would be reciting from the same officially sanctioned copy.

Removing the alternatives

Alongside this distribution, ’Uthman took a further, more drastic step: he ordered that other personal or partial written collections of the Qur’an already in circulation — including well-known companions’ own copies, such as those associated with ’Abdullah ibn Mas’ud and Ubay ibn Ka’b — be gathered in and destroyed. Hafsa’s original suhuf, the source manuscript itself, was returned to her rather than destroyed; it was specifically the other, competing personal copies that were removed from circulation. The reasoning behind this was not that those companions’ copies were considered inaccurate or corrupted — it was that once a single, carefully standardised official text existed, allowing multiple respected companions’ individually annotated copies to keep circulating alongside it would recreate exactly the “whose version is correct” dispute Hudhayfah had just warned against, only this time between named, prestigious companions rather than anonymous soldiers.

Not universally welcomed at first

The measure met real resistance in places. ’Abdullah ibn Mas’ud in particular is reported to have been unhappy — both at not being included on the standardising committee himself, despite being one of the Prophet’s most trusted reciters, and at seeing his own long-used personal copy called in for destruction. This resistance is worth including honestly rather than glossing over: it shows that ’Uthman’s decision was a genuine, costly judgement call, not an uncontroversial administrative tidy-up. But the opposition did not persist or split the community, and the standardised text, generally referred to in modern scholarship as the ’Uthmanic codex or Mushaf ’Uthmani, became — and has remained ever since — the single agreed text of the Qur’an across the entire Muslim world.

The legacy: fourteen centuries of one text

The practical result of ’Uthman’s project is the reason a Qur’an printed today in Indonesia, Nigeria, Pakistan or Britain is textually identical, letter for letter, to one printed in Saudi Arabia — a level of manuscript uniformity across so many centuries, languages and regions that has no close parallel among other ancient religious texts. Muslim scholars point to this directly as part of the case for the Qur’an’s preserved authenticity: not simply that a text survived, but that a specific, traceable, well-documented decision by an identifiable committee, working from an already-verified source manuscript, is the reason it survived identically everywhere.

□ Where this comes from

This lesson’s core narrative — Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman’s warning after witnessing Syrian/Iraqi recitation disputes during campaigns in Armenia and Azerbaijan, ’Uthman requesting Hafsa’s suhuf, the four-man committee, the Quraysh-dialect rule, the distribution of standard copies to major centres, and the gathering and destruction of rival personal copies — is drawn from Sahih al-Bukhari, Kitab Fada’il al-Qur’an, the hadith narrated by Anas ibn Malik, cross-checked via sunnah.com. The identification of this material as “the ’Uthmanic codex” in modern scholarship, and the wider two-stage framing linking it to the previous lesson, draws on the Yaqeen Institute’s research paper The ’Uthmanic Codex, already used elsewhere in this project’s Topic 2.1–2.3 textbook chapter on this subject.

□ Common mix-up to avoid — suhuf vs mushaf, and “compiled” vs “standardised”

Exam answers frequently blur Abu Bakr’s and ’Uthman’s projects into one undifferentiated event. They were two distinct projects with two distinct purposes and two distinct results. Abu Bakr’s compilation (previous lesson) produced the suhuf — one private manuscript, made under pressure of losing the text from memory. ’Uthman’s project (this lesson) started from that same suhuf and produced the mushaf copies — multiple official texts, made to stop regional recitation disputes, deliberately distributed, with rival personal copies destroyed. If a question asks specifically about ’Uthman, answer with Hudhayfah’s warning, the committee, the Quraysh-dialect rule, and the distribution/destruction of copies — not the Yamama crisis, which belongs to the previous lesson.

□ A related question this lesson does not fully answer

One past-paper question — N21 w21 · 0493/21 · Paper 2, Q3 — asks about ’Uthman’s election as the third Caliph and the administration of his caliphate more broadly (10 marks), with part (b) asking whether compiling the Qur’an was his greatest achievement (4 marks). That question is catalogued under Paper 2 Topic 3 (the Rightly Guided Caliphs’ rule) rather than this Qur’an-history unit, and a full AO1 answer to its part (a) would need material on ’Uthman’s wider caliphate — his election, administration and military record — that goes beyond this lesson’s scope. This lesson gives you everything needed for the part (b) argument: the case for “yes, it was his greatest achievement” is the point above about worldwide textual uniformity ever since; the case against would need his other achievements as caliph, which belong to that separate unit.

PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · PAPER 1, Q2 · MAY/JUNE 2021 (J21 S12)

(a) Roles of Abu Bakr, ’Umar and ’Uthman in compiling and preserving the Qur’an. 10 marks · (b) Significance of the Qur’an being written as a book. 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Abu Bakr: after the Battle of Yamama killed a large number of huffaz, he was persuaded by ’Umar to commission a single written compilation, despite his own initial hesitation (see “Compilation under Abu Bakr” for full detail).
  2. Abu Bakr appointed Zayd ibn Thabit to lead the compilation, who accepted material only where writing was corroborated by witness testimony.
  3. ’Umar: first to raise the alarm after Yamama; later kept the completed suhuf in his own custody as the second Caliph, then arranged for it to pass to Hafsa bint ’Umar on his death.
  4. ’Uthman: responded to Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman’s warning that Muslims in Syria and Iraq were disputing over correct recitation.
  5. ’Uthman requested Hafsa’s suhuf and commissioned a four-man committee, again led by Zayd ibn Thabit, to produce a standardised text.
  6. Where wording was disputed, the committee wrote according to the dialect of Quraysh, since the Qur’an was revealed in that dialect.
  7. ’Uthman had multiple standard copies distributed to major Muslim centres and ordered other personal/variant copies destroyed.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

A written book gave the Qur’an a fixed, checkable physical form independent of any one person’s memory, guarding against the kind of loss shown by the Yamama huffaz deaths; it allowed the exact same text to be taught and checked consistently as Islam spread far beyond Arabia; it removed the risk of regional recitation disputes hardening into real division, the exact danger Hudhayfah warned against; and it gave every generation since a single unchanging reference point, which Muslims point to as part of the evidence for the Qur’an’s preserved authenticity.

Examiner note: This question rewards keeping each Caliph’s specific role distinct — Abu Bakr’s commissioning under crisis, ’Umar’s role as instigator and interim custodian, ’Uthman’s standardisation under a different kind of pressure — rather than describing “the Caliphs” as a single undifferentiated group who “preserved the Qur’an.”
PAST PAPER · 0493/22 · PAPER 2, Q4 · OCT/NOV 2021 (N21 W22)

(a) Role of ’Umar and ’Uthman in preserving the Qur’an. 10 marks · (b) Were the two stages of Qur’an preservation equally important? Give reasons. 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. ’Umar’s role: after the Battle of Yamama, he recognised the danger of losing parts of the Qur’an as huffaz died, and persuaded a reluctant Abu Bakr to commission a written compilation.
  2. ’Umar kept the completed suhuf in his own custody throughout his caliphate, after Abu Bakr’s death.
  3. On his own death, ’Umar ensured the suhuf passed to his daughter Hafsa bint ’Umar rather than being lost or scattered.
  4. ’Uthman’s role: responded to Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman’s warning that Muslims from Syria and Iraq were disputing over correct recitation.
  5. ’Uthman obtained Hafsa’s suhuf and commissioned Zayd ibn Thabit’s four-man committee to produce a standard text, using the dialect of Quraysh for disputed wording.
  6. ’Uthman distributed standard copies to the major centres of the Muslim world and had rival personal copies destroyed to remove competing versions.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

A balanced answer can argue either way but must justify it. Case for “equally important”: without ’Umar’s stage there would have been no single authoritative suhuf for ’Uthman to standardise from at all — the second stage depended entirely on the first. Case for “’Uthman’s stage mattered more”: without standardisation, regional recitation disputes could have split the Muslim community’s text the way Hudhayfah feared, so uniformity — not just existence — is what protected the Qur’an long-term. The strongest answers explicitly weigh both stages against each other rather than simply describing them again.

Examiner note: Because this (b) is explicitly a “were they equally important” question, restating AO1 content about what each Caliph did (without a direct judgement and reasoning) scores poorly — the answer must reach and justify a verdict.
PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · PAPER 1, Q2 · OCT/NOV 2022 (N22 W12)

(a) Ways the Qur’an was compiled and preserved after the Prophet’s death. 10 marks · (b) How useful is it that Muslims today can access the Qur’an in many formats? 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. After the Battle of Yamama killed a large number of huffaz, ’Umar persuaded Abu Bakr to commission a written compilation of the whole Qur’an (see “Compilation under Abu Bakr” for full detail).
  2. Zayd ibn Thabit led the compilation, accepting written material only when corroborated by independent witness testimony.
  3. The resulting suhuf was kept safe, passing from Abu Bakr to ’Umar and then to Hafsa bint ’Umar.
  4. Under ’Uthman, Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman’s warning about regional recitation disputes prompted a second stage: standardisation.
  5. A four-man committee led by Zayd ibn Thabit produced a definitive text from Hafsa’s suhuf, resolving disputes using the dialect of Quraysh.
  6. Standard copies were sent to the major centres of the Muslim world, and rival personal copies were destroyed to remove competing versions.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

Modern formats (print, audio recitation, apps, websites, searchable digital text) make the Qur’an instantly accessible anywhere, support memorisation through repeated listening, and allow quick verification of a verse’s wording or translation — but this convenience only works because ’Uthman’s standardisation already fixed one single agreed text; every modern format, whatever the medium, is still reproducing that same ’Uthmanic text, so accessibility today is really the fourteen-centuries-later benefit of that original standardisation.

Examiner note: Strong (b) answers explicitly connect the modern-format benefit back to the historical standardisation described in part (a), rather than treating “many formats today” as a completely separate, disconnected point.
PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · PAPER 1, Q2 · MAY/JUNE 2024 (J24 S12)

(a) The way the Qur’an was compiled and standardised into one book. 10 marks · (b) Benefits of Muslims having a written Qur’an. 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Compilation began after the Battle of Yamama, when the deaths of a large number of huffaz alarmed ’Umar into persuading Abu Bakr to act.
  2. Zayd ibn Thabit compiled a single manuscript (the suhuf), accepting material only where writing and witness testimony corroborated each other.
  3. The suhuf was kept by Abu Bakr, then ’Umar, then Hafsa bint ’Umar.
  4. Standardisation began when Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman warned ’Uthman that Muslims in Syria and Iraq were disputing over correct recitation.
  5. ’Uthman commissioned a four-man committee, again led by Zayd ibn Thabit, using Hafsa’s suhuf as its source.
  6. Disputed wording was resolved using the dialect of Quraysh, since the Qur’an was revealed in that dialect.
  7. Multiple standard copies were distributed to major Muslim centres, and other personal or partial copies were destroyed.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

A written, standardised Qur’an gives a fixed reference that cannot drift the way purely oral transmission risked drifting; it allows scholars, students and ordinary Muslims anywhere to check a recitation or ruling against the same agreed text; it unifies a global, multilingual community around one shared scripture regardless of local dialect; and it removes the kind of internal dispute over “whose version is correct” that Hudhayfah specifically warned ’Uthman about.

Examiner note: Nearly identical in structure to the N22 w12 and N25 w21 questions on this same topic — a single well-drilled 8–9 point AO1 sequence (Yamama → Zayd’s suhuf → custody chain → Hudhayfah → ’Uthman’s committee → Quraysh dialect rule → distribution and destruction of rivals) answers all three with only the (b) angle changing.
PAST PAPER · 0493/21 · PAPER 2, Q2 · OCT/NOV 2025 (N25 W21)

(a) Compilation and standardisation of the Qur’an, first under Abu Bakr and then under ’Uthman. 10 marks · (b) Benefits of a written Qur’an, then and now. 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Under Abu Bakr: after Yamama’s huffaz losses, ’Umar persuaded a reluctant Abu Bakr to commission a compilation; Zayd ibn Thabit, also reluctant at first, was chosen to lead it.
  2. Zayd’s method cross-checked written fragments against corroborating witness testimony, producing the single suhuf manuscript.
  3. The suhuf passed from Abu Bakr to ’Umar, and finally to Hafsa bint ’Umar, who kept it safely.
  4. Under ’Uthman: Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman’s warning about Syrian/Iraqi recitation disputes prompted the second stage.
  5. ’Uthman’s four-man committee, led again by Zayd ibn Thabit, produced a standard text from Hafsa’s suhuf, using the dialect of Quraysh for any disputed wording.
  6. Standard copies were sent to major centres of the Muslim world; competing personal copies were gathered and destroyed.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

Then: a written Qur’an protected the text once memory alone had shown itself vulnerable (Yamama), and stopped regional dialects splintering into rival “correct” versions (Hudhayfah’s fear). Now: it means every Muslim worldwide, whatever their first language, recites and studies from an identical text, supports accurate memorisation and teaching across generations, and provides a stable, checkable reference for Islamic law and scholarship that has not changed in fourteen centuries.

Examiner note: Because this question names both Caliphs explicitly in the (a) wording, an answer describing only one of the two compilation stages — however detailed — cannot reach full marks; both the suhuf stage and the standardisation stage need covering, which is exactly why this course splits the story into two lessons but expects you to be able to combine them in an answer.
