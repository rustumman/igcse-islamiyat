First Revelation

2.1 The Revelation of the Qur’an · Ramadan 610 CE · Cave of Hira, Makkah

One night in the year 610 CE, a forty-year-old merchant who had never composed a line of poetry or learned to read a single word was gripped by an angel in a mountain cave and commanded to “Read” — and Islam began. Almost every mark this unit offers turns on how precisely you can tell what happened next.

□ Predict before you read

The Prophet Muhammad (pbuh) could not read or write. Before you read on, write down: why might it matter — as evidence, not just as biography — that the very first words of the Qur’an were given to a man who was unable to read them himself? You’ll meet the examiner’s version of this reasoning later in the lesson (it’s exactly the kind of AO2 point that earns marks in a “significance” question).

Before the cave: a man already withdrawing to think

By the age of forty, Muhammad (pbuh) had spent years developing a habit the Arabs of Makkah called tahannuth — periods of solitary retreat for reflection and worship, away from the noise and idol-worship of the city. His chosen place was a small, bare cave on Jabal al-Nur (“the Mountain of Light”), a short, steep climb outside Makkah, inside which sits the cave known as Ghar Hira — the Cave of Hira. He would take a little food and water and stay for days at a time, troubled, according to the earliest biographers, by the corruption and idol-worship he saw among his own people, though he had no idea yet that he was being prepared for prophethood. It was during one of these retreats, in the month of Ramadan, in the year later reckoned as 610 CE, that everything changed.

The angel commands “Iqra”

As he sat in the cave, the angel Jibreel (Gabriel) appeared to him and said: اقرأ — “Iqra”, “Read.” Muhammad (pbuh), who had never learned to read, answered honestly: “I am not one who reads” (in some translations, “I cannot read”). Jibreel then seized him and pressed him firmly against himself — hard enough that, in the Prophet’s own later description, he reached the limit of what he could bear — before releasing him and repeating the same single-word command. The Prophet gave the same answer a second time, and was pressed a second time. Only after the third command and the third embrace did Jibreel finally recite the words for him to repeat — and those words became the opening verses of the Qur’an.

“Read! In the name of your Lord who created — created man from a clinging substance. Read! And your Lord is the Most Generous — who taught by the pen — taught man that which he knew not.” Qur’an 96:1–5, the first verses ever revealed

Notice what these first words actually say: before anything else, before any law or ritual, the Qur’an opens by naming God as Creator and as the one who taught by the pen — teaching humanity to write and to know things it did not know. A book that would eventually be memorised, recited, and eventually written down by a community began with a command to read, addressed to a man who could not.

Terrified, he leaves the cave

The experience left the Prophet shaken to his core. According to the earliest biographical accounts (the Sira literature, notably Ibn Ishaq’s), as he made his way back down the mountainside in fear, he heard a voice from the sky above him calling, “O Muhammad, you are the Messenger of Allah, and I am Jibreel” — and looking up, he saw the angel in his true, immense form, filling the horizon, his form visible wherever the Prophet turned his gaze. (See the Source Box below — this detail needs careful handling for exam purposes, because a very similar vision is also recorded, in the most rigorously authenticated hadith collection, in connection with a later moment in the story.)

□ He runs to Khadija

Shaking and drenched in sweat, he hurried home to his wife Khadija bint Khuwaylid and said, “Cover me! Cover me!” — convinced, in his fear and confusion, that something terrible had happened to him. Khadija wrapped him in a cloak until his trembling passed, and once he was able to speak, he told her everything and said, “I fear for myself.” Khadija did not hesitate.

“Never! By Allah, Allah will never disgrace you. You keep good relations with your kith and kin, help the poor and the destitute, serve your guests generously, and assist those afflicted by calamity.”
— Khadija bint Khuwaylid, as recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari, Hadith 3

Her reasoning is worth pausing on: she did not reassure him with a promise or a guess — she reasoned from his known character. A man of that integrity, she was saying, was not the kind of man God would abandon.

Still needing more than reassurance, Khadija took her husband to see her elderly cousin, Waraqah ibn Nawfal, a man who had converted to Christianity in the pre-Islamic period and had studied the Torah and the Gospel in their earlier languages. When the Prophet described what had happened, Waraqah recognised it immediately:

“This is the Namus [the same one who keeps secrets — i.e. the angel of revelation] that Allah sent down to Musa. I wish I were young, and could live to see the time when your people will turn you out.” The Prophet asked, “Will they turn me out?” Waraqah replied: “Yes — never has a man brought something similar to what you have brought without being treated with hostility. If I live to see that day, I will support you strongly.”
— Waraqah ibn Nawfal, as recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari, Hadith 3

Waraqah died not long afterwards, before the persecution he had predicted fully began — but his identification mattered enormously: here was a man outside the family, trained in earlier scripture, independently recognising the pattern of true prophecy and naming the very angel — Jibreel — who had also brought revelation to Musa (Moses).

Why this matters as evidence, not just as story

Three separate people reacted to the same single night in three different ways, and together their reactions form part of the Muslim case that the revelation was genuine rather than invented: the Prophet himself reacted with fear and uncertainty — not the confident excitement of a man fabricating a claim to power; Khadija reasoned from years of firsthand knowledge of his honesty; and Waraqah, a trained outsider with no reason to flatter him, recognised the experience against an independent standard (his knowledge of earlier scripture) and even predicted the costly persecution that following through on it would bring — hardly what an ambitious impostor would forecast for himself.

What came immediately after

Following this first encounter, revelation did not continue at once. A period passed — known as the fatrah (the gap or pause) — during which no further revelation came, and the Prophet was left in anxious uncertainty. It was only afterwards, according to Sahih al-Bukhari’s continuation of this same hadith, that — while out walking — he again heard a voice from the sky, looked up, and saw the same angel he had seen at Hira, this time described as seated on a throne between heaven and earth. Frightened, he again ran home calling “Cover me! Cover me!”, and it was at this point that Surah al-Muddaththir (74:1–5) was revealed, opening with the command “O you wrapped in a cloak! Arise and warn!” — the verses that marked the actual start of his public mission as a prophet, as opposed to the private, overwhelming first contact in the cave.

“Sometimes it is [revealed] like the ringing of a bell, and that is the hardest on me; then it leaves me and I have grasped what he said. And sometimes the angel comes to me in the form of a man and talks to me, and I grasp whatever he says.” Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), describing what revelation felt like, narrated by ’A’ishah, Sahih al-Bukhari

□ Where this comes from

The core narrative — the Cave of Hira, “Iqra” repeated three times, the revelation of Surah al-’Alaq 96:1–5, Khadija’s reassurance and Waraqah ibn Nawfal’s confirmation — is drawn directly from Sahih al-Bukhari, Hadith 3 (Kitab Bad’ al-Wahy, “The Book of the Beginning of Revelation”), narrated by ’A’ishah, cross-checked via sunnah.com. The classification of the forms revelation took, and the general Qur’anic framing of wahy (revelation) in 42:51, is drawn from Abu Ammaar Yasir Qadhi’s academic study An Introduction to the Sciences of the Qur’aan (Al-Hidaayah, 1999). The detail of Jibreel appearing in the sky and announcing himself immediately after the Hira encounter is drawn from the earliest surviving biography of the Prophet, Ibn Ishaq’s Sirat Rasul Allah (as preserved by Ibn Hisham; English translation by A. Guillaume, The Life of Muhammad, Oxford University Press), and is also the version presented in the widely used modern biography Safiur Rahman al-Mubarakpuri’s Ar-Raheeq al-Makhtum (“The Sealed Nectar”). Where the Qur’an or a hadith is quoted directly in this lesson, treat the wording as exam-ready — examiners consistently reward candidates who cite the actual phrasing rather than paraphrase it loosely.

□ Common mix-up to avoid — two very similar “angel in the sky” moments

Students very often lose accuracy by merging two separate visions of Jibreel “filling the horizon.” The version most textbooks (and this lesson) place immediately after the Hira encounter comes from the early Sira biographies (Ibn Ishaq / Ibn Hisham; also Ar-Raheeq al-Makhtum). Sahih al-Bukhari’s own version of Hadith 3, however, explicitly places a near-identical vision — Jibreel seated on a throne between heaven and earth — at a later point: after the fatrah (the pause in revelation), immediately before Surah al-Muddaththir (74:1–5) was revealed. For exam purposes, the safest, most tightly source-supported sequence to write is: Cave of Hira → “Iqra” x3 → Surah al-’Alaq 96:1–5 → trembling home to Khadija → Khadija’s reassurance → Waraqah’s confirmation, as the complete answer to “the first revelation.” If a question specifically asks about the fatrah or “different ways revelation came to the Prophet,” that is where the throne/horizon vision and Surah al-Muddaththir belong.

□ Elaborate — three strands of evidence

Without looking back at the text, explain out loud or in writing why each of the three human reactions on that night — the Prophet’s fear, Khadija’s calm reasoning from character, and Waraqah’s recognition from scriptural knowledge — works as a separate strand of evidence that the revelation was genuine rather than invented. Then go one step further: why might an examiner specifically reward you for keeping all three strands distinct, rather than writing “everyone believed him” as a single point?

PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · PAPER 1, Q2 · MAY/JUNE 2022 (J22 S12)

(a) Describe the Prophet’s experience of receiving the first revelation. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Context: by age 40, Muhammad (pbuh) regularly withdrew to the Cave of Hira on Jabal al-Nur for solitary reflection.
  2. In Ramadan, 610 CE, the angel Jibreel appeared to him there for the first time.
  3. Jibreel commanded “Iqra” (Read); the Prophet, unable to read, said so each time, and was embraced tightly three times before Jibreel recited the words for him.
  4. The first revelation was the opening of Surah al-’Alaq (96:1–5) — “Read, in the name of your Lord who created…”
  5. Terrified, he returned home trembling and asked Khadija to cover him.
  6. Khadija reassured him, reasoning from his honesty, kindness to relatives, generosity to guests and support for the poor.
  7. She took him to her cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal, a scholar of earlier scripture.
  8. Waraqah identified the angel as the Namus sent to Musa, confirmed the experience was genuine prophecy, and predicted the Prophet would face hostility from his own people.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) Significance of the Qur’an being revealed over 23 years, rather than all at once.” Model answer approach: strengthened the Prophet’s heart and made the message easier to memorise and live by gradually; allowed guidance to respond directly to real events as they happened, giving the community practical, relevant answers; allowed laws (e.g. the prohibition of alcohol) to be introduced in manageable stages rather than all at once, which helped acceptance; the Qur’an itself answers this exact objection from opponents in Surah al-Furqan 25:32 — “that We may strengthen your heart thereby, and We have spaced it distinctly.”

Examiner note: Candidates frequently answer this as “different ways revelation came to the Prophet” (bell-sound, angel as a man, etc.) instead of narrating the specific story of that first night — that general-forms answer belongs to a different question and scores poorly here. Tell the actual sequence of events with names (Jibreel, Khadija, Waraqah) and the “Iqra” x3 detail to hit full AO1 marks.
PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · PAPER 1, Q2 · MAY/JUNE 2023 (J23 S11)

(a) Describe the Prophet’s experience of the first revelation at age 40. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. At age 40, during a retreat in the Cave of Hira on Jabal al-Nur, the angel Jibreel appeared to Muhammad (pbuh) for the first time.
  2. Jibreel commanded “Iqra”; the Prophet replied he could not read, and was pressed tightly by the angel — this happened three times.
  3. Jibreel then recited the opening verses of Surah al-’Alaq (96:1–5).
  4. The Prophet returned home shaking and afraid, asking Khadija to cover him.
  5. Khadija comforted and reassured him, citing his good character as proof God would not abandon him.
  6. She took him to Waraqah ibn Nawfal, her elderly Christian cousin who had studied earlier scripture.
  7. Waraqah recognised the angel as the same one (the Namus) who brought revelation to Musa, and predicted future persecution.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) Importance of the revelation going to a Prophet who could not read or write.” Model answer approach: because he was unlettered (ummi), he could not have composed the Qur’an’s eloquent Arabic himself, which Muslims see as evidence the words came from God, not from him; it rules out the accusation that he copied or reworded earlier scriptures he had read; the miraculous linguistic quality of the Qur’an (i’jaz al-Qur’an) is strengthened precisely because its source could not read; it also mirrors how the very first verses revealed to him were themselves about the act of reading and writing — a sign given to a man who could not do either.

Examiner note: Weaker answers repeat AO1 narrative in part (b) instead of directly addressing “importance.” Make sure part (b) explicitly uses reasoning words like “because,” “this shows,” or “this proves” to connect the fact (unlettered Prophet) to its significance (authenticity of the message).
PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · PAPER 1, Q2 · OCT/NOV 2025 (N25 W11)

(a) Describe the first revelation, and the roles played by Jibril, Khadija and Waraqah ibn Nawfal. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Jibril’s role: appeared to the Prophet in the Cave of Hira; commanded “Iqra” three times, embracing him tightly each time; recited the first revealed verses, Surah al-’Alaq 96:1–5.
  2. Khadija’s role: when the Prophet returned home trembling and asked to be covered, she comforted him physically and then reassured him verbally, reasoning that God would not disgrace a man of his known honesty and generosity.
  3. Khadija’s further role: she took her husband to her cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal for an outside, expert opinion.
  4. Waraqah’s role: as a scholar familiar with the Torah and Gospel, he identified the angel as the Namus who had brought revelation to Musa, confirming that this was genuine prophecy.
  5. Waraqah’s further role: he predicted that the Prophet’s own people would turn him out and treat him with hostility, and promised his own support if he lived to see it.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) Significance of God sending the first revelation through an angel.” Model answer approach: it matches the pattern described in the Qur’an itself (42:51) for how God communicates with His chosen messengers, showing continuity with earlier prophets such as Musa; using an intermediary (rather than direct, unmediated contact) reflects God’s transcendence; it made the revelation recognisable to someone trained in earlier scripture — Waraqah could identify the Namus specifically because the method matched what he already knew about how God had revealed the Torah; it also protected the Prophet from being overwhelmed, since even an angelic messenger’s presence left him shaken and physically affected.

Examiner note: This question rewards keeping the three names distinct rather than blending them into one paragraph — examiners are explicitly looking for a clear role for each of Jibril, Khadija and Waraqah, so structure your answer person-by-person or event-by-event rather than as one continuous narrative blur.
