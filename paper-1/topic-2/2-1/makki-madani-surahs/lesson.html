Makki and Madani Surahs

2.1 The Revelation of the Qur’an · classifying every surah by when it was revealed

Two surahs can sound like they belong to different books: one thunders in short, urgent verses about the sun going dark and the stars falling; another calmly lays out, over many long verses, exactly how a family’s inheritance should be divided. They are not from different books — they are from different moments in the same twenty-three-year revelation, and learning to tell those moments apart is what this lesson is about.

□ Predict before you read

Before you read on: which of the two surahs described above — the short, urgent one about cosmic signs, or the long, calm one about inheritance law — do you think came first in the Prophet’s mission, and why might the order matter for how a Muslim reads either one today? Hold onto your answer; you’ll test it against the actual pattern by the end of the lesson.

One revelation, two eras

Scholars of the Qur’an divide its 114 surahs (and, more precisely, its individual verses) into two broad categories: Makki (Makkan) and Madani (Madinan). The obvious guess is that this is simply about geography — which city the words came down in. It isn’t, quite, and getting that distinction right is worth marks on its own.

The definition scholars actually use

The standard, majority definition used by scholars of ’Uloom al-Qur’aan (“the sciences of the Qur’an”) is chronological, not geographical: a verse is Makki if it was revealed at any point before the Prophet’s migration (Hijrah) to Madinah in 622 CE, and Madani if it was revealed after the Hijrah — regardless of where in the world the Prophet physically was standing when it came down. Some scholars have proposed alternative definitions — classifying by the actual place of revelation, or by which audience a verse addresses — but these run into real problems: plenty of “Makki-era” verses were revealed on journeys away from Makkah (for instance during the Hijrah itself), and, more strikingly, entire surahs revealed inside or near Makkah years after the Hijrah — such as Surah al-Fath, revealed near Hudaybiyyah when the Prophet returned toward Makkah in 6 AH — are still classified as Madani, because by then the Hijrah had already happened. Chronology, not location, is what actually does the classifying.

How scholars actually know which is which

Given that this classification depends on timing rather than an obvious label attached to each verse, how did scholars establish it at all? Two methods:

1

Transmitted testimony (naql)

Direct reports from the Companions and the earliest generation of Muslims, who had witnessed the circumstances of a verse’s revelation firsthand and passed that information down.

2

Deduction from internal evidence (ijtihaad)

Where no direct testimony survived, scholars examined a verse’s language, themes, and the historical situation it responds to, and inferred its period from recognisable internal patterns — the same patterns that let students today learn to recognise Makki and Madani style on sight.

“By Time. Indeed, mankind is in loss, except for those who believe and do righteous deeds and advise each other to truth and advise each other to patience.” Qur’an 103:1–3 (Surah al-’Asr) — a short, oath-opened, Makki-style surah
“Allah instructs you concerning your children: for the male, what is equal to the share of two females…” Qur’an 4:11 (Surah an-Nisa) — a long, detailed, Madani-style legal ruling

Makki verses — the earlier, Makkan-period style

  • Themes: establishing core belief — the Oneness of God (tawhid), the reality of the afterlife (Paradise and Hell), and stories of earlier prophets and the nations that rejected them.
  • Purpose: confronting the polytheism of the Quraysh and calling individuals to personal faith, before any Muslim community or state existed to legislate for.
  • Style: generally shorter surahs and verses, frequent oaths (“By the sun… by the moon…”), vivid, urgent, often poetic language, and heavy repetition for emphasis.
  • Address: often opens with the universal “O mankind” (yaa ayyuhan-naas), since the audience was still the whole unconverted city, not an established believing community.
  • Disjointed letters: the mysterious opening letters (al-huroof al-muqatta’ah, e.g. Alif-Laam-Meem) appear at the start of 29 surahs — all but two of which are Makki.
  • Sajdah verses: most of the Qur’an’s recognised prostration verses occur in Makki surahs.

Madani verses — the later, Madinan-period style

  • Themes: practical legislation — family law, inheritance, commerce, criminal penalties, the rules of treaties and warfare, and guidance for governing an actual, growing community.
  • Purpose: organising and regulating a Muslim society that now existed, including its relationships with hypocrites (munafiqoon) within Madinah and Jewish and Christian communities nearby.
  • Style: generally longer surahs and verses, more measured, legally precise language, with detailed conditions and exceptions spelled out.
  • Address: characteristically opens with “O you who believe” (yaa ayyuhal-ladheena aamanoo), addressing an already-established community of believers directly.
  • Disjointed letters: only two of the 29 surahs that open with disjointed letters are Madani — Surah al-Baqarah and Surah Aal-’Imran — a well-known exception worth remembering precisely because it breaks the pattern.
  • Length pattern: although Madani surahs are far fewer in number (roughly a quarter of the total), they make up a large share of the Qur’an’s total length, because they run so much longer verse-for-verse.

Categories, not just a two-way split

Because the Qur’an as a finished book is arranged by length and theme rather than by the order verses were revealed, individual surahs don’t always fall neatly into one category. Scholars recognise three categories:

1

Purely Makki

Surahs revealed in their entirety before the Hijrah, with no later material added, such as Surah al-’Alaq (96), the surah of the first revelation, and Surah al-Fatiha.

2

Purely Madani

Surahs revealed in their entirety after the Hijrah, such as Surah al-Baqarah, the Qur’an’s longest surah and a dense collection of Madinan-period law.

3

Mixed surahs

Surahs that, as they appear in the mushaf today, combine verses from both periods; a surah’s overall classification is then usually decided by whichever period contributed the majority of its verses, or by where its opening verses were revealed.

Why the mushaf’s order doesn’t tell you the story

It’s worth stating plainly, because it trips students up: the order surahs appear in the Qur’an today has nothing to do with the order they were revealed in. Surah al-Baqarah, for example, sits second in the mushaf but was revealed years into the Madinan period; Surah al-’Alaq, which contains the very first words ever revealed, sits 96th. Knowing a surah’s Makki/Madani classification is one of the main tools scholars use to reconstruct the actual chronological sequence of revelation, separate from the book’s final, thematic arrangement.

Why any of this earns marks

1

Context for interpretation

Knowing whether a verse addresses new believers in a hostile city or an established community in Madinah stops a student from misreading who a verse is actually speaking to.

2

Explains the shift from belief to law

It shows why the Qur’an spends its earlier years on faith and its later years on legislation: you cannot sensibly legislate inheritance or criminal penalties for a community that doesn’t exist yet and doesn’t yet believe in God.

3

Helps resolve apparent contradictions

Where two verses seem to give different guidance on the same subject, knowing which came first (Makki or Madani, and roughly when within each period) is the essential first step in understanding abrogation (naskh) and the refinement of rulings over time.

4

Corroborates the historical record

The internal evidence within the verses themselves (their themes, their audience, their concerns) independently matches the outside historical account of the Prophet’s life, reinforcing that the Qur’an’s text and Islamic history describe the same real events.

□ Common mix-up to avoid — “Makki” does not mean “revealed in Makkah”

It’s tempting to assume the names are purely geographic, but the standard definition is chronological (before/after the Hijrah), not locational. The clearest proof is Surah al-Fath: revealed near Hudaybiyyah, right outside Makkah, in the sixth year after the Hijrah — geographically about as “Makkan” as a revelation gets — yet it is classified as Madani, because it came after the migration. If an exam-style question ever asks you to justify a surah’s classification, reach for “before or after the Hijrah,” never “where the Prophet happened to be standing.”
