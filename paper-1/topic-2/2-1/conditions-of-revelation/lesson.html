Conditions of Revelation

2.1 The Revelation of the Qur’an · the different forms wahy took across 23 years

Revelation was never one single experience the Prophet (pbuh) simply repeated 6,000-odd times. It arrived as a true dream, as a voice from behind a veil, as the sound of a ringing bell, as a man sitting beside him — and each form left a different mark on him that people nearby could actually see. This lesson is about how revelation came, not why it was spaced out or what happened the very first time — those are covered elsewhere.

□ Predict before you read

Before reading on: if you had been sitting near the Prophet the exact moment revelation began, what physical signs do you think might have tipped you off that something was happening to him — before he said a single word? Write down at least two guesses, then check them against the actual recorded evidence later in this lesson.

The three channels the Qur’an itself names

The Qur’an sets out, in a single verse, the complete range of ways God communicates with a chosen messenger:

“It is not possible for any human being that Allah should speak to him except by inspiration, or from behind a veil, or that He sends a messenger to reveal, by His permission, what He wills.” Qur’an 42:51

That gives three categories: direct inspiration with no intermediary at all, speech from behind a veil, and revelation through a sent messenger — an angel. Scholars of ’Uloom al-Qur’aan group the first two together as “without an intermediary” and treat the third as its own category, “with an intermediary,” because in practice almost everything the Prophet (pbuh) received — including the entire text of the Qur’an — came through the third route.

Without an intermediary (rare, and never how the Qur’an’s text arrived)

  • True dreams — the very first form of revelation the Prophet experienced, before prophethood even began. ’A’ishah reported that his early dreams “came true like the rising of the sun.” Ibrahim’s dream commanding him to sacrifice Isma’il (37:102) and the Prophet’s own dream of entering Makkah for ’Umrah — later fulfilled through the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah (48:27) — are both examples of prophetic dreams carrying real instruction.
  • Direct speech from behind a veil — used when God spoke to Musa at Mount Tur (7:143), and reported for the Prophet himself during the Night Journey and Ascension (al-Isra’ wal-Mi’raj), when a companion asked him directly whether he had seen God and he answered, “Light — how could I see Him?”, describing a veil of light between them.
  • A small number of scholars have argued a handful of Qur’anic verses came this way, but the strongest scholarly position is that the Qur’an’s actual text was never delivered without Jibreel as intermediary — these two channels carried other forms of guidance, not the revealed wording itself.

With an intermediary — via the angel Jibreel (the only route the Qur’an’s text took)

  • Jibreel first heard the Qur’an directly from God; a hadith describes the angels of heaven falling into a swoon at the sound of God’s speech, with Jibreel the first to raise his head and pass the message on.
  • Jibreel then delivered it to the Prophet in one of two forms: as the sound of a ringing bell — described as the hardest form to receive — or as an angel appearing in the shape of a man, sitting and speaking directly, which was easier.
  • On rare occasions, Jibreel appeared to the Prophet in his true, immense angelic form — most famously at the very first revelation in the Cave of Hira (see the “First Revelation” lesson).
  • This route — Jibreel, in one of his forms — is how virtually the entire Qur’an reached the Prophet over 23 years.
“Sometimes it is [revealed] like the ringing of a bell, and that is the hardest on me; then it leaves me and I have grasped what he said. And sometimes the angel comes to me in the form of a man and talks to me, and I grasp whatever he says.” Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), replying to a direct question from a companion about how revelation felt, narrated by ’A’ishah, Sahih al-Bukhari

What it looked like from the outside

Companions who were physically present when revelation descended left specific, sometimes startling descriptions — this is the material that turns a vague answer (“it was hard for him”) into a precise, mark-worthy one.

1

Sweating, even in cold weather

’A’ishah reported that on the coldest of days, his forehead would be running with sweat by the time a revelation ended.

2

A change in his face and posture

The companion ’Ubadah ibn as-Samit described the Prophet’s face changing colour and his head lowering during the experience.

3

A reddened face and a sound like snoring

Ya’la ibn Umayyah, who once stood close enough to watch a revelation descend while the Prophet was in the state of ihram, described his face turning deep red, accompanied by a sound resembling snoring, before he returned to normal and was able to answer the question that had prompted the revelation.

4

A physical sense of weight

The companion Zayd ibn Thabit reported that when he was seated with his thigh beneath the Prophet’s, revelation once descended, and the Prophet’s thigh grew so heavy against his own that he feared it would be crushed. The Qur’an itself describes revelation in exactly these terms: “Verily, We shall cast upon you a heavy word” (Qur’an 73:5).

Why the “hardest” form still mattered

It would be easy to assume God would only ever choose the easiest method available. Instead, the harder bell-like form continued throughout the 23 years alongside the easier man-form — a reminder that revelation was never designed purely for the Prophet’s comfort, but to convey something of the seriousness and weight of what was being delivered. Scholars point to this as one more reason the physical toll of revelation is treated as significant evidence in its own right: an experience this consistently and visibly demanding, sustained for over two decades, is not easily explained as something the Prophet could have simply performed or imagined at will.

□ Where this comes from

The framework of the three channels named in Qur’an 42:51, the two forms of revelation “without an intermediary” (dreams; direct speech from behind a veil) and the two-stage chain “with an intermediary” (God to Jibreel; Jibreel to the Prophet, as either the bell-sound or a man) are drawn from Chapter 3, “Inspiration — al-Wahy,” Section III (“The Procedure of Wahy”), of Abu Ammaar Yasir Qadhi’s An Introduction to the Sciences of the Qur’aan (Al-Hidaayah, 1999), including his citation of the hadith of ’A’ishah (narrated via Harith ibn Hisham’s question, reported in Sahih al-Bukhari) and the account of the angels’ reaction when God speaks. The physical-effects details (’Ubadah ibn as-Samit’s account, Ya’la ibn Umayyah’s account, and Zayd ibn Thabit’s account) are cross-checked hadith material also used in this project’s earlier textbook chapter on 2.1. Qur’an citations are given in standard English translation.

□ Common mix-up to avoid — three different “revelation” lessons, three different questions

This unit now has three lessons that all touch “revelation,” and past papers test them separately. Use this lesson (Conditions of Revelation) for any question about the forms revelation took — “different ways the Prophet received revelation.” Use the “First Revelation” lesson for the specific story of the Cave of Hira, Khadija and Waraqah. Use the “Why Gradual Revelation” lesson for any question about timing and pacing — why it took 23 years rather than arriving complete. Mixing these up is one of the most common ways marks are lost on this topic, because the three question types sound similar but expect completely different content.

□ Elaborate

The Qur’an’s actual text was never revealed through a dream or through direct, unmediated speech — both valid channels named in 42:51 — only ever through Jibreel. Why might it matter, as evidence of authenticity, that the Qur’an consistently came the “harder,” more externally verifiable way (an angel appearing, with observable physical effects) rather than through a private, unverifiable dream that only the Prophet himself could ever have confirmed?

(a) Different ways the Prophet received revelation. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Sometimes as true dreams, before the start of his prophethood, which always came true — though the Qur’an’s own text was never delivered this way.
  2. Sometimes as direct speech from behind a veil, with no intermediary — the method God used with Musa at Mount Tur, and reported for the Prophet during the Night Journey (al-Isra’ wal-Mi’raj).
  3. Most commonly through the angel Jibreel as a sent messenger — the route the entire Qur’an actually took.
  4. Within that route, sometimes as the sound of a ringing bell, described as the hardest form to receive.
  5. Sometimes as Jibreel appearing in the form of a man, sitting and speaking directly — an easier form.
  6. Rarely, Jibreel appeared in his true, immense angelic form, as at the very first revelation in the Cave of Hira.
  7. Physical signs accompanying revelation included sweating even in cold weather, changes in his face and colour, and on one occasion such a sense of weight that a companion’s thigh, pressed beneath his, was feared to be crushed.
