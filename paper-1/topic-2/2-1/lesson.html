2.1 · The Revelation of the Qur’an

610 – 632 CE · Cave of Hira, Makkah & Madinah · al-Wahy, the descent of the divine word

For twenty-three years, a message arrived to one man in fragments — sometimes five verses, sometimes a single phrase — through a process so physically overwhelming it left him sweating on cold mornings. Understanding how that process worked is worth as many marks as understanding what was revealed.

□ Predict before you read

Every scripture Muslims believe came before the Qur’an — the Tawrah given to Musa, the Injeel given to ‘Isa — is described as having been handed down essentially complete. The Qur’an alone arrived in pieces, over more than two decades, often verse by verse in response to a specific event. Before reading on: write down two reasons you think that difference might exist. You'll meet the examiner's version of your answer later in this lesson — compare notes.

What exactly is ‘Wahy’?

Muslims call the process by which God communicated the Qur’an to the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) al-wahy — inspiration, or divine communication. It was not a new phenomenon invented for him: the Qur’an states that God has always spoken to His chosen messengers this way, naming Nuh, Ibrahim, Isma’il, Ishaq, Ya’qub, ’Isa, Ayyub and others as recipients of wahy before him (Qur’an 4:163–165), and describing the general principle directly:

“It is not for any human being that Allah should speak to him except by revelation, or from behind a veil, or that He sends a messenger to reveal, by His permission, what He wills.” Qur’an 42:51

That single verse is effectively the syllabus for this section: it names the three channels through which revelation reached a prophet, and Muhammad (pbuh) is the only messenger in Islamic belief to have experienced all three. So central was this connection that when he died in 11 AH, his household's freed servant Umm Ayman was found weeping. Asked whether she wept because the Prophet had died and moved to something better, she replied that she knew that — she wept because revelation from the sky had now stopped. Abu Bakr and ’Umar wept with her.

How revelation physically arrived

Only two of those three channels concern the delivery of the Qur’anic text itself, and both run through the angel Jibreel. ’A’ishah, the Prophet's wife, was asked directly what revelation felt like to him, and her answer is one of the most quoted hadiths in this whole topic — it is worth memorising close to word for word:

“Sometimes it is (revealed) like the ringing of a bell, and that is the hardest on me; then it leaves me and I have grasped what he said. And sometimes the angel comes to me in the form of a man and talks to me, and I grasp whatever he says.” Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), narrated by ’A’ishah · Sahih al-Bukhari

’A’ishah adds physical detail from her own observation: on even the coldest mornings, his forehead would be running with sweat by the time a revelation ended. Another companion, ’Ubadah ibn as-Samit, describes his face changing colour and his head lowering during the experience. A third narration — from Ya’la ibn Umayyah, who once stood close enough to watch a revelation descend while the Prophet was in ihram — records his face turning deep red and a sound like snoring before he returned to normal, now able to answer the question that had prompted the revelation. Jibreel appeared to him in his true angelic form, filling the horizon, on only a handful of occasions — most famously the very first time, in the cave.

□ Where this comes from

This lesson draws on Abu Ammaar Yasir Qadhi's academic study An Introduction to the Sciences of the Qur’aan (Al-Hidaayah, 1999) for the classification of wahy, cross-checked against the hadith collections of Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim directly. Where the Qur’an or a hadith is quoted, treat the quotation itself as exam-ready — examiners consistently reward candidates who can cite the actual wording rather than paraphrase it.

The night in the cave

By the age of forty, Muhammad (pbuh) had developed a habit of withdrawing for days at a time to a small cave on Jabal al-Nur, a mountain just outside Makkah, to reflect in solitude. It was there, in Ramadan of the year that would come to be counted as 610 CE, that Jibreel appeared to him for the first time and issued a single command: اقرأ — “Iqra”, “Read.”

Ramadan, 610 CE

Jibreel commands “Iqra” — three times

The Prophet, who could not read, replied each time, “I am not one who reads.” Each time Jibreel embraced him tightly until he reached the limit of his endurance, then released him and repeated the command.

Same night

The first five verses are given

Jibreel then recited what became the opening of Surah al-’Alaq (96:1–5): “Read in the name of your Lord who created — created man from a clinging substance. Read, and your Lord is the Most Generous — who taught by the pen — taught man that which he knew not.”

Same night

He returns home trembling

He hurried to his wife Khadijah and said, “Cover me! Cover me!” — convinced something terrible had happened to him.

Same night

Khadijah's reassurance

She answered him without hesitation, before either of them understood what had occurred.

Days later

Waraqah ibn Nawfal confirms it

Khadijah's elderly cousin, a Christian familiar with the earlier scriptures, identifies the visitor and predicts what is coming.

□ In her own words — Khadijah bint Khuwaylid

“Never! By Allah, Allah will never disgrace you. You keep good relations with your kith and kin, help the poor and the destitute, serve your guests generously, and assist those afflicted by calamity.”

She then took him to her cousin, Waraqah ibn Nawfal, an elderly man who had studied the Christian scriptures. On hearing the account, Waraqah said:

“This is the Namus [the same one who keeps secrets, i.e. Jibreel] that Allah sent down to Musa. I wish I were young, and could live up to the time when your people will turn you out.” The Prophet asked, “Will they turn me out?” Waraqah replied, “Yes — never has a man brought something similar to what you have brought without being treated with hostility. If I live to see that day, I will support you strongly.”

Sahih al-Bukhari 3, narrated by ’A’ishah

□ Elaborate — explain it in your own words

Three different people responded to the same event in three different ways: the Prophet with fear, Khadijah with immediate practical faith, Waraqah with scriptural recognition and an accurate prediction of persecution to come. Without looking back at the text, explain out loud (or on paper) why each of their three reactions matters as evidence for Muslims that the revelation was genuine. This kind of reasoning is exactly what an AO2 “(b) part” question is testing.

The pause in revelation, and the second wahy

After that first night in the cave, revelation stopped. Scholars call this gap al-fatra — reports differ on exactly how long it lasted — and the Prophet (pbuh) was left anxious and longing for it to return.

“While I was walking, I heard a voice from the sky. I looked up, and there was the same angel who had come to me in Hira, sitting on a throne between the sky and the earth. I was terrified of him and went back home…” Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), narrated in Sahih al-Bukhari

This time Jibreel appeared in his true, enormous angelic form, filling the horizon wherever the Prophet turned his gaze, and identified himself directly: “O Muhammad, you are the Messenger of Allah, and I am Jibreel.” The fear passed, the fatra ended, and shortly afterwards Surah al-Muddaththir (74:1–5) was revealed — “O you who is covered! Arise and warn…” — formally launching his public mission as a warner.

Why twenty-three years, and not one day?

The Prophet's opponents in Makkah raised this exact objection during his lifetime, and the Qur’an answers it directly:

“And those who disbelieve say, ‘Why was the Qur’an not revealed to him all at once?’ Thus [it is] that We may strengthen your heart thereby, and We have spaced it distinctly.” Qur’an 25:32

Scholars of ’uloom al-Qur’an draw out several distinct benefits from that answer — and a strong exam response cites more than one:

1

It strengthened the Prophet's heart

Facing more than a decade of ridicule and persecution in Makkah with no guarantee of what came next, a steady trickle of support and reassurance sustained him in a way a single, distant text could not.

2

It matched how the message could be memorised and lived

The first community was largely non-literate. Verses arriving in manageable portions could be memorised precisely, understood, and put into practice before the next portion arrived.

3

It allowed law to be introduced gradually

Deeply rooted habits — the clearest example is the drinking of alcohol — were addressed across several revelations of increasing firmness (2:219, then 4:43, then finally 5:90–91) rather than an outright ban imposed on a society not yet ready to keep it.

4

It let revelation answer real events as they happened

Many verses were sent down in direct response to a question the Prophet was asked, a dispute in the community, or a specific incident — this is the science of asbab al-nuzul, the “occasions of revelation.” A single one-time text could not have done this.

5

It stood as evidence against the charge of forgery

An unlettered man inventing scripture alone would have produced it all at once, from memory of what he already believed. A message that kept arriving, unpredictably, over twenty-three years, responding to events he could not have anticipated, is much harder to explain as human authorship.

□ One layer deeper — how scholars describe the descent itself

Qadhi's study outlines a majority scholarly view that revelation moved in three stages before reaching the Prophet: first inscribed in full on the Lawh al-Mahfooz (the Preserved Tablet, Qur’an 85:21–22); then sent down in its entirety to the lowest heaven on Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Decree, in Ramadan (97:1); and only then delivered to the Prophet piecemeal across the following twenty-three years as circumstances required. Ibn ’Abbas is recorded explaining it this way, and Imam Abu Shaamah later argued the middle stage let Muhammad's revelation both match the pattern of earlier prophets and still be distinguished by its gradual delivery. This detail sits above what the exam requires — but a candidate who can mention it accurately signals real depth of reading.

Makki and Madani surahs

Every surah in the Qur’an is classified by scholars according to whether it came before or after the Hijrah — a two-way split examiners expect you to know by name, count and character.

Makki surahs

  • Count: 86 surahs, revealed in and around Makkah, 610–622 CE — before the Hijrah.
  • Style: generally shorter surahs, with short, rhythmic verses and forceful, vivid language.
  • Themes: tawhid, core Islamic ethics, stories of earlier prophets and destroyed nations, warnings of the Last Day, and open invitation (da’wah) to Islam — much of it also consoling and strengthening the Prophet (pbuh) through years of persecution.

Madani surahs

  • Count: 28 surahs, revealed after the Hijrah to Madinah in 622 CE.
  • Style: generally longer surahs, with longer, more detailed, legislative verses.
  • Themes: detailed Islamic law (shari’ah), the practice of the five pillars, family and social legislation, treaties and relations with other communities, and the battles and events of the growing Muslim state at Madinah.

86 + 28 = 114, the total number of surahs in the Qur’an. A useful rule of thumb, not an absolute one: Makkan surahs address the individual's belief before there was a state to legislate for; Madani surahs address the community once one existed.

Further evidence of how physically demanding revelation was

Beyond ’A’ishah's description of the bell-like sound and the sweat on his forehead, several other reports capture just how overwhelming receiving revelation could be:

1

“My soul had been torn away from me”

The Prophet (pbuh) himself said: “Never once did I receive a revelation without thinking that my soul had been torn away from me.”

2

His riding animal felt the weight

On more than one occasion, while the Prophet was riding, a revelation descended and his mount's legs strained under a sudden, otherwise inexplicable weight.

3

Zayd ibn Thabit felt it too

Zayd ibn Thabit reported that once, while his own thigh was placed beneath the Prophet's during a revelation, it grew so heavy he feared it would be crushed — and separately described feeling intense heat from the Prophet as revelation came down.

Preservation of the Qur’an during the Prophet's lifetime

Even though receiving revelation could be physically overwhelming, the text itself was carefully safeguarded from the very start — well before it was ever gathered into a single book.

1

Written down by literate companions

The Prophet appointed scribes of revelation, who recorded each verse as it came — on whatever material was available: pieces of leather, flat stones, the shoulder-bones of camels, and palm-leaf stalks.

2

Recorded in the order it arrived, not the order it was later arranged

Because revelation came down in portions responding to specific situations across 23 years, verses were not written — or later arranged in the mus-haf — strictly in the order they were revealed.

3

Checked annually with Jibreel

Every Ramadan the Prophet recited the whole of the Qur’an revealed so far to Jibreel, who reviewed it with him — a check on accuracy that happened twice in the final Ramadan of his life.

4

Not yet a single book

What did not exist yet was one bound volume gathering every verse together in a fixed order — that step only came after the Prophet's death, once revelation itself had ended (covered in full in Topic 2.2).

□ Common mix-up to avoid

Don't confuse the Prophet's yearly review of the Qur’an with Jibreel during his own lifetime with the later event where companions compared their written copies against each other. That second event — triggered by Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman's warning about regional recitation disputes — belongs to ’Uthman's standardisation in Topic 2.2, roughly two decades after the Prophet's death, not to the preservation process described here.

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · PAPER 1, Q2

(a) Describe the different ways in which the Prophet (pbuh) received revelation. 10 marks

This exact question — in close variants — has appeared in May/June 2021, May/June 2022, May/June 2023, Oct/Nov 2024 and Nov 2025 sessions. It is one of the most reliably recurring questions in the whole syllabus. Model answer structure for the 10-mark (a) part:

AO1 · model answer points

  1. True dreams, before formal prophethood began — ’A’ishah reports these always came true, “like the break of dawn.”
  2. Direct speech from Allah, described as “from behind a veil” — the method used with Musa at Mount Tur, and with the Prophet during the Night Journey (al-Isra wal-Mi’raj).
  3. Through the angel Jibreel appearing “like the ringing of a bell” — described by the Prophet himself as the hardest form to receive.
  4. Through the angel Jibreel appearing in the form of a man, speaking to him directly — easier to receive, and the form in which companions sometimes witnessed him receiving revelation.
  5. Jibreel appearing in his true, enormous angelic form — witnessed only rarely, including at the very first revelation in the Cave of Hira.
  6. Physical evidence accompanying revelation: sweating even in cold weather, a change in facial colour, a sound resembling snoring, and a temporary loss of awareness of his surroundings.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

A typical follow-up: “Why was it important the Qur’an wasn't revealed all at once?” Answer using the five reasons from the Steps section above — pick two, explain each in a sentence, and where possible tie one back to a named example (the staged prohibition of alcohol is the strongest, most specific example to use).

Examiner pattern: across the 2021–2025 papers, candidates consistently lose marks for naming only one or two methods of revelation and stopping, rather than working through the full range above. Aim to cover at least four distinct methods with one supporting detail each, rather than one method described at length.
PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · PAPER 1, Q2

(a) Describe the Prophet's (pbuh) experience of receiving the first revelation, including the roles of Jibreel, Khadijah and Waraqah ibn Nawfal. 10 marks

A close relative of this question has appeared in May/June 2022, May/June 2023 and Nov 2025 — a distinct family from the “different ways revelation came” question above. This one wants the narrative of the very first revelation, not the general classification of revelation-types — naming Jibreel, Khadijah or Waraqah, or saying “the first revelation,” is the signal to switch to this answer instead.

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Context: at forty years old, the Prophet regularly withdrew to the Cave of Hira on Jabal al-Nur to reflect in solitude.
  2. Jibreel appeared and commanded “Iqra” (“Read”) three times, embracing him tightly each time the Prophet said he could not read.
  3. Jibreel then recited the first five verses of Surah al-’Alaq (96:1–5).
  4. The Prophet returned home trembling, saying “Cover me! Cover me!”, convinced something terrible had happened to him.
  5. Khadijah reassured him immediately and without hesitation, citing his honesty, kindness and generosity as reasons Allah would not disgrace him.
  6. She took him to her cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal, who identified the visitor as the Namus — the same angel who came to Musa — and predicted the Prophet would face hostility from his own people.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks, two recurring angles

Angle 1 (J23 s11): “Why is it important the revelation came to a prophet who could not read or write?” An unlettered man producing language this eloquent and structured is presented by Muslims as evidence the words could not be his own composition — strengthening the claim that the Qur’an is divine speech, not human authorship.

Angle 2 (N25 w11): “Why is it significant that God sent the first revelation through an angel?” Qur’an 42:51 states no human speaks with God except by revelation, from behind a veil, or through a sent messenger — using an angel let the Prophet receive the message directly and repeatedly, in the same form earlier prophets such as Musa had received it, linking his prophethood to theirs.

Examiner pattern: candidates who prepared only the “different ways revelation came” answer often reuse it here and lose marks for not telling the actual story. Prepare both answers separately, and check which one the question wording is actually asking for before writing.
