Preservation of the Qur’an

2.1 The Revelation of the Qur’an · how memorisation and writing safeguarded the text during the Prophet’s own lifetime

By the time the Prophet (pbuh) died in 11 AH, not one verse of the Qur’an had been lost — and yet no single bound book existed anywhere in Arabia containing all of it in order. That is not a contradiction. It is the result of two independent safety nets running side by side for twenty-three years: hundreds of companions who carried the entire text in memory, and a body of scribes who wrote each verse down the moment it was revealed. This lesson stays inside the Prophet’s own lifetime — what happened to the text after his death, when those scattered written pieces were finally gathered into one mus-haf, belongs to a different unit and is covered there.

□ Predict before you read

Before reading on: if there was no printing press, no paper as we know it, and revelation kept arriving piece by piece for over two decades, how do you think the Prophet (pbuh) and his companions made sure nothing was ever lost or changed along the way? Write down at least two methods you’d guess they relied on, then compare them with what actually happened below.

Two tracks, not one

’Uloom al-Qur’aan scholarship describes preservation during the Prophet’s lifetime as running on two parallel tracks at once, each acting as a check on the other. The first was memorisation: as each portion of revelation arrived, the Prophet recited it aloud to those present, and companions — drawing on a culture that already prized memorising long tribal poetry — committed it to memory on the spot. The second was writing: the Prophet also had verses recorded in real time by a body of companions known as the Scribes of the Revelation (Kuttab al-Wahy), so that the text existed in a fixed physical form as well as a remembered one. Neither track depended on the other for its accuracy — which is exactly the point.

Why there was still no single book yet

It would be reasonable to ask why, with both memorisation and writing already happening, the Qur’an wasn’t simply bound into one volume during the Prophet’s own lifetime. The answer is structural, not accidental: revelation was still arriving, right up until shortly before his death, and portions could still be abrogated (naskh) or have their place within a surah reassigned by fresh instruction from Jibreel. Binding a finished book before the process of revelation itself had finished would have made no sense — and there was, in any case, a living authority on hand, the Prophet himself, who could confirm the correct wording and order at any moment. That authority is precisely what disappeared at his death, which is why compiling a single fixed mus-haf became urgent immediately afterwards, in a different chapter of this story.

Track one — Memorisation (the human safety net)

  • Verses were memorised by companions the moment they were recited, drawing on the strong oral-memory culture of pre-Islamic Arabia.
  • Those who memorised the complete Qur’an were called the huffaz; by the time of the Prophet’s death, they numbered in the hundreds.
  • The Prophet himself named specific companions as reliable teachers of recitation: “Learn the recitation of the Qur’an from four: ’Abdullah ibn Mas’ud, Salim (the freed slave of Abu Hudhayfah), Mu’adh ibn Jabal, and Ubay ibn Ka’b” (Sahih al-Bukhari).
  • This track had no physical object that could be lost, burned or damaged — but it depended entirely on human memory staying accurate across a growing number of people.

Track two — Writing (the physical safety net)

  • The Prophet appointed dedicated Scribes of the Revelation, among them a young Ansari companion named Zayd ibn Thabit, to record each verse as it was revealed.
  • Verses were written on whatever material was to hand: palm-leaf stalks, thin flat stones, the shoulder blades of animals, pieces of leather, and parchment.
  • The Prophet personally directed, on Jibreel’s instruction, exactly where each new verse belonged within its surah — arrangement was never left to a scribe’s own judgement.
  • This track produced a fixed physical record that memory alone could never provide — but the pieces were scattered across many hands and materials, not yet gathered in one place.
“Learn the recitation of the Qur’an from four: ’Abdullah ibn Mas’ud, Salim (the freed slave of Abu Hudhayfah), Mu’adh ibn Jabal, and Ubay ibn Ka’b.” Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), Sahih al-Bukhari

Jibreel’s yearly check

A third safeguard ran on top of the other two: an annual review. Every Ramadan, Jibreel came and reviewed with the Prophet everything that had been revealed of the Qur’an so far — companions understood this as a formal, angel-verified confirmation that nothing had drifted or been forgotten. In the very last Ramadan of the Prophet’s life, this review happened twice rather than once, a detail companions only understood in hindsight as a sign that his death was near. Zayd ibn Thabit — the same companion who had served as a scribe throughout the Madani period — is reported to have been present for this final, doubled review, which is part of why Abu Bakr later turned to him specifically when the text needed to be gathered into one volume.

The Scribes’ wider job

It’s worth knowing that “Scribes of the Prophet” was a broader title than “Scribes of Revelation” alone. The same body of literate companions who wrote down verses as they were revealed also handled the Prophet’s wider correspondence: letters inviting rulers and tribal leaders to Islam, the terms of treaties such as Hudaybiyyah, and administrative records connected to zakat and treaties with neighbouring tribes. Several dozen companions are recorded as having served as scribes at one time or another across the Makkan and Madani periods — Zayd ibn Thabit and Ubay ibn Ka’b are the two names that recur most often in connection with the Qur’an specifically.

□ Common mix-up to avoid — during the Prophet’s life vs after his death

This lesson covers only the two safety nets that operated while the Prophet was alive: memorisation and real-time writing, with no single bound book yet because revelation itself hadn’t finished. A separate part of this course (unit 2.2, “Compilation of the Qur’an,” already covered in this project’s textbook) picks up the story after his death — Abu Bakr’s compilation following the huffaz losses at the Battle of Yamama, and ’Uthman’s later standardisation into one official text. If an exam question asks about Abu Bakr, ’Umar or ’Uthman by name, or mentions the Battle of Yamama, it belongs to that later unit, not this lesson.

(a) Tasks performed by some Companions as the Prophet’s Scribes. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Writing down verses of the Qur’an as they were revealed, taking dictation directly from the Prophet.
  2. Recording each verse on whatever material was available — palm-leaf stalks, thin flat stones, shoulder blades of animals, leather, or parchment.
  3. Writing the verse in the exact place within its surah that the Prophet specified, following Jibreel’s instruction, rather than choosing an order themselves.
  4. Writing the Prophet’s letters to rulers and tribal leaders inviting them to Islam.
  5. Recording the terms of treaties, such as the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah.
  6. Keeping administrative and financial records, including matters connected to zakat.
  7. Several dozen companions served as scribes at different points; Zayd ibn Thabit and Ubay ibn Ka’b are the two most closely associated with recording the Qur’an itself.

Part (b) — “Benefits of the Qur’an being available to everyone on the internet” (4 marks) — the internet makes the Qur’an instantly accessible worldwide, in the original Arabic and in translation, with audio recitation that helps memorisation and correct pronunciation; it allows searching for specific verses or topics quickly; and countless digital copies make the text effectively impossible to lose or destroy — echoing, in a modern form, exactly the redundancy the Scribes and the huffaz built in fourteen centuries earlier.

Examiner note: Strong answers list several distinct scribal tasks rather than describing Qur’an-writing alone at length — the mark scheme rewards breadth (Qur’an, letters, treaties, records) as much as detail on any one task.
PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · PAPER 1, Q5 · OCT/NOV 2025 (N25 W12)

(a) Main roles played by the Scribes of the Prophet. 10 marks

Use the same seven points listed under J22 s11 above — this (a) question covers essentially the same ground as “tasks performed by the Scribes,” worded as “roles” instead.

Part (b) — “Is it easier to record Islamic knowledge now than in the Prophet’s time?” (4 marks) — a balanced answer weighs both sides: recording is far faster and more widely accessible now (printing, audio, video, digital storage, instant global reach) than relying on scribes writing by hand on stone or leather; but ease of recording is not the same as guaranteed accuracy, and Islamic knowledge today still depends on the same kind of careful verification (checking sources, authenticity, scholarly oversight) that the Scribes and huffaz provided in the Prophet’s own time.

Examiner note: This is a “to what extent” style (b) question dressed as a yes/no — full marks need both a “yes, it’s easier” point and a “but accuracy still needs safeguarding” point, not a one-sided answer.
