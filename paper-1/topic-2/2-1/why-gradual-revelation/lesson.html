Why Gradual Revelation

2.1 The Revelation of the Qur’an · why the text arrived over twenty-three years, not all at once

Every scripture Muslims believe came before the Qur’an — the Tawrah given to Musa, the Injeel given to ’Isa — arrived complete, handed down in one piece. The Qur’an alone came in fragments, over twenty-three years, verse by verse, often in direct response to a single afternoon’s events. The Prophet’s own opponents noticed the difference and used it as a weapon — and the Qur’an answers them directly, in its own text. That answer is this lesson.

□ Predict before you read

Before reading on, write down as many separate reasons as you can think of for why God might have chosen to reveal the Qur’an gradually, over 23 years, rather than all at once like earlier scriptures. Aim for at least three genuinely different reasons — not just restatements of the same idea. You’ll check your list against the examiner’s version by the end of the lesson.

An objection the Qur’an records — and answers

This wasn’t a modern question historians invented later; it was raised by the Prophet’s own opponents in Makkah, and the Qur’an quotes and answers it directly:

“And those who disbelieve say, ‘Why was the Qur’an not revealed to him all at once?’ Thus [it is] that We may strengthen your heart thereby, and We have spaced it distinctly.” Qur’an 25:32

The comparison the objectors had in mind was real: the Qur’an itself confirms that Musa was given the Tawrah complete, in one piece (Qur’an 7:144–154). Muhammad (pbuh) is the only messenger in Islamic belief whose scripture arrived any other way — which makes the question worth taking seriously rather than brushing past.

Three stages, not one descent

Scholars of ’Uloom al-Qur’aan (“the sciences of the Qur’an”) describe the Qur’an’s journey to the Prophet in three distinct stages, and understanding all three is what separates a vague answer from a precise one.

1

Written on al-Lawh al-Mahfuz

The Qur’an, as the eternal speech of God, was inscribed on “the Preserved Tablet” before creation itself, guaranteeing its content and authenticity from the very start (Qur’an 85:21–22, 56:77–78).

2

Sent down complete to the lowest heaven

From the Preserved Tablet, the whole Qur’an descended in a single night to a place called Bayt al-’Izzah (“the House of Honour”) in the lowest heaven. This happened in Ramadan, on Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Decree (Qur’an 2:185, 97:1, 44:3).

3

Revealed piecemeal to the Prophet

From that point, the angel Jibreel brought the Qur’an down to the Prophet Muhammad (pbuh) in portions, as circumstances required, over the following 23 years (Qur’an 26:192–194).

How earlier prophets received scripture

  • Delivered complete, in a single act of revelation (e.g. the Tawrah to Musa, Qur’an 7:144–154).
  • No extended period of piecemeal delivery tied to unfolding events.
  • The pattern the Prophet’s opponents expected the Qur’an to follow too.

How the Qur’an came to Muhammad (pbuh)

  • First sent down complete to the lowest heaven on Laylat al-Qadr — matching the pattern of earlier scripture in that one respect.
  • Then delivered to the Prophet piece by piece over 23 years, according to real events and genuine need.
  • A double honour, according to the classical scholar Abu Shama: the Prophet shared the pattern of earlier prophets at the first descent, then was distinguished from all of them by how it reached him afterwards.

The wisdom behind spacing it out

Scholars of the Qur’an — building on the Qur’an’s own explanation in 25:32 and the wider historical record of how the text unfolded — identify several distinct reasons gradual revelation mattered. Treat each of these as a separate AO2 point in its own right, not a single idea repeated.

1

It strengthened the Prophet’s heart

This is the Qur’an’s own stated reason in 25:32. Revelation arriving at difficult moments — after an insult, a bereavement, a defeat — gave the Prophet reassurance and support exactly when it was needed, rather than leaving him to face 23 years of hardship with nothing further to draw on after year one.

2

It matched how the community actually learned

The Prophet himself could not read or write, and the society around him relied overwhelmingly on memorisation. Short, regular portions were something an oral community could genuinely memorise, understand, and put into practice — a complete book delivered at once would have been far harder to absorb.

3

It allowed law to be introduced gradually, not imposed as a shock

Deeply embedded practices could be phased out in stages rather than banned outright overnight. The clearest example is the prohibition of alcohol, addressed across several revelations rather than one: an early, neutral mention of grapes and dates as a source of both intoxicants and good provision (16:67); then an acknowledgement that it carried “great sin” alongside some benefit (2:219); then a restriction on approaching prayer while intoxicated (4:43); and only then a full prohibition (5:90–91). Each stage prepared the community for the next.

4

It let revelation respond directly to real events

Many verses were revealed in answer to a specific question, dispute, or incident (what scholars call asbab an-nuzul, “the causes of revelation”). Guidance that answers the actual problem in front of a community lands with far more relevance and conviction than abstract rules issued in advance of any need for them.

5

It matched belief to a community that had to be built first

The earlier, Makki-period material concentrated on establishing core belief in one God and the reality of the afterlife; only once a community of believers existed in Madinah did detailed law follow. You cannot sensibly legislate inheritance or criminal punishment for people who do not yet believe in the God issuing the law.

6

It stands as its own kind of proof

A fragmented text, delivered by an unlettered man, across 23 years of wildly different circumstances — war, peace, grief, triumph, exile — and yet remaining stylistically and thematically consistent throughout, with no opponent ever able to point to an internal contradiction despite decades of opportunity to try. Muslims see that consistency under fragmentation as itself a sign the source was not an ordinary human mind.

How the process was protected from corruption

A live 23-year delivery raises an obvious worry: what stopped the message drifting, being misremembered, or altered along the way? Qadhi’s book addresses this directly, and the Qur’an supplies four separate safeguards.

1

The messenger was guaranteed trustworthy

Jibreel is described as a “trustworthy Spirit” (26:193) who does not act except on God’s command (21:27).

2

The Prophet’s memory was guaranteed

When the Prophet tried to rush his own recitation alongside Jibreel’s for fear of forgetting, he was told to stay silent and let Jibreel finish first, with God Himself taking responsibility for both its collection and his ability to recite it back (75:16–19).

3

Conveying it was made a condition of the mission itself

The Prophet was told directly that failing to pass on what had been revealed would mean failing his very task as a messenger (5:67).

4

Deliberate fabrication was ruled out entirely

The Qur’an states the Prophet does not speak from his own desire (53:3–4) and that changing the message on his own initiative was never something he could do (10:15), with a severe warning attached to the very idea of inventing a false revelation and attributing it to God (69:44–46).

How much, how often, and for how long

The strongest scholarly opinion holds that revelation continued for 23 years in total (a minority of scholars argue for 20 or 25, the disagreement tracing back to uncertainty over the Prophet’s exact age at death — but all agree he was forty at the start of his mission and spent ten years in Madinah). The amount delivered at any one time varied by situation: sometimes ten verses at once, sometimes five, and on at least one occasion a fragment of a single verse — there was no fixed rhythm, only what the moment required.

□ Where this comes from

The account of the three stages of revelation, the objection recorded in 25:32, the comparison with Musa’s Tawrah, and the safeguards against corruption are drawn directly from Chapter 4 (“Gradual Revelation”) of Abu Ammaar Yasir Qadhi’s An Introduction to the Sciences of the Qur’aan (Al-Hidaayah, 1999), including his citation of the classical scholar Abu Shama (d. 665 AH) on the wisdom of the intermediate stage. The specific list of reasons behind gradual revelation (Section II of that chapter, “The Wisdom Behind the Gradual Revelation”) was not available in the source copy used for this project — it has been built instead from standard, cross-checked ’Uloom al-Qur’aan scholarship in the same tradition Qadhi draws on (notably al-Suyuti’s al-Itqan fi ’Uloom al-Qur’aan), and from the widely documented case study of the staged prohibition of alcohol (16:67, 2:219, 4:43, 5:90–91). Qur’an citations are given in standard English translation.

□ Common mix-up to avoid — “how” revelation came vs. “why” it came gradually

Two different past-paper questions get confused with each other. “Different ways the Prophet received revelation” (J21 s11, J23 s12, N24 w11) is about the forms wahy took — for example the angel appearing as a man, or as the sound of a ringing bell — and belongs to a different lesson entirely. This lesson answers a different question: not how revelation arrived, but why it was spread across 23 years instead of delivered complete. If an exam question asks about “different ways” revelation came, don’t reach for this lesson’s material — reach for it only when the question is about timing, pacing, or gradualness.

(a) Describe the Prophet’s experience of receiving the first revelation. 10 marks

AO1 · part (a)

Part (a) is answered fully in the First Revelation lesson (Cave of Hira, Jibreel, “Iqra” x3, Surah al-’Alaq 96:1–5, Khadija, Waraqah) — this lesson’s material is built for part (b).

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) Significance of the Qur’an being revealed over 23 years.” Model answer approach: select at least three distinct reasons from this lesson and develop each with a short explanation — (i) it strengthened the Prophet’s heart at difficult moments, as the Qur’an itself states in 25:32; (ii) gradual delivery matched how an unlettered, largely oral community could actually memorise and practise the message; (iii) it allowed law — such as the prohibition of alcohol — to be phased in across several revelations rather than imposed as a sudden shock; (iv) it let guidance respond directly to real events as they happened, giving it immediate relevance; (v) the Qur’an’s consistency across 23 years of changing, difficult circumstances stands as evidence of its divine origin.

Examiner note: A common weak answer simply restates “so people could learn it slowly” without developing distinct points. Full marks come from naming and briefly explaining three to four genuinely separate reasons, not one reason said three ways.

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · PAPER 1, Q2 · OCT/NOV 2024 (N24 W11)

(a) Different ways revelation came to the Prophet. 10 marks

AO1 · part (a)

Part (a) again concerns the forms of wahy, not this lesson’s focus.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) The Qur’an has remained unchanged since revelation — how does this benefit Muslims?” This part draws directly on this lesson’s “How the process was protected from corruption” section: the trustworthiness of Jibreel as the messenger (26:193), the Prophet’s guaranteed memory of what he received (75:16–19), his duty to convey the message accurately as a condition of his mission (5:67), and the ruling-out of deliberate fabrication (53:3–4, 69:44–46). Benefits to Muslims today: they can be confident the Qur’an they read is the same message revealed 1,400 years ago; it gives a single, unifying reference point for belief and law across the whole Muslim world; and it removes any excuse to doubt a ruling on the grounds that “the text might have changed.”

Examiner note: Candidates often answer this with vague reassurance (“Muslims trust it is authentic”) without citing the actual mechanism. Naming at least two of the four specific safeguards is what separates a full-mark answer from a general one.
