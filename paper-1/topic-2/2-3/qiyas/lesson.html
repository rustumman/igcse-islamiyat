Qiyas — Analogy

2.3 The Qur’an in Legal Thinking · the fourth and final source, and how the whole system fits together

Qiyas works through four fixed components, and naming all four by their Arabic terms is exactly the kind of precision that separates a Grade A* answer from a Grade B one.

The four components of qiyas

  • Asl — the original case, already ruled on directly by the Qur’an or Sunnah.
  • Far’ — the new case, not directly addressed by any text.
  • ’Illah — the underlying reason shared by both cases.
  • Hukm — the ruling, transferred from the original case to the new one because the ’illah is shared.

Worked example: modern intoxicants

  • Asl: the Qur’an explicitly forbids khamr (wine, 5:90).
  • ’Illah: khamr causes intoxication, clouding judgement and harming the body and community.
  • Far’: a modern recreational drug never mentioned in the Qur’an or Sunnah.
  • Hukm: forbidden by qiyas, since it shares wine's defining ’illah — intoxication.

Because qiyas depends on human reasoning to identify the ’illah correctly, scholars historically disagreed — sometimes sharply — over how far it could be pushed, and some legal schools rely on it more heavily than others. This is exactly the tension behind a recurring exam prompt: “Why might some Muslims be reluctant to use qiyas today?” A strong answer notes that because qiyas is a human, reasoned extension rather than a directly revealed text, an incorrectly identified ’illah risks producing a ruling God never actually intended — which is precisely why it sits fourth, used only once the first three sources have been exhausted.

Why the four work as one system

The recurring higher-mark question in this unit — “How do the four sources of Islamic law work together in law-making?” — is really asking you to describe a hierarchy, not a list. Picture it as a single decision process a scholar works through in order: does the Qur’an address this directly? If not, does an authentic hadith? If not, is there scholarly consensus? Only if all three are silent does qiyas apply — and even then, strictly by analogy to a principle the Qur’an or Sunnah already established. No source at any stage may contradict the one above it. That constraint is what keeps Islamic law, across fourteen centuries and dozens of legal schools, still recognisably rooted in one Qur’an.

PAST PAPER · 0493/21 / 0493/22 · PAPER 2, Q2

(a) Explain how the four sources of Islamic Law are used together in law-making. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. The Qur’an is the primary and highest source, direct in both wording and meaning from God; nothing may contradict it.
  2. The Sunnah explains and applies Qur’anic principle in practical detail — give a named example (salah or zakat).
  3. Ijma’ is scholarly consensus used only where the Qur’an and Sunnah are silent — give a named example (the compilation of the Qur’an, or a modern fiqh-council ruling).
  4. Qiyas extends an existing ruling to a new case sharing the same ’illah, used last and most cautiously — give a named example (khamr and modern intoxicants) and name its four parts (asl, far’, ’illah, hukm).
  5. State the governing principle explicitly: each source operates only where the ones above it are silent, and none may override the Qur’an — this is the point examiners most reward, since it shows understanding of the system, not just a list of four definitions.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

Typical prompt: “Is ijma’ and qiyas more important today than in the past?” A balanced answer argues both sides briefly, then commits to a reasoned position: modern life raises questions (medical ethics, digital finance, environmental law) the seventh-century Qur’an and Sunnah never addressed directly, which arguably makes ijma’ and qiyas more essential now — while a counter-view notes the core beliefs and worship obligations they never touch remain exactly as important as they always were.

Examiner pattern: the single most common lost mark in this question is treating the four sources as an unordered list. Every top-band answer states the hierarchy explicitly and explains why the order exists — not just what each source is.
