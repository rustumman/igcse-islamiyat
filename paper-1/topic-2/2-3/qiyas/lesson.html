Qiyas — Analogical Reasoning

2.3 The Qur’an in Legal Thinking · the fourth and final source, used only once the first three have all stayed silent

Qiyas is the source a jurist reaches for only after the Qur’an, the Hadith and scholarly consensus have all had their say and stayed silent — which is exactly why it is the source responsible for giving brand-new inventions, from nail polish to modern drugs, their first Islamic ruling.

□ Predict before you read

The Qur’an names khamr — wine made from grapes — as forbidden, but it never mentions modern drugs, vaping, or synthetic intoxicants by name. Before you read on, write your own answer: if a jurist wanted to argue that a brand-new substance is forbidden too, using the method this whole unit has been building toward, what is the one property they would need to prove the new substance shares with khamr for that comparison to actually work?

What Qiyas is, and why it comes last

Qiyas means deriving a ruling for a new situation by comparing it to an existing case that already has a clear ruling, on the basis that the two cases share the same underlying reason. It is the fourth and last source of Islamic law — used only when a matter cannot be resolved by the Qur’an, Hadith, or ijma’. Like ijma’, qiyas is a secondary, derived source: it creates no new revealed text and can never contradict anything the Qur’an, Hadith, or an established ijma’ has already settled. It is, quite literally, the source of last resort.

Who may perform Qiyas — and the Prophet’s own permission for it

As with ijma’, only qualified mujtahids — leading jurists with deep training in the Qur’an, Hadith and legal method — are permitted to perform qiyas. Its legitimacy as a method goes back to an exchange the Prophet himself approved of. When he sent Mu’adh ibn Jabal to Yemen as a judge, he asked Mu’adh how he would decide cases:

“I will judge according to the Book of Allah... then according to the Sunnah of the Messenger of Allah... then I will use my own reasoning (ijtihad), and I will spare no effort.” The Prophet was pleased and said, “Praise be to Allah, who has guided the messenger of the Messenger of Allah to that which pleases the Messenger of Allah.” Mu’adh ibn Jabal, recorded in Sunan Abu Dawud and Sunan al-Tirmidhi

This exchange is the classical foundation for treating careful, qualified personal reasoning — of which qiyas is the most disciplined and structured form — as a legitimate part of Islamic law, provided it comes only after the Qur’an and Sunnah have genuinely been consulted first.

The method of Qiyas — four working parts

Every valid qiyas is built from four elements, each with its own Arabic term.

□ The four working parts of every qiyas

1

Asl (the original case)

An existing ruling, clearly established in the Qur’an or Hadith.

2

’Illah (the effective cause)

The specific underlying reason behind that existing ruling.

3

Far’ (the new case)

The new situation, with no ruling of its own in the Qur’an, Hadith or ijma’.

4

Hukm (the extended ruling)

The original ruling, transferred to the new case, because the far’ shares the same ’illah as the asl.

Worked example: khamr and modern drugs

The Qur’an prohibits khamr — “intoxicants and gambling... are an abomination of Satan’s handiwork” (Qur’an 5:90–91) — and this becomes the asl. Scholars identify the ’illah behind this prohibition as intoxication: the way khamr impairs the mind and judgement. Modern drugs and synthetic intoxicants, unknown in the Prophet’s time, form the far’ — they were never named in the Qur’an or Hadith, so no direct ruling exists for them. But because they produce the same intoxicating effect as khamr, jurists extend khamr’s hukm — prohibition — to cover them too. Nothing new has been revealed; an existing, clearly established ruling has simply been carried across to a new case that shares its real underlying cause.

Worked example: wudu and nail polish

A second, very practical example shows the same method applied to worship rather than to criminal law. The Qur’an commands washing the hands and arms as part of wudu (5:6), and a hadith narrated by Abu Dharr states:

“If you find water, then let it touch your skin, for that is good.” Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), narrated by Abu Dharr, Sunan Abu Dawud 332

Together these form the asl: water must physically reach the skin for wudu to be valid. The ’illah jurists identify is straightforward: anything forming an impermeable barrier prevents that contact and breaks the requirement. Long before modern cosmetics existed, classical jurists had already applied this reasoning to barriers such as dried dough, wax and thick henna on the fingers. Modern nail polish is the same kind of case (far’) — an impermeable coating unknown to early jurists in this specific form — so the same hukm applies: it must be removed (or water must otherwise reach the nail) for wudu to be valid.

Why Qiyas cannot simply be “someone’s opinion”

It’s worth being precise about what qiyas is not. It is not a jurist guessing, and it is not simply “what seems reasonable.” A valid qiyas requires a genuinely identifiable, shared ’illah between the new case and an existing textual ruling — not a superficial resemblance. This is also why qiyas is restricted to qualified mujtahids: identifying the real ’illah behind a Qur’anic or Hadith ruling, rather than a plausible-sounding but mistaken one, takes the same depth of training that ijma’ requires.

Ijma’ and Qiyas together: how this unit’s four sources fit

With this lesson, all four sources of Islamic law introduced at the start of this unit are now in place, in strict order of authority: the Qur’an (independent, first), the Sunnah/Hadith (explains and supplements the Qur’an), ijma’ (the collective, agreed judgement of qualified scholars where the first two are silent), and qiyas (individual or collective analogical reasoning where even ijma’ offers nothing). No later source may ever override an earlier one — the whole system depends on that order being respected.

Why this matters for the exam

The strongest answers on qiyas always do two things weaker answers skip: they name all four working parts (asl, ’illah, far’, hukm) rather than describing analogy vaguely, and they use a fully worked example — stating exactly what the asl is, what the shared ’illah is, and what the extended hukm is — rather than simply asserting “qiyas is used for new problems.”

Ijma’ — the third source

  • The agreed, collective judgement of qualified scholars on one question.
  • Used once the Qur’an and Hadith are both silent.
  • Reached by consensus — no matching prior case is needed, only agreement.

Qiyas — the fourth source

  • Reasoning by qualified jurists comparing a new case to an existing, already-decided one.
  • Used once the Qur’an, Hadith and ijma’ are all silent.
  • Reached by identifying a shared ’illah with a case the Qur’an or Hadith has already ruled on.

□ Where this comes from

The hadith of Mu’adh ibn Jabal is recorded in Sunan Abu Dawud and Sunan al-Tirmidhi and is the standard classical evidence cited for the legitimacy of qiyas and ijtihad. The khamr prohibition is Qur’an 5:90–91; the wudu principle draws on Qur’an 5:6 and the hadith of Abu Dharr in Sunan Abu Dawud (Hadith 332), both cross-checked via sunnah.com. The four-part asl/’illah/far’/hukm framework reflects standard classical Usul al-Fiqh, consistent across explanatory sources such as the “Usul al-Fiqh Made Easy” series (arriqaaq.com). The nail-polish application of the water-must-reach-the-skin principle, including the classical jurist discussion of dough, wax and henna as comparable barriers, is discussed in contemporary fatwas including those of Shaykh Ibn Baz’s fatwa site (binbaz.org.sa) and Islam Question & Answer.

□ Common mix-up to avoid

The line often quoted as “’A’ishah said wudu is not valid if flour or dough dries on the nails” is not an authenticated hadith — a Saudi fatwa council checking this exact claim (binbaz.org.sa) confirms it is not transmitted as a saying of the Prophet or ’A’ishah at all, but a later juristic principle (qaida fiqhiyyah): “anything that prevents water from reaching [the skin] must be removed.” That principle is itself derived from the genuine textual basis — Qur’an 5:6 and the hadith of Abu Dharr — and it is that principle, not a supposed statement from ’A’ishah, that jurists extended by qiyas to dough, henna, wax, and eventually nail polish. This is worth remembering precisely, because misquoting a juristic conclusion as if it were itself a hadith is exactly the kind of error this whole unit has been teaching you to avoid — Hadith is the Prophet’s own words and actions; qiyas is human reasoning built on top of them.

PAST PAPER · 0493/22 · PAPER 2, Q2 · MAY/JUNE 2024 (J24 S22)

(a) Describe analogy (qiyas) and how it is used in Islamic law, with an example. 10 marks · (b) Why might some Muslims be less likely to use qiyas today? 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Qiyas is the fourth and last source of Islamic law, used only when the Qur’an, Hadith and ijma’ are all silent on a matter.
  2. It works by comparing a new case (far’) to an existing case that already has a clear ruling (asl) in the Qur’an or Hadith.
  3. The comparison is only valid where the two cases share the same underlying effective cause (’illah).
  4. Once a shared ’illah is identified, the existing ruling (hukm) is extended from the asl to the far’.
  5. Worked example: the Qur’an prohibits khamr (asl) because it intoxicates and impairs the mind (’illah); modern drugs (far’) produce the same intoxicating effect, so scholars extend the same prohibition (hukm) to them by qiyas.
  6. Only qualified jurists (mujtahids) may perform qiyas, and its conclusions can never contradict the Qur’an, Hadith or an established ijma’.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

Some may feel less need for fresh qiyas because centuries of earlier, well-researched jurist rulings already cover most major categories of new problem by extension; others may be cautious because correctly identifying a genuine shared ’illah requires deep specialist training that few individuals now have, making scholars more careful — and slower — about issuing new qiyas-based rulings than in the earlier classical period when a small number of widely trusted mujtahids were relied upon to do so.

Examiner note: Candidates frequently describe qiyas as “using your own judgement” without naming the four working parts. Full marks require explicitly identifying the asl, ’illah, far’ and hukm in a specific worked example — a general description of “comparing new things to old things” is not enough on its own.
PAST PAPER · 0493/21 · PAPER 2, Q2 · MAY/JUNE 2025 (J25 S21)

(a) Describe the rules of analogy (qiyas) in law-making, using two modern examples. 10 marks · (b) Why are some Muslims reluctant to use qiyas today? 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Qiyas is Islamic law’s fourth source, used only once the Qur’an, Hadith and ijma’ offer no ruling on a matter.
  2. Its method has four parts: the asl (an existing textual ruling), the ’illah (the shared underlying cause), the far’ (the new, unaddressed case), and the hukm (the ruling extended from asl to far’).
  3. Qiyas can only be performed by qualified jurists (mujtahids), and it must never contradict the Qur’an, Hadith, or an established ijma’.
  4. First modern example: the Qur’an’s prohibition of khamr (asl), caused by intoxication (’illah), is extended to modern drugs (far’), which are ruled prohibited (hukm) on the same basis.
  5. Second modern example: the requirement that water reach the skin during wudu (asl, from Qur’an 5:6 and the hadith of Abu Dharr), with any barrier to that contact identified as the ’illah, is extended to modern nail polish (far’), which classical and modern jurists alike rule must be removed for valid wudu (hukm) — the same reasoning earlier jurists had already applied to dough, wax and henna.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

Reluctance can come from a concern that an incorrectly identified ’illah leads to a mistaken ruling, unlike a ruling taken directly from a clear Qur’anic or Hadith text; different schools of law have historically differed in how freely they apply qiyas (some more expansive, some more cautious), which can make Muslims wary of rulings reached this way if they are unsure which approach was used; and in a more connected modern world, many prefer to wait for a collective ijma’-style ruling from an established fiqh council rather than rely on one jurist’s individual analogical reasoning.

Examiner note: This question specifically asks for two modern examples — an answer describing only qiyas in the abstract, or giving only one worked example instead of two, will not reach full AO1 marks even with an accurate general definition.
