Ijma’ — The Consensus of the Scholars

2.3 The Qur’an in Legal Thinking · the third source of Islamic law, in practice

When doctors first offered married couples IVF, no verse of the Qur’an and no recorded hadith had anticipated it — and yet Muslim scholars still reached a workable answer, using exactly the mechanism this lesson explains.

□ Predict before you read

Only a handful of people ever heard the angel Jibreel say “Iqra,” and no new revelation has come since 632 CE. Before you read on, write down your own answer: centuries later, how could an entire body of Muslim scholars agree on an answer to a question — like whether a brand-new medical procedure is permissible — that neither the Qur’an nor any hadith ever mentions? What would need to be true of their agreement for Muslims to trust it as carrying real legal weight?

What Ijma’ is, and why Islamic law needs it

Ijma’ is the unanimous agreement of qualified Muslim jurists (mujtahids) of a particular time on a ruling for a matter that the Qur’an and Hadith do not directly address. It is the third source of Islamic law, and — like qiyas, the fourth source covered in the next lesson — it is often described as a secondary or derived source: unlike the Qur’an and Hadith, ijma’ does not introduce any new revealed text. It is a method by which qualified scholars extend and apply what the Qur’an and Hadith have already established, to situations those two primary sources never directly covered.

The hadith that gives Ijma’ its authority

The classical basis for treating ijma’ as a genuine source of law is a hadith of the Prophet:

“My Ummah will never unite upon error.” Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), recorded in Sunan Ibn Majah and Sunan al-Tirmidhi

Muslim scholars have long read this as a guarantee: if the whole qualified scholarly community of a given time reaches one agreed answer, that agreement itself carries protection from being simply wrong. Alongside this hadith, scholars commonly cite Qur’an 3:110 as broader Qur’anic support for trusting the collective religious judgement of the Muslim community:

“You are the best of peoples evolved for mankind, enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong, and believing in Allah.” Qur’an 3:110

Ijma’ already at work: compiling the Qur’an

The clearest historical example of ijma’ in action is one you have already studied: the compilation of the Qur’an (see Unit 2.2). After the Battle of Yamama killed a large number of those who had memorised the Qur’an, Caliph Abu Bakr acted on ’Umar’s proposal and agreed, together with the Companions, to have the revelation gathered into a single written copy. Later, Caliph ’Uthman had a committee produce one standard, agreed text — the ’Uthmanic mushaf — which the wider community of Companions accepted, while variant copies were withdrawn without significant recorded objection. Neither step was commanded word-for-word in the Qur’an itself; both were reached by the collective agreement of the Companions, acting on what they judged necessary to preserve the first source of law intact.

□ Three types of Ijma’

1

Ijma’ of the Sahaba (the Companions)

Consensus reached during the lifetime of the Companions, closest in time to the Prophet himself. It carries the highest weight of all, since those reaching it had witnessed revelation and the Prophet’s own practice directly. The compilation of the Qur’an is the standard example.

2

Ijma’ of the Mujtahids (later qualified jurists)

Consensus reached by recognised, qualified scholars of a later generation, applying the same method to questions that arose after the Companions’ era.

3

Ijma’ Sukuti (tacit or silent consensus)

Consensus that forms when one or more qualified scholars state a ruling, other qualified scholars become aware of it, and none of them raises an objection. Their silence, under these conditions, is treated as agreement.

Ijma’ cannot contradict the Qur’an or Hadith: the second adhan

A vital rule governs all three types: no ijma’ may ever contradict a clear ruling already established by the Qur’an or an authentic Hadith. A well-known example shows this rule working exactly as intended. As Madinah grew, people going about their business in the marketplace could no longer hear the single call to prayer (adhan) that had always been given from the mosque immediately before the Friday sermon — and some were missing the prayer altogether as a result. Rather than let people miss an obligation the Qur’an and Hadith had already firmly established, Caliph ’Uthman ibn ’Affan, with the agreement of the Companions of his time, introduced an additional, earlier adhan — a step recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari. This did not create a new law or override anything already settled; it was a practical solution, reached by ijma’, that protected people’s ability to fulfil an obligation the Qur’an and Hadith had already made binding.

Ijma’ meeting genuinely new problems: IVF

Modern medical technology produces exactly the kind of situation ijma’ exists for: questions entirely unknown to the Qur’an and Hadith. In-vitro fertilisation (IVF) is a leading example. Contemporary scholarly bodies — such as the OIC’s International Islamic Fiqh Academy — have reached rulings permitting IVF for a married couple using their own egg and sperm, while ruling out any use of a third-party donor’s egg, sperm, or womb, since that would conflict with clearly established Qur’anic and Hadith principles concerning lineage and paternity. This shows ijma’ doing precisely what it is meant to do: reaching a considered, collective answer to a new question, without contradicting anything the Qur’an or Hadith had already settled.

Why this matters for the exam

The pattern is the same across every example in this lesson: ijma’ steps in only where the Qur’an and Hadith are silent (compiling the Qur’an into one text, ruling on IVF) or where a practical solution is needed to protect an existing obligation (the second adhan) — and in every case, it works within limits the first two sources already set, never against them. An exam answer that defines ijma’ correctly but gives no properly explained example, or that implies ijma’ can overturn a Qur’anic ruling, will lose marks either way.

What Ijma’ can do

  • Rule on entirely new matters the Qur’an and Hadith never addressed (e.g. IVF).
  • Reach a practical solution that protects an existing obligation rather than changing it (e.g. the second adhan).
  • Draw on the Prophet’s own guarantee that the qualified scholarly community will not unite on error.

What Ijma’ cannot do

  • Contradict a clear ruling already established by the Qur’an or an authentic Hadith.
  • Be reached by unqualified individuals — only recognised mujtahids count towards a valid ijma’.
  • Be assumed casually — a genuine, complete consensus has to be established, not just widespread popular practice.

□ Where this comes from

The hadith “My Ummah will never unite upon error” is recorded (with varied wording) in Sunan Ibn Majah (Hadith 3950, Kitab al-Fitan) and Sunan al-Tirmidhi (Hadith 2167), cross-checked via sunnah.com; individual chains of this hadith have some weaknesses, but scholars — including the analysis at hadithanswers.com — note that its meaning is supported by multiple independent narrations and by hadiths of similar meaning in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, which is why classical jurists still treat it as the foundational evidence for ijma’. Qur’an 3:110 is commonly cited alongside it as Qur’anic support (see, for example, the treatment on olevelislamiat.com). ’Uthman’s introduction of the second adhan for Friday prayer is recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari’s Book of Jumu’ah — a detail worth checking carefully, since it is very commonly misattributed to ’Umar in student answers (see the Common Mix-Up box below). The IVF example reflects rulings issued by bodies such as the International Islamic Fiqh Academy (affiliated with the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation).

□ Common mix-up to avoid

It was ’Uthman ibn ’Affan, not ’Umar, who introduced the second adhan for Friday prayer — the two “U” caliphs are frequently swapped in student answers, so it is worth fixing this one precisely, especially since both caliphs are associated with other organisational reforms that are easy to blur together. A second common mix-up: describing ijma’ as simply “lots of Muslims agreeing on something.” Classical ijma’ specifically requires the unanimous agreement of qualified, recognised jurists (mujtahids) of a given time — not a majority vote among ordinary Muslims, and not mere widespread custom.

PAST PAPER · 0493/22 · PAPER 2, Q2 · MAY/JUNE 2022 (J22 S22)

(a) Describe consensus (ijma’) as the third source of Islamic Law, giving two examples from the time of the Companions. 10 marks · (b) Does qiyas let Islamic law apply at any time or in any case? 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Ijma’ is the third source of Islamic law: the unanimous agreement of qualified scholars (mujtahids) on a ruling for a matter the Qur’an and Hadith do not directly address.
  2. Its authority rests on the Prophet’s own hadith that his Ummah would never unite upon error (Sunan Ibn Majah 3950 / Sunan al-Tirmidhi 2167).
  3. First example: the compilation of the Qur’an — Abu Bakr agreed to ’Umar’s proposal to gather the revelation into one text after the Battle of Yamama; ’Uthman’s committee later produced a single standard mushaf, accepted by the consensus of the Companions.
  4. Second example: ’Uthman’s introduction of a second adhan for the Friday prayer, agreed among the Companions of his time, once Madinah’s growth meant many could no longer hear the original single call.
  5. Both examples show ijma’ filling a genuine gap or solving a genuine practical problem, without contradicting anything the Qur’an or Hadith had already established.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

Supporters argue qiyas allows Islamic law to keep functioning across every era by reasoning from the same underlying cause (’illah) behind an existing ruling to a new case (e.g. extending the khamr prohibition to modern intoxicants); critics note qiyas depends entirely on the soundness of a jurist’s reasoning, so its conclusions can be disputed in a way a clear Qur’anic or Hadith ruling cannot be, and it can never be applied where the Qur’an or Hadith have already spoken directly on a case.

Examiner note: This question specifically asks for two examples “from the time of the Companions” — a definition of ijma’ with no properly named and explained example, or examples drawn from later centuries instead of the Companions’ era, will not score full AO1 marks even if the definition itself is accurate.
PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · PAPER 1, Q2 · OCT/NOV 2021 (N21 W12)

(a) Describe how the Qur’an and Sunna are used in Islamic Law. 10 marks · (b) Give an example of ijma’ or qiyas that is significant for Muslims today. 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. The Qur’an is the first, independent source of Islamic law; the Sunnah is the second, explaining and supplementing it (see the previous lesson’s examples: the method of prayer, the zakat nisab thresholds).
  2. A complete answer notes that where the Qur’an and Sunnah are both silent, the next step in the chain of Islamic law-making is ijma’ — the agreed ruling of qualified scholars — rather than the process simply stopping.
  3. Ijma’s authority is based on the hadith that the Ummah will never unite upon error, and its classic example is the compilation of the Qur’an by consensus of the Companions.
  4. Where ijma’ itself does not settle a new question, qiyas (analogy) is used next — but ijma’ and qiyas can never override a clear ruling the Qur’an or Sunnah has already given.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

Modern fiqh councils, such as the International Islamic Fiqh Academy, have used ijma’ to rule that IVF is permissible for a married couple using their own egg and sperm — a procedure entirely unknown at the time of the Qur’an and Hadith, resolved instead through the collective, qualified scholarly agreement that ijma’ provides, while still ruling out anything (such as third-party donor gametes) that would conflict with existing Qur’anic principles on lineage.

Examiner note: Because this question allows either ijma’ or qiyas, weaker answers hedge by mentioning both vaguely without properly explaining either. Choose one, name a specific modern example, and explain the reasoning behind it — that is what earns the AO2 marks, not simply naming the source.
