The Four Sources of Law

2.3 The Qur’an in Legal Thinking · the Qur’an, Sunnah, Ijma’ and Qiyas, in order of authority

A society needs more than a set of beliefs to function — it needs answers to concrete questions: how much charity is owed, what counts as a valid contract, how a new medical technology should be judged. Islamic law, Shari’ah, answers those questions by drawing on four sources in a fixed order of authority — and the Qur’an's exact relationship to the other three is one of the most consistently examined ideas in this whole syllabus.

Four sources, one hierarchy

Sunni Islamic legal theory (usul al-fiqh) recognises four sources of law, and their order matters as much as their content:

1

The Qur’an — the foundation

The direct, unchanging word of God. It is the only source Muslims believe is divine in both wording and meaning, so nothing from any other source may ever contradict it. Some rulings are given in precise, explicit detail — the shares of inheritance (Surah 4:11–12), the punishment for theft (5:38) — while most guidance is given as broad principle, left for the next source to apply in practice.

2

The Sunnah / Hadith — the explanation

The Prophet's own words, actions and silent approvals. Where the Qur’an commands prayer without describing its mechanics, the Prophet supplied it directly: “Pray as you have seen me praying.” Where the Qur’an commands zakat without stating a rate, the Prophet specified the nisab (minimum threshold) and percentage owed. The Sunnah cannot overturn a clear Qur’anic ruling — its role is to clarify, specify and apply what the Qur’an leaves in principle.

3

Ijma’ — consensus

The unanimous agreement of qualified Muslim scholars on a ruling not explicitly settled by the first two sources. It only carries authority when the Qur’an and Sunnah are silent on the specific question — it cannot invent a ruling that contradicts either.

4

Qiyas — analogical reasoning

Extending an existing ruling to a new situation that shares its underlying reason (’illah), where no direct text or consensus yet exists. It is the most flexible source, and consequently the one used most cautiously — and last.
