The Relationship of the Qur’an with Hadith

2.3 The Qur’an in Legal Thinking · how Islam’s second source of law explains, restricts and fills the gaps the Qur’an leaves open

The Qur’an commands Muslims to pray five times a day but never once states how many times to bow — a gap that is not an oversight, but the exact place where the Qur’an’s second source of law was always meant to step in.

□ Predict before you read

The Qur’an commands prayer, but nowhere describes the number of bowings, the words to say while bowing, or what to do with your hands. Before you read on, write down your own answer: if this detail isn’t in the Qur’an, where do you think fourteen centuries of Muslims found out exactly how to pray — and why might Allah have deliberately left that detail for something other than the Qur’an to supply?

What Hadith and Sunnah actually mean

The Sunnah is the Prophet Muhammad’s (pbuh) example — everything he said, did, and silently approved of in others. Hadith are the individual recorded reports that preserve and transmit that example, each one carefully traced back to him through a chain of narrators. In everyday use — and in this syllabus — the two words are often used almost interchangeably to refer to the Prophet’s words and actions as a source of law, but it’s worth keeping the distinction straight: Sunnah is the example itself; Hadith are the documents that record it.

The second primary source of Islamic law

After the Qur’an, Hadith is recognised as the second primary source of Shari’ah. Unlike the Qur’an, Hadith is not itself the direct, word-for-word speech of Allah — which is why it is a dependent rather than an independent source. Its authority as law rests on the Qur’an’s own instruction to obey the Prophet: “Obey Allah and obey the Messenger” (Qur’an 4:59). Because the Qur’an itself commands obedience to the Prophet, following his recorded example carries the force of law, second only to the Qur’an itself.

The Hadith explains the Qur’an — ’A’ishah’s description of the Prophet’s character

When the Prophet’s wife ’A’ishah was asked to describe his character, she gave an answer that Muslim scholars have long treated as a summary of the whole relationship between Qur’an and Hadith:

“His character was the Qur’an.” ’A’ishah, describing the Prophet’s character, recorded in Sahih Muslim

What she meant was that his entire life — every word, action and habit — was the Qur’an’s teaching put into visible practice. If the Qur’an is the instruction, the Prophet’s life recorded in Hadith is the demonstration. This is why classical scholars describe the Sunnah’s relationship to the Qur’an in four distinct ways: it can confirm what the Qur’an already states, explain a general Qur’anic command in practical detail, restrict a broad Qur’anic wording to a more specific meaning, or independently rule on a matter the Qur’an does not mention at all. The three examples below show the second and fourth of these at work.

When the Qur’an is silent: the grandmother’s inheritance

The Qur’an’s inheritance verses (4:11–12) give exact, numerical shares to parents, children, spouses and certain siblings — but they say nothing at all about a grandmother. This gap was filled entirely by Hadith. It is reported, in a hadith narrated by al-Mughirah ibn Shu’bah and corroborated by Muhammad ibn Maslamah, that when the Prophet was asked about a grandmother’s share of inheritance, he ruled that she receives one-sixth. This ruling — found in Sunan Abu Dawud and Sunan al-Tirmidhi rather than anywhere in the Qur’an — became a settled part of Islamic inheritance law precisely because Hadith is authorised to rule independently on matters the Qur’an leaves untouched.

When the Qur’an is general: the zakat nisab

The Qur’an repeatedly commands zakat (obligatory almsgiving) but never states the minimum amount of wealth (the nisab) at which it becomes due, nor the exact rate. That detail comes from Hadith:

“There is no zakat due on property less than five awsuq [of grain or dates], there is no zakat due on camels fewer than five, and there is no zakat due on silver less than five awaq [uqiyyah].” Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), narrated by Abu Sa’id al-Khudri, Sahih al-Bukhari

Without this hadith, “pay zakat” would be a command with no practical starting point. The Hadith doesn’t contradict the Qur’an’s command — it makes it workable, by fixing the precise threshold the Qur’an leaves general.

When the Qur’an is silent on method: prayer, hajj and purification

The clearest examples of Hadith supplying method rather than new law come from worship itself. On prayer, the Prophet said:

“Pray as you have seen me praying.” Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), narrated by Malik ibn al-Huwayrith, Sahih al-Bukhari

The Qur’an commands salah; the Hadith supplies every physical and verbal detail of how it is performed. The same pattern holds for hajj — during his Farewell Pilgrimage the Prophet told the assembled Muslims:

“Take from me your rites [of hajj].” Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), Farewell Pilgrimage sermon, recorded in Sahih Muslim

This means that the exact sequence of hajj rituals is learned by following his demonstrated practice, not by reading it in the Qur’an. Rules about ritual impurity work the same way: the Qur’an establishes that cleanliness matters for prayer, but detailed rulings — such as the hadith instructing that a vessel licked by a dog be washed seven times (Sahih Muslim) — come entirely from Hadith.

A hadith that looks ahead to ijma’ and qiyas

One further hadith is worth knowing now, because the next two lessons build directly on it. When the Prophet sent Mu’adh ibn Jabal to Yemen as a judge, he asked Mu’adh how he would judge cases. Mu’adh answered that he would judge by the Qur’an first, then by the Sunnah, and — if he found nothing in either — by his own reasoning (ijtihad). The Prophet approved of this answer (recorded in Sunan Abu Dawud and Sunan al-Tirmidhi). This single exchange is often cited as the origin point for scholarly consensus (ijma’) and analogical reasoning (qiyas) as legitimate sources that operate only after the Qur’an and Hadith have been consulted first — exactly the order this unit is teaching them in.

Why the relationship matters for the exam

Every example in this lesson does the same job in a different sphere: it shows Hadith stepping in exactly where the Qur’an is either silent (the grandmother) or general (zakat, prayer, hajj, purification) — never overriding or contradicting what the Qur’an has already settled. An exam answer that only says “Hadith is important because it comes from the Prophet” is missing the specific mechanism — confirming, explaining, restricting, or independently ruling — that the strongest answers name and illustrate.

□ Four ways Hadith relates to the Qur’an

1

Confirming (ta’kid)

Some hadith simply restate and reinforce a command already given in the Qur’an, adding the Prophet’s own emphasis.

2

Explaining (bayan/tafsir)

Most legal hadith explain a general Qur’anic command in practical detail, as with the exact method of prayer or hajj.

3

Restricting (taqyid)

Some hadith narrow a broad Qur’anic wording to a more specific meaning, as with the precise nisab thresholds fixing when zakat becomes due.

4

Ruling independently (istiqlal)

Where the Qur’an is silent altogether, Hadith can establish a new ruling on its own authority, as with the grandmother’s one-sixth inheritance share.

What the Qur’an does

  • Direct, word-for-word revelation from Allah (wahy) — the independent source.
  • Often states core principles, and sometimes exact rulings (e.g. fixed inheritance shares for children, parents and spouse).
  • Rarely gives step-by-step method for acts of worship.
  • Cannot be overridden or contradicted by any other source.

What the Hadith adds

  • The Prophet’s recorded words, actions and approvals — a dependent source, authorised by the Qur’an itself (4:59).
  • Explains and demonstrates exactly how to carry out Qur’anic commands (salah, hajj, purification).
  • Rules independently on matters the Qur’an does not mention at all (e.g. the grandmother’s inheritance share).
  • Can never contradict a clear Qur’anic ruling — only confirm, explain, restrict or supplement it.

□ Where this comes from

’A’ishah’s description of the Prophet’s character is recorded in Sahih Muslim; the zakat nisab hadith is narrated by Abu Sa’id al-Khudri in Sahih al-Bukhari’s Book of Zakat; “Pray as you have seen me praying” is narrated by Malik ibn al-Huwayrith in Sahih al-Bukhari’s Book of Adhan; the Farewell Pilgrimage hajj instruction and the ruling on washing a vessel licked by a dog are both recorded in Sahih Muslim; the grandmother’s one-sixth inheritance ruling is recorded in Sunan Abu Dawud and Sunan al-Tirmidhi (Book of Fara’id/Inheritance), narrated via al-Mughirah ibn Shu’bah and Muhammad ibn Maslamah; the hadith of Mu’adh ibn Jabal being sent to Yemen is recorded in Sunan Abu Dawud and Sunan al-Tirmidhi. All hadith wording has been cross-checked via sunnah.com. The four-fold classification of Hadith’s relationship to the Qur’an (confirming, explaining, restricting, ruling independently) reflects standard classical Usul al-Fiqh, as set out in early legal-theory works such as Imam al-Shafi’i’s Al-Risala.

□ Common mix-up to avoid

Students often write “Hadith” and “Sunnah” as though they mean exactly the same thing in every context — for exam purposes that’s usually fine, but strictly, Sunnah is the Prophet’s example itself, while Hadith are the individual recorded reports that document it. A more serious mix-up is treating Hadith as a source that can override or contradict the Qur’an: it cannot. Hadith only confirms, explains, restricts, or fills a genuine silence in the Qur’an — never overturns a clear Qur’anic ruling. Also take care with terminology: “isnad” is the chain of narrators behind a hadith, and “matn” is its actual text/content — these are covered fully in the History and Importance of the Hadiths unit, but the terms often appear in “relationship with Qur’an” exam questions too.

PAST PAPER · 0493/21 · PAPER 2, Q2 · OCT/NOV 2022 (N22 W21)

(a) Describe how Hadiths and the Qur’an are used together in law-making. 10 marks · (b) Why is ijma’ important in the Muslim world today? 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Hadith — the recorded words, actions and approvals of the Prophet (pbuh) — is the second primary source of Islamic law, after the Qur’an.
  2. Hadith explains and demonstrates Qur’anic commands in practice: “Pray as you have seen me praying” supplies the exact method of salah that the Qur’an commands but never details.
  3. Hadith restricts or specifies general Qur’anic commands: the zakat nisab hadith (no zakat due on fewer than five camels, five awsuq, or five awaq of silver) fixes the precise threshold the Qur’an leaves general.
  4. Hadith rules independently where the Qur’an is silent altogether: the grandmother’s one-sixth inheritance share appears in Hadith, not in the Qur’an’s inheritance verses.
  5. Hadith’s authority as law comes from the Qur’an’s own command to obey the Prophet (4:59) — so the two sources are connected, not competing.
  6. Hadith can never contradict a clear Qur’anic ruling; it can only confirm, explain, restrict, or supplement it.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

Ijma’ lets qualified scholars reach an agreed, collective ruling on questions that arise after the Prophet’s death and are not directly settled by the Qur’an or Hadith — for example, agreeing on a single standard mushaf of the Qur’an in the time of ’Uthman, or, in the present day, reaching collective rulings on new medical or financial questions. It keeps Islamic law a shared, scholarly process rather than one person’s private opinion, which matters even more today given how many genuinely new situations (unmentioned by either Qur’an or Hadith) modern Muslims face.

Examiner note: Candidates often describe Hadith and Qur’an as two separate, unconnected lists of rules. Full marks come from explicitly naming how they interact — Hadith explaining, restricting, or filling a silence in the Qur’an — with a specific named example for each function, rather than a general statement that “Hadith supports the Qur’an.”
PAST PAPER · 0493/22 · PAPER 2, Q2 · OCT/NOV 2025 (N25 W22)

(a) Describe how the four sources of Islamic law are used in law-making. 10 marks · (b) Why did the Prophet allow personal reasoning (qiyas)? 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. The Qur’an is the first, independent source — the literal word of Allah and the standard all other sources must agree with.
  2. The Sunnah/Hadith is the second source: it explains general Qur’anic commands in practical detail (e.g. the method of prayer and hajj) and rules independently on matters the Qur’an does not mention (e.g. the grandmother’s inheritance share).
  3. Ijma’ is the third source — the consensus of qualified scholars, used once the Qur’an and Hadith have both been consulted and neither settles the question.
  4. Qiyas is the fourth source — reasoning by analogy from an existing Qur’anic or Hadith ruling to a new case sharing the same underlying cause.
  5. A worked example across all four: the Qur’an commands zakat in general terms; Hadith fixes the exact nisab thresholds; later scholarly ijma’ and qiyas extend those thresholds to new forms of wealth (such as paper currency or business stock) that did not exist in the Prophet’s own time.
  6. The order is fixed: a later source can never override an earlier one, and qiyas or ijma’ can only be used once the Qur’an and Hadith are truly silent.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

When the Prophet sent Mu’adh ibn Jabal to Yemen as a judge, Mu’adh explained he would judge first by the Qur’an, then by the Sunnah, and only then by his own reasoning if he found nothing in either — and the Prophet approved of this. This shows that personal reasoning was sanctioned from the earliest period of Islam precisely because new situations would always keep arising that a fixed text, however comprehensive, could not individually name in advance; allowing qiyas gave Islamic law the flexibility to keep functioning long after revelation ended.

Examiner note: This question is often answered as if the four sources were an unordered list of options a jurist can pick between. Keep the sequence explicit — Qur’an first, Hadith second, ijma’ third, qiyas fourth — and use one connected worked example (like the zakat chain above) rather than four disconnected definitions.
PAST PAPER · 0493/22 · PAPER 2, Q2 · MAY/JUNE 2023 (J23 S22)

(a) Describe the terms isnad, matn, musnad and musannaf, and their relationship to each other. 10 marks · (b) How do compilations of genuine Hadiths benefit Muslims today? 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Isnad is the chain of narrators through which a hadith is traced back to the Prophet — each name in the chain was checked for honesty and reliability of memory.
  2. Matn is the actual wording/content of the hadith being transmitted — separately checked for consistency with the Qur’an and with other established, authentic hadith.
  3. A hadith is only accepted as reliable when both its isnad and its matn pass scrutiny — this scholarly checking process is what makes a hadith like “Pray as you have seen me praying” or the grandmother’s inheritance ruling trustworthy rather than hearsay.
  4. Musnad refers to a hadith collection arranged by the narrator who first reported each hadith (e.g. grouped by Companion), rather than by subject.
  5. Musannaf refers to a hadith collection arranged by subject or topic (e.g. a chapter on zakat, a chapter on inheritance), making it easier to find rulings on a specific legal question.
  6. Both arrangements draw on the same underlying body of individually verified hadith — they organise the material differently, but the isnad/matn checking process behind each hadith is the same either way.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

Because each hadith’s isnad and matn were checked and recorded, a Muslim today can still look up the exact chain and wording behind a ruling such as the zakat nisab or the grandmother’s inheritance share in a musannaf collection like Sahih al-Bukhari’s Book of Zakat or Sunan Abu Dawud’s Book of Inheritance, rather than relying on rumour or later invention. This protects the second source of Islamic law from being corrupted over time, the same way the Qur’an’s own compilation protected the first.

Examiner note: Candidates frequently confuse musnad and musannaf, or define isnad and matn but never explain how they combine to establish authenticity. State the definitions and then explicitly connect them to reliability — that connection is what the mark scheme is looking for, not just two isolated definitions.
