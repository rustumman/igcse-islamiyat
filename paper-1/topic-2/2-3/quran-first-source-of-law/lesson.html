The Qur’an as the First Source of Law

2.3 The Qur’an in Legal Thinking · the independent, primary source every other source of law defers to

A book revealed to settle questions of faith and the unseen also tells a court exactly how many shares a daughter inherits and how many lashes a false accuser receives — the Qur’an’s claim to be a complete guide is tested most directly in its laws, not its theology.

□ Predict before you read

The Qur’an is usually described first as a book of guidance about God, the unseen, and the Day of Judgement. Before you read on, write down your own answer: can a book like that also function as a working source of law — covering things as concrete as theft, marriage, trade and war — without becoming a dry legal code? What would it need to do differently from an ordinary law book to manage both jobs at once?

Shari’ah and its four sources — where the Qur’an sits

Islamic law is known as Shari’ah, and Muslim jurists recognise four sources from which its rulings are drawn: the Qur’an, the Sunnah (the Prophet’s words, actions and approvals), ijma’ (the consensus of qualified scholars) and qiyas (analogical reasoning). The next three lessons in this unit deal with the Sunnah, ijma’ and qiyas in turn — how each works, what authority it carries, and how it relates back to the Qur’an. This lesson deals with the source that sits above all three: the Qur’an itself, and why it is always the first place a Muslim jurist looks.

Independent and primary

Among the four sources, only the Qur’an is independent. It does not draw its legal authority from any other text or human ruling — its authority is self-standing, because Muslims hold it to be the literal, word-for-word speech of Allah. The other three sources are, by contrast, all dependent on the Qur’an in some way: the Sunnah’s authority as a source of law rests partly on the Qur’an’s own instruction to obey the Prophet (Qur’an 4:59, “O you who believe, obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those charged with authority among you”); ijma’ and qiyas are only used to reach a ruling when the Qur’an and Sunnah are silent on a question, and neither may ever contradict what the Qur’an has already established. This is what it means to call the Qur’an the primary source: whenever a legal question arises, it is consulted first, and nothing in the other three sources may override a clear Qur’anic ruling.

Divine, eternal and complete

Because Muslims believe the Qur’an to be Allah’s own word, revealed through Jibreel over twenty-three years and preserved unchanged since, obeying its legal rulings is understood as obeying Allah directly — a matter of worship (ibadah), not simply of civic compliance. The Qur’an itself makes a striking claim about its own scope, in words that Muslims take as a description of its completeness as a guide:

“And We have sent down to thee the Book explaining all things, a guide, a mercy, and glad tidings to Muslims.” Qur’an 16:89

This does not mean the Qur’an lists a specific rule for every possible situation — it does not. It means the Qur’an lays down the essential principles and the clearest rulings needed to guide every sphere of life, leaving the Sunnah, ijma’ and qiyas to work out detail and new cases within the framework it sets.

Allah’s sovereignty as the foundation of law

Underlying all of this is a principle Muslim scholars call al-hakimiyyah — the sovereignty of Allah in law-making. The Qur’an states plainly: “The command is for none but Allah” (Qur’an 12:40). For Muslims, this means human beings do not invent moral and legal rules from nothing; they discover and apply the rules Allah has already laid down, or reason carefully within the framework Allah has provided (which is exactly the role ijma’ and qiyas play, as later lessons will show). This is also why the Qur’an addresses believers directly as those who owe obedience to God’s law, rather than presenting its rulings as optional advice.

A guide covering belief, worship and everyday dealing alike

The Qur’an opens by describing itself as guidance “without doubt” for anyone conscious of Allah (Qur’an 2:2), and that guidance is not confined to ritual. Islamic scholars traditionally divide human conduct into two broad categories, and the Qur’an legislates for both: ’ibadat (acts of worship — prayer, fasting, zakat, hajj) and mu’amalat (transactions and dealings between people — marriage, trade, crime, government, war). A student who thinks of the Qur’an only as a book about belief and worship is missing half of what makes it a source of law rather than only a source of faith.

Family law: marriage, divorce, women, slaves and orphans

The Qur’an sets out detailed rules for marriage (including the marriage contract and the mahr, or dowry) and for divorce, and repeatedly instructs husbands to treat their wives with fairness and kindness: “and live with them in kindness” (Qur’an 4:19). It regulates the treatment of slaves, urging kindness towards them (Qur’an 4:36) and treating the freeing of a slave as a highly praised act (Qur’an 90:13), while also providing a legal path — the mukatabah contract — by which a slave could formally purchase their own freedom (Qur’an 24:33). It gives particular protection to orphans, whose property guardians are sternly warned not to touch except to increase it:

“And come not nigh to the orphan’s property, except to improve it, until he attain the age of full strength.” Qur’an 6:152 (a near-identical warning is repeated at 17:34)

The Qur’an also sets out fixed inheritance shares for family members in precise detail (Qur’an 4:11–12) — one of the clearest examples anywhere in the text of law given as an exact, numerical rule rather than a general principle.

Civil transactions, trade and economic justice

The Qur’an regulates ordinary commercial dealing, condemning dishonesty in trade in the strongest terms:

“Woe to those that deal in fraud — those who, when they have to receive by measure from men, exact full measure, but when they have to give by measure or weight to men, give less than due.” Qur’an 83:1–3

It also addresses the wider distribution of wealth in society, warning against a system in which riches simply circulate among those who already have them: “…so that this [wealth] may not circulate solely among the wealthy among you” (Qur’an 59:7). Between the individual case of a trader shortchanging a customer and the whole-society case of wealth concentrating among the rich, the Qur’an treats economic fairness as a single connected concern, regulated at both scales.

Penal law: theft and retaliation

The Qur’an lays down specific criminal penalties (part of what Islamic law calls the hudud, fixed punishments) as well as the general principle of proportionate retaliation, qisas:

“…We ordained therein for them: life for life, eye for eye, nose for nose, ear for ear, tooth for tooth, and wounds equal for equal.” Qur’an 5:45

Theft carries an explicit prescribed penalty — “As to the thief, male or female, cut off his or her hand: a punishment by way of example from Allah” (Qur’an 5:38) — and false accusation of unchastity against a chaste person (qadhf) is punished with eighty lashes and permanent loss of the accuser’s testimony in court unless they can produce four witnesses (Qur’an 24:4). The prohibition of intoxicants — “intoxicants and gambling… are an abomination of Satan’s handiwork” (Qur’an 5:90–91) — belongs in this same penal and moral sphere, and, as a later lesson on qiyas will show, became one of the classic examples of a Qur’anic ruling later extended by analogy to cases the text does not name directly.

War, booty and international relations

The Qur’an’s legal reach extends even to the conduct of war between states and communities. It sets out how the spoils of war (ghanimah) are to be shared out — one-fifth reserved for Allah, the Messenger, relatives, orphans, the needy and the traveller, with the remainder divided among those who fought: “And know that out of all the booty that you may acquire in war, a fifth share is assigned to Allah, and to the Messenger, and to near relatives, orphans, the needy, and the wayfarer” (Qur’an 8:41). It also legislates for the fair treatment of prisoners of war, instructing that once an enemy is subdued, captives are to be dealt with either through generosity or through ransom rather than mistreatment (Qur’an 47:4), and it praises believers who feed the captive out of love for Allah alongside the orphan and the needy (Qur’an 76:8).

Why the scope matters for the exam

Taken together, this sweep — belief, worship, personal morality, marriage and divorce, the rights of women, slaves and orphans, trade and wealth distribution, criminal punishment, and the rules of war and international dealing — is exactly what the syllabus means when it asks about “the Qur’an as a primary source of law.” An answer that only mentions ritual worship, or only mentions punishments, is describing a fraction of what the Qur’an actually legislates for. The strongest answers name specific spheres and back each with the sort of precise reference used above.

The spheres of life the Qur’an legislates for

1

Belief (’aqidah)

Establishes the oneness of Allah (tawhid), the unseen (angels, the Last Day) and the framework of faith that everything else rests on.

2

Worship (’ibadat)

Commands the core acts of worship — prayer, fasting, zakat, hajj — in outline, leaving the Sunnah to supply the exact method.

3

Personal morality

Sets standards for honesty, modesty, patience and kindness that run through the text as a whole, not confined to any single ruling.

4

Family law

Regulates marriage, divorce, inheritance, and the treatment of women, slaves and orphans.

5

Civil and commercial transactions (mu’amalat)

Governs trade, honest measure, contracts and the distribution of wealth in society.

6

Penal law

Lays down fixed punishments (hudud) for offences such as theft and false accusation, and the principle of proportionate retaliation (qisas).

7

International relations and the conduct of war

Rules on the distribution of the spoils of war and the treatment of prisoners.

□ Where this comes from

The English wording of the verses quoted above (16:89, 83:1–3, 5:45, 6:152, 8:41, 59:7) follows Abdullah Yusuf Ali’s classic translation, cross-checked against the Saheeh International translation and the Arabic text via quran.com/Quran Corpus. The framework of the four sources of Islamic law (Qur’an, Sunnah, ijma’, qiyas) and the ’ibadat/mu’amalat distinction reflect standard, widely taught Usul al-Fiqh (principles of Islamic jurisprudence), consistent with how the syllabus itself frames “use in legal thinking” alongside the relationship with Hadith, ijma’ and qiyas. This lesson deliberately keeps to Qur’anic material and leaves the mechanics of Sunnah, ijma’ and qiyas to the three lessons that follow, per this unit’s sequence.

□ Common mix-up to avoid

Students often write as if the Qur’an’s “sovereignty of Allah” principle means human beings play no active role in Islamic law at all. That overstates it: Allah’s sovereignty means the ultimate source of legal authority is divine, not that humans do no reasoning — ijma’ (scholarly consensus) and qiyas (analogy) are both human intellectual processes, but they operate strictly within the boundaries the Qur’an and Sunnah set, and can never override a clear Qur’anic ruling. A second common mix-up: candidates sometimes say the Sunnah is “just as independent” as the Qur’an. It isn’t — the Sunnah’s authority as a legal source is itself established by Qur’anic verses commanding obedience to the Prophet (e.g. 4:59), which is precisely why the Qur’an alone is called the independent source.

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · PAPER 1, Q2 · MAY/JUNE 2022 (J22 S11)

(a) Describe how the Qur’an and Sunnah are used, together with ijma’, in Islamic Law. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. The Qur’an is the first and only independent source of Islamic law — the literal word of Allah, and the standard against which every other source is measured.
  2. It gives explicit rulings in some areas (e.g. the fixed inheritance shares of 4:11–12, the penalty for theft in 5:38) and broader principles in others.
  3. The Sunnah — the Prophet’s words, actions and approvals — is the second source; it explains and supplements Qur’anic law, for example filling in exactly how the prayers commanded by the Qur’an are performed.
  4. The Sunnah’s authority as a legal source itself rests on Qur’anic instruction to obey the Prophet (4:59), showing the sources work in a hierarchy rather than as equals.
  5. Where the Qur’an and Sunnah are silent on a new question, the early Muslim community turned to ijma’ — the consensus of qualified scholars or Companions — to reach an agreed ruling.
  6. Ijma’ must not contradict anything already settled by the Qur’an or Sunnah; it fills gaps rather than overriding the first two sources.

Part (b) — “How has the Qur’an, as a primary source of law, helped unify Muslims over time?” (4 marks) — because the Qur’an gives every generation, region and later school of Islamic law the same unchanging textual starting point, jurists across very different times and places have always built their reasoning from one shared Book — meaning core rulings (the same five daily prayers, the same zakat principle, the same central prohibitions) stayed common ground even where scholars later differed on secondary detail reached through ijma’ or qiyas; a single, fixed text acts as an anchor that keeps the wider Ummah connected across centuries and continents.

Examiner note: Candidates often answer part (a) as if the three sources were three separate, equally-ranked lists of rules, rather than showing that ijma’ operates only within limits set by the Qur’an and Sunnah. Explicitly stating the hierarchy — Qur’an first and independent, Sunnah second and dependent on Qur’anic authorisation, ijma’ third and gap-filling — is what separates full-marks answers from partial ones.
PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · PAPER 1, Q2 · OCT/NOV 2021 (N21 W12)

(a) Describe how the Qur’an and Sunnah are used in Islamic Law. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. The Qur’an is the primary and independent source: its authority does not depend on any other text, since Muslims hold it to be Allah’s own word.
  2. It provides direct legislation across belief, worship, family law, civil transactions, penal law and international relations.
  3. Where the Qur’an gives a general command rather than a detailed method — for example, commanding prayer and zakat without specifying every step — the Sunnah supplies the missing detail through the Prophet’s own recorded practice.
  4. The Sunnah cannot contradict a clear Qur’anic ruling; its role is to explain, clarify, or occasionally add a further rule consistent with the Qur’an’s principles.
  5. Together, the two sources form the two most authoritative texts in Islamic law, with the Qur’an always taking precedence where the two might appear to differ.

Part (b) — “Give an example of ijma’ or qiyas that is significant for Muslims today” (4 marks) — the Qur’an explicitly names khamr — wine made from grapes — as prohibited (5:90–91). Because no other intoxicant is named directly, scholars used qiyas to extend the same ruling to modern intoxicants and drugs, reasoning from the shared underlying cause (’illah) of impairing the mind, rather than the specific substance named in the text. This lets a seventh-century ruling remain workable for entirely new substances today.

Examiner note: Weaker answers describe qiyas or ijma’ in the abstract without giving the specific worked example the question asks for. Naming the khamr-to-modern-intoxicants case (or a comparable, clearly explained example) with the reasoning behind it is what earns the marks — a definition alone is not enough.
PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · PAPER 1, Q2 · OCT/NOV 2024 (N24 W12)

(a) Describe how the four sources of Islamic Law work together in law-making. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. The Qur’an is the foundational and independent source, containing Allah’s own word and taking precedence over the other three.
  2. The Sunnah is the second source, explaining and supplementing Qur’anic rulings — its authority is itself established by the Qur’an’s instruction to obey the Prophet.
  3. Ijma’ is the third source: the consensus of qualified scholars (or, in the earliest period, the Companions), used to settle questions the Qur’an and Sunnah do not directly address.
  4. Qiyas is the fourth source: reasoning by analogy from an existing Qur’anic or Sunnah ruling to a new situation that shares the same underlying cause.
  5. A worked example showing all four in sequence: the Qur’an prohibits khamr; the Sunnah records the Prophet clarifying the broader principle behind the prohibition; scholarly ijma’ agreed on this understanding; qiyas then extended the same ruling to modern intoxicants not named in the original text.
  6. The four sources are ranked, not equal — a ruling from qiyas can never override the plain wording of the Qur’an or an authentic Sunnah.

Part (b) — “Is ijma’ and qiyas more important today than in the past?” (4 marks) — a balanced answer weighs both sides: some argue ijma’ and qiyas matter more today because modern issues (banking and finance, medical ethics, new technology) are not directly addressed by the Qur’an or Sunnah and require fresh scholarly reasoning; others argue the Qur’an and Sunnah remain permanently the most important sources in principle, since ijma’ and qiyas only ever operate within the boundaries those first two sources set, and can never generate a ruling that contradicts them — so their “importance” is about volume of use, not rank.

Examiner note: This question rewards candidates who keep the four sources in their correct hierarchical order while still explaining how they cooperate — don’t describe them as four independent, equal options a jurist can pick between; describe them as a sequence consulted in order until an answer is found.
