Harb al-Fijar and Hilf al-Fudul

3.1 Life in Makkah · the sacrilegious war · the pact of the virtuous

A teenager who hated war, and a pact he later said he’d honour even after becoming a prophet — two events, a few years apart, that reveal exactly the kind of leader Muhammad (pbuh) would become long before he received a single verse of revelation.

□ Predict before you read

A war broke out in Makkah during Muhammad’s teenage years — but he is never described as fighting in it directly. What kind of role do you think a fifteen-year-old from a respected clan, present at a battle but not a combatant, might actually have played?

Harb al-Fijar: “The Sacrilegious War”

When Muhammad was around fifteen years old, fighting broke out between the tribal confederation of Kinana — which included the Quraysh — and the rival tribe of Qays Aylan, following violations of the sanctity that was supposed to be observed during the sacred months and within the Haram (sanctuary) around Makkah. Because this sanctity was broken, the conflict became known as Harb al-Fijar, “the war of sacrilege” or “the evil war.”

Muhammad was present, but the sources are clear that he did not take part in the actual fighting. His role was to collect the arrows that missed their targets and hand them back to his uncles so they could be reused. He later recalled this himself: “I remember participating in the Fijar Wars, and I would collect arrows for my uncles and hand them back to them.” It is a small, almost domestic detail — a teenage boy running arrows back to older relatives on a battlefield — but it matters for the seerah because of what it tells us about the values he carried out of that experience. Having witnessed the destruction and pointless bloodshed of tribal warfare up close, without directly causing any of it, Muhammad developed what tradition remembers as a lifelong dislike of war and unnecessary violence — a distaste that shaped his conduct even decades later as the leader of a growing Muslim community obliged, at times, to fight in self-defence.

Hilf al-Fudul: The Pact of the Virtuous

Out of the disorder and tribal rivalry exposed by Harb al-Fijar came a very different kind of gathering. A group of Quraysh clans met at the house of Abdullah ibn Jud’an, one of Makkah’s most respected elders, and agreed a pact that has come to be called Hilf al-Fudul — the “Pact of the Virtuous” (also called Hilf al-Mutayyabin, “the pact of the perfumed ones,” from the way the participants sealed their agreement by dipping their hands in perfume and touching the Kaabah). Its purpose was clear and, for tribal Arabia, remarkably principled: the signatories pledged to stand together against any injustice committed in Makkah, and to support the wronged party against the oppressor — regardless of which tribe or clan either side belonged to. This meant an outsider or a weaker clan wronged by a stronger one could now expect a body of allied clans to take their side, rather than tribal loyalty alone deciding the outcome.

Muhammad, then in his early twenties, was present and took part in this pact. Decades later, after prophethood, he spoke of it with real pride, recalling: “I witnessed in the house of Abdullah ibn Jud’an a treaty that, were I asked to uphold it even in Islam, I would do so.” That he was willing to honour a pre-Islamic agreement even as a prophet of Islam is a striking endorsement — it shows that Hilf al-Fudul’s core principle, defending the wronged regardless of tribe or status, was one he saw as entirely consistent with the justice Islam itself would go on to teach.

Why These Two Episodes Are Paired

Harb al-Fijar and Hilf al-Fudul sit together in the seerah almost as cause and effect: the chaos and injustice of tribal warfare exposed the need for exactly the kind of principled, cross-tribal alliance that Hilf al-Fudul provided. Seen together, they show a young Muhammad already forming the instincts that would define his later leadership — reluctance toward violence, and a firm commitment to justice for the wronged that did not depend on tribal politics.

Harb al-Fijar

  • A war between Kinana (incl. Quraysh) and Qays Aylan
  • Caused by violation of the sacred months/Haram
  • Muhammad (c. age 15) took no combat role — only collected arrows for his uncles
  • Left him with a lasting dislike of war and bloodshed

Hilf al-Fudul

  • A peace pact agreed at the house of Abdullah ibn Jud’an
  • Formed in response to the injustice exposed by tribal conflict
  • Muhammad (early 20s) was a willing participant
  • He later said he would honour it even as a prophet — a mark of its enduring principle

□ Elaborate — justice inherited from pre-Islamic Arabia

The Prophet later said he would still honour the Hilf al-Fudul “even in Islam.” Using what you know about Islamic teachings on justice, explain in your own words why a pre-Islamic pact to defend the wronged, regardless of tribe, might fit comfortably within Islamic values rather than contradicting them.

□ A note on the famous “red camels” saying

You may encounter a popular retelling of the Prophet’s later comment on Hilf al-Fudul phrased as “I would not exchange it for a herd of red camels” (red camels being a byword for the greatest wealth an Arab could own). The exact form quoted in this course’s primary source is: “I witnessed in the house of Abdullah ibn Jud’an a treaty that, were I asked to uphold it even in Islam, I would do so.” Both versions carry the same essential meaning — that he valued the pact enormously — so either form is acceptable in an exam answer, but be ready to explain the meaning behind whichever wording you use rather than quoting it without understanding.
