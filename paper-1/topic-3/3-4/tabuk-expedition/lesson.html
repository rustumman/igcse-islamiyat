Tabuk Expedition

3.4 Life in Madinah · Rajab, 9 AH · the Army of Hardship, and the three who told the truth

No enemy shows up. Not a single sword is drawn. And yet the Battle of Tabuk, fought in the “Year of Hardship,” turns out to be one of the most revealing episodes of the entire seerah — a test of sincerity that separates true believers from hypocrites more sharply than any battle ever did.

□ Predict before you read

Before you read: the Prophet called an army of roughly 30,000 out in punishing summer heat, during harvest season, on a 650 km march toward an enemy that — as it turned out — never actually arrived. What do you think the real purpose of such an expedition could be, if not combat itself? Keep this in mind as you read about who went, who made excuses, and why it mattered so much.

Why “the Year of Hardship”

In Rajab, 9 AH — roughly six months after the siege of Ta’if — reports reached Madinah of a major Byzantine military mobilisation on the northern frontier. The Prophet, unusually, announced both the destination and the seriousness of the threat well in advance, calling for the largest expedition Arabia had ever seen. The campaign earned the name Jaysh al-Usrah, “the Army of Hardship” or “the Year of Hardship,” for several compounding reasons:

  • It fell during the hottest season, with temperatures reportedly reaching around 110°F (43°C).
  • It required marching roughly 650 km northwest — the farthest expedition the Muslims had yet undertaken.
  • It coincided with the harvest season, forcing many to abandon their main annual income.
  • Supplies ran desperately short en route; some reports describe soldiers extracting water from slaughtered camels’ stomachs to survive.

Excuses, generosity, and hypocrisy exposed

Because the expedition was announced openly and demanded genuine sacrifice, it became an unusually clear test of sincerity. The hypocrites (munafiqun) made a variety of excuses to avoid joining — some fabricated, some outright refusals — behaviour the Qur’an addresses directly and harshly in Surah at-Tawbah. In sharp contrast, the sincere Companions gave with striking generosity:

  • Abu Bakr donated his entire wealth, leaving nothing behind for his own family.
  • Umar donated half of everything he owned.
  • Uthman ibn Affan funded roughly a third of the entire army’s costs, prompting the Prophet’s declaration: “nothing will harm Uthman after this day, whatever he does.”

Roughly 30,000 Muslims ultimately reached Tabuk, on the frontier of modern-day Jordan/Saudi Arabia — by far the largest Muslim force the Prophet ever commanded.

At Tabuk: no battle, but real gains

On arrival, the Muslims discovered no Byzantine army had actually assembled to meet them — the threat had either dissipated or been exaggerated. Rather than treating the expedition as wasted effort, the Prophet used the opportunity to establish peace treaties and tribute agreements with several local Christian and Jewish communities along the route, including at Aylah, extending Muslim political influence peacefully into the region without a single battle being fought.

On the return march, an assassination plot by a group of hypocrites was discovered and foiled: they had planned to startle the Prophet’s camel over a dangerous mountain pass at night, hoping to cause a fatal fall. The plot failed, and the Prophet was informed of at least some of the individuals responsible, though he chose not to publicly expose or punish most of them.

The three who told the truth

Among those who had stayed behind without a valid excuse, most later offered false justifications and swore oaths to the Prophet, who accepted their apologies at face value — a leniency the Qur’an itself later corrects (9:43). Only three Companions — most famously Ka’b ibn Malik, along with Murara ibn al-Rabi’ and Hilal ibn Umayyah — chose honesty over convenient excuses, admitting plainly that they had simply failed to prepare in time, with no valid reason at all.

Their honesty came at a real cost: the Prophet ordered the entire Muslim community to boycott the three of them completely for fifty days — no one, including close family, was to speak to them. Ka’b later described the isolation as agonising; even a tempting offer of asylum and support from a foreign king, sent to exploit his situation, was recognised by Ka’b as a test and burned unread. After fifty days of genuine hardship, Allah revealed their forgiveness in the Qur’an (9:117–119), and Ka’b resolved never again to tell anything but the truth for the rest of his life.

Why it mattered

Though bloodless, Tabuk was a major show of Muslim strength and unity — the largest army Arabia had ever witnessed, mobilised and sustained across hundreds of kilometres despite crushing hardship. Its impact rippled outward: over the following months, tribes across the Arabian Peninsula, recognising this display of power and organisation, began sending delegations to Madinah to accept Islam, a period that came to be known as the Year of Delegations (9–10 AH) — the final, peaceful consolidation of Arabia under Islam before the Prophet’s Farewell Hajj and death, covered in the next lesson.

(a) Describe the main events of the Battle of Khaybar and the Battle of Tabuk. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Causes: reports of Byzantine mobilisation in 9 AH prompted the Prophet to call the largest expedition yet, openly announcing the destination unlike previous campaigns.
  2. Hardship: extreme heat, harvest-season timing, and a roughly 650 km march earned it the name “the Year of Hardship.”
  3. Generosity versus excuses: Abu Bakr’s total donation, Uthman’s funding of a third of the army, contrasted with the hypocrites’ evasions.
  4. Roughly 30,000 reached Tabuk; no Byzantine army was found, but peace treaties were made with local communities such as Aylah.
  5. An assassination plot on the return journey was foiled.
  6. Ka’b ibn Malik and two others’ honest confession and subsequent 50-day boycott, ending in their Qur’anically-revealed forgiveness (9:117–119).

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

See the Khaybar and Letters to Rulers lesson for the (b) content specific to Khaybar's perseverance, since this question's (b) part targets Khaybar rather than Tabuk.

Examiner note: This is a two-battle question with only 10 AO1 marks — candidates must budget detail proportionally across both Khaybar and Tabuk rather than writing exhaustively on one. For Tabuk specifically, examiners look for the cause (Byzantine mobilisation rumour), the scale (30,000, the largest army), and the outcome (no battle, but treaties made) as the minimum core facts.
