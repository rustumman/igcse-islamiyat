Mu’tah and Hunayn

3.4 Life in Madinah · 8 AH · a retreat called a victory, and an ambush turned into a rout

Two battles in the same year, 8 AH, either side of the Muslims’ single greatest triumph — one a costly retreat against the mightiest empire in the region, the other an ambush that nearly routs the largest Muslim army yet assembled. Both end with the Prophet’s mercy on full display.

□ Predict before you read

Before you read: at Mu’tah, three Muslim commanders are killed in succession against a vastly larger force, yet the Prophet calls the resulting retreat a victory, not a defeat. What do you think could justify calling a retreat a “victory”? Keep this question in mind as you read about Khalid ibn al-Walid’s role.

The compensatory Umrah (context)

Before either battle, the Muslims fulfilled the promise made at Hudaybiyyah: in 7 AH, roughly 2,000 pilgrims performed the delayed Umrat al-Qada (“compensatory Umrah”) in Makkah, watched anxiously by the Quraysh. It was during this visit that three especially significant figures accepted Islam: the future general Khalid ibn al-Walid, the statesman Amr ibn al-As, and Uthman ibn Talha, later custodian of the Ka’bah — conversions that would shape the military campaigns to come.

The Battle of Mu’tah, 8 AH

The immediate cause of Mu’tah was a serious breach of diplomatic protocol: the Prophet’s envoy, al-Harith ibn Umayr, sent to deliver a message toward Byzantine territory, was intercepted and executed by the Ghassanid governor Shurahbil ibn Amr, a Christian Arab vassal of Byzantium. In response, the Prophet sent an army of roughly 3,000 north into what is now Jordan in Jumada al-Awwal, 8 AH — with an unusual precaution: he named three successive commanders in advance in case each fell in turn — Zayd ibn Harithah, then Ja’far ibn Abi Talib, then Abdullah ibn Rawahah.

The Muslim force met a far larger Byzantine-aligned army (traditional accounts describe overwhelming numbers, though modern estimates suggest perhaps 10,000 combined Roman and Arab tribal fighters). All three named commanders were killed in succession during the fighting. At that point Khalid ibn al-Walid — recently converted, and now taking command in the field — recognised that outright victory was not achievable, and instead executed a skilful fighting withdrawal: repositioning archers, rotating units to give the impression of reinforcement, and extracting the army largely intact rather than allowing it to be annihilated. Muslim losses were fewer than 20 out of roughly 3,000.

When the army returned to Madinah, some accused the survivors of cowardice for retreating. The Prophet firmly rejected this framing, describing the retreat instead as “the day of retreat that Allah opened the way for,” not a defeat — and gave Khalid the title Sayfullah, “the Sword of Allah,” for saving the army from destruction.

The Battle of Hunayn, 8 AH

Hunayn followed almost immediately after the Conquest of Makkah (covered in the previous lesson), in the same year, 8 AH. The tribes of Hawazin and Thaqif, led by the young chieftain Malik ibn Awf, feared that the newly triumphant and rapidly growing Muslim force would turn against them next, and moved first to attack before that could happen.

The Prophet marched out with the largest Muslim army yet assembled — around 12,000 — combining seasoned Madinan Muslims with thousands of newly converted Makkans. Confidence in these numbers proved a danger in itself: as the army advanced into the narrow Hunayn valley, Hawazin and Thaqif forces, positioned in ambush on the high ground, launched a sudden volley that threw the Muslim vanguard into panic and flight. The Qur’an records the moment directly:

Allah has already given you victory in many regions and [even] on the day of Hunayn, when your great number pleased you, but it did not avail you at all, and the earth was confining for you with [in spite of] its vastness; then you turned back, fleeing. Qur’an 9:25

At the height of the crisis, the Prophet himself held his ground, mounted and visible, with only a small core of Companions around him — including Abu Bakr, Umar, and Ali. His uncle al-Abbas, known for his powerful voice, was instructed to call the retreating fighters back by name and by tribe, and the army gradually rallied and turned. The Qur’an credits this turning point to divine reinforcement:

Then Allah sent down His tranquillity upon His Messenger and upon the believers and sent down soldiers [i.e., angels] whom you did not see. Qur’an 9:26

The regrouped Muslims routed Hawazin and Thaqif decisively, capturing enormous spoils — reportedly around 6,000 prisoners, and tens of thousands of camels and goats.

Winning hearts: gifts to new converts, and the Ansar’s concern

In distributing the spoils, the Prophet gave especially large gifts — around 100 camels each — to recently converted Makkan leaders such as Abu Sufyan and Safwan ibn Umayyah, a category the Qur’an describes as al-mu’allafatu qulubuhum (“those whose hearts are to be won over”). This pragmatic generosity worked: Safwan later said the Prophet had gone “from the most despised person to me, to the most beloved.”

The Ansar, who received comparatively little from this distribution, felt briefly overlooked, prompting murmuring among some of the younger men. When this reached the Prophet, he gathered the Ansar and delivered a deeply moving address, reminding them of their unmatched place in his heart: “If all mankind went in one direction, and the Ansar in another, I would go with the Ansar,” and asking them: “are you not content that others go back with sheep and camels, while you go back with the Messenger of Allah?” The Ansar were moved to tears, and the moment resolved any lingering resentment.

□ Common mix-up to avoid

Mu’tah is not a defeat in the way Uhud was — the Muslim army was never destroyed, and Khalid’s withdrawal preserved it for future campaigns. Examiners want you to explain specifically why the Prophet called the outcome a victory (avoiding annihilation against overwhelming odds), rather than simply labelling it a loss.

□ Elaborate — winning over hearts

At Hunayn, the Prophet gave especially large gifts to recently converted Makkan leaders, while the long-loyal Ansar initially received much less. In your own words, explain the political and spiritual reasoning behind “winning over hearts” (al-mu’allafatu qulubuhum), and why the Prophet’s later words to the Ansar mattered so much in resolving their feelings.

PAST PAPER · N25 w11 · 0493/11 · Q14

(a) Describe the Battles of Mu'ta and Hunayn. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Mu’tah: caused by the execution of the Prophet’s envoy; an army of about 3,000 faced a much larger Byzantine-aligned force; three named commanders (Zayd, Ja’far, Abdullah ibn Rawahah) were killed in turn; Khalid ibn al-Walid took command and extracted the army, earning the title Sayfullah.
  2. Hunayn: fought immediately after the Conquest of Makkah, 8 AH; Hawazin and Thaqif under Malik ibn Awf ambushed the roughly 12,000-strong Muslim army in the Hunayn valley, initially routing the vanguard.
  3. The Prophet held his ground with a small core of Companions; al-Abbas called the army back by name and tribe; the Muslims regrouped and won decisively, taking large spoils.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) What lessons can be learnt from the Prophet's actions at Hunayn?” Model answer approach: courage and steadfastness in crisis (the Prophet holding his ground when others fled); the danger of overconfidence in numbers (Qur'an 9:25); wise leadership in a crisis (using al-Abbas's voice to rally troops); and generosity/diplomacy after victory (gifts to recent converts, reassurance to the Ansar).

Examiner note: Since this question covers two separate battles in only 10 marks, candidates should give a balanced, concise account of each rather than exhausting the word count on one — examiners specifically credit candidates who cover both events with clear causes and outcomes.
