Khaybar and Letters to Rulers

3.4 Life in Madinah · 7 AH · the last hostile stronghold, and letters to the rulers of the world

Fresh from Hudaybiyyah’s two years of peace, the Prophet (pbuh) does two very different things at once: he clears out the last hostile stronghold near Madinah, and he writes to the most powerful rulers on earth. One is a siege; the other is a letter. Both change the future of Islam.

□ Predict before you read

Before you read: the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah gave the Muslims ten years of peace with the Quraysh. What would you do with that breathing space if you were the Prophet — expand militarily, focus on diplomacy, or both? Read on to see how he used the peace for exactly that combination.

Why Khaybar

With the Quraysh threat neutralised by the Hudaybiyyah truce, the remaining danger near Madinah was Khaybar, a fertile oasis roughly 180 km to the north, and by 7 AH home to the exiled leaders of Banu Nadir and remnants of Banu Qaynuqa. From Khaybar, these leaders had already helped incite the coalition that besieged Madinah at the Trench, and they continued plotting against the Muslims, including an earlier attempted poisoning of the Prophet. Khaybar itself was heavily fortified: 8–15 separate strongholds, each a largely self-contained walled compound housing 100–500 people, built using fortification knowledge its Jewish inhabitants had brought originally from Yemen.

The siege

The Prophet marched out with roughly 1,400–1,700 troops, covering the 180 km in under two days and achieving near-total surprise by camping overnight and attacking just before dawn. Because Khaybar’s strongholds were not unified into a single defensive system, the Muslims were able to besiege and capture them one by one rather than facing the whole garrison at once. The fortress of Na’im put up the longest resistance, holding out for around ten days. After early attempts by other commanders failed to take it, the Prophet announced: “tomorrow I will hand the banner to someone whom Allah and His Messenger love, and who loves Allah and His Messenger.” The banner went to Ali ibn Abi Talib, who led the decisive assault — famously said to have used a captured fortress door as an improvised shield after his own was destroyed.

During the campaign, a Jewish woman attempted to poison the Prophet by lacing roasted lamb, targeting the shoulder joint specifically. The Prophet detected the taste and stopped eating before it took full effect, though he reportedly suffered lasting effects from the exposure for the rest of his life.

Settlement

Once the strongholds fell, the Prophet did not expel Khaybar’s population as he had Banu Nadir and Banu Qurayza. Instead, a peaceful settlement was reached: the Jewish farmers of Khaybar were permitted to remain on their land as sharecroppers (muzara’ah), working the fertile date groves in exchange for a share of the harvest paid to the Muslim state — a pragmatic arrangement that preserved a skilled agricultural community and its produce rather than destroying it.

Letters to the rulers of the world

In this same broad period — following the peace of Hudaybiyyah, which gave the Prophet room to think beyond Arabia — he sent formal letters to major regional rulers, inviting each personally to accept Islam. Each letter followed a consistent diplomatic protocol: sealed with wax, opened with “Bismillah,” addressed to the recipient by their highest title, and kept to a single concise paragraph. The results varied dramatically:

  • Heraclius, Byzantine Emperor. The letter warned that rejecting the message would burden him with the sins of his followers. Heraclius, intrigued, interrogated the merchant Abu Sufyan — still not yet Muslim — who happened to be in the region, deliberately placing his companions behind him so they could expose any lie. Abu Sufyan’s honest answers (that the Prophet came from a noble family, had never lied, never broken a promise, and that his followers were growing rather than abandoning him) led Heraclius to conclude privately that the message was likely true — remarking that “if I could reach him, I would go and wash his feet” — yet he never converted, remaining Christian for political reasons.
  • The Negus (Najashi) of Abyssinia. This letter, uniquely, omitted the warning clause given to other rulers, instead stressing shared belief about Jesus (pbuh) as God’s messenger and word. The Negus accepted Islam.
  • The rulers of Oman and Bahrain. Both accepted the message and Islam spread peacefully in these regions.
  • Muqawqis, ruler of Egypt. He did not convert, but responded courteously with valuable gifts, including a mule and two women, one of whom, Maria the Copt, later became a companion of the Prophet’s household.
  • Chosroes (Khosrow II) of Persia. Enraged by being addressed as an equal, he tore the letter to pieces before the messenger. The Prophet responded with a prediction: “he has torn up my letter; Allah will tear apart his kingdom.” Within months, Khosrow was overthrown and killed in a palace coup by his own son — an outcome later Muslims took as a striking fulfilment of the Prophet’s words, and one that also contributed to the rapid collapse of the Persian Empire in the following decades.

□ Elaborate — adapting the message to the audience

The letter to the Negus of Abyssinia was worded differently from the others — it stressed shared belief about Jesus (pbuh) rather than including a warning clause. In your own words, explain what this difference suggests about how the Prophet adapted his approach to different audiences while keeping the same core message.

PAST PAPER · J22 s12 · 0493/12 · Q14

(a) Describe the main events of the Battle of Khaybar and the Battle of Tabuk. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Khaybar: causes (exiled Banu Nadir leaders based there continuing to plot against Madinah); the march of roughly 1,400–1,700 troops in 7 AH; the siege and capture of multiple fortresses one by one; Ali’s leadership at Na’im after being given the banner; the peaceful sharecropping settlement afterward.
  2. Tabuk: covered in the dedicated Tabuk lesson in this unit — note the pairing here but see that lesson for full AO1 content on Tabuk itself.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) What might Muslims learn from the Muslims' perseverance at the Battle of Khaybar?” Model answer approach: the value of persistence through a difficult, multi-stage siege (Na'im took around ten days after other attempts failed); trusting capable leadership (giving the banner to Ali only after prior attempts failed); and that patience under prolonged difficulty is ultimately rewarded.

Examiner note: Because this question pairs two separate battles, candidates must budget their time and detail across both rather than writing exhaustively on one and leaving the other thin — examiners expect a reasonably balanced account of each within the 10 marks.
PAST PAPER · J25 s12 · 0493/12 · Q14

(a) Describe the letters the Prophet sent to spread Islam outside Madina. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Context: after the peace secured by Hudaybiyyah, the Prophet sent formal letters to major rulers beyond Arabia, each following consistent diplomatic protocol (sealed, opening with Bismillah, concise).
  2. Heraclius of Byzantium: received respectfully; Abu Sufyan’s interrogation by Heraclius is a key supporting detail; did not convert.
  3. The Negus of Abyssinia: accepted Islam; the letter’s wording stressed shared belief in Jesus (pbuh).
  4. Rulers of Oman and Bahrain: accepted Islam.
  5. Muqawqis of Egypt: did not convert but sent respectful gifts, including Maria the Copt.
  6. Chosroes of Persia: tore up the letter in contempt; the Prophet’s prediction of his kingdom’s downfall was fulfilled within months.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) Explain why it was important to invite outside leaders to Islam.” Model answer approach: spreading the message of Islam beyond Arabia was part of the Prophet's universal mission; inviting rulers directly could open entire nations to Islam peacefully through their example; it demonstrated confidence and legitimacy as an emerging political power; and it fulfilled a religious obligation to convey the message to all people, regardless of the outcome.

Examiner note: Candidates often forget to name the specific rulers and their differing responses — a strong answer names at least three or four rulers and states clearly what each one did (accepted, declined respectfully, or rejected with contempt), since this level of detail is what distinguishes AO1 answers scoring highly.
