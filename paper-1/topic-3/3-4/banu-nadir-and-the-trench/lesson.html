Banu Nadir and the Trench

3.4 Life in Madinah · 4–5 AH · Salman al-Farsi’s khandaq, and betrayal from within

A foreign idea — dig a trench around the city — turns Arabia’s largest coalition army into a stalled, freezing, demoralised mob. The Battle of the Trench is the seerah’s masterclass in defensive strategy, and its ugliest chapter of betrayal from within.

□ Predict before you read

Before you read: facing an army of roughly 10,000 with only around 3,000 defenders, the Muslims could not win a pitched battle in the open. What kind of strategy would you choose if you simply needed to survive a siege long enough for the enemy’s own morale to collapse? Compare your idea with Salman al-Farsi’s actual proposal.

The expulsion of Banu Nadir

Before the great siege of Madinah, another of the city’s Jewish tribes broke faith with the Constitution of Madinah. In 4 AH, when the Prophet visited the territory of Banu Nadir to negotiate blood-money for victims of an earlier massacre, their leaders secretly plotted to kill him by dropping a millstone from a rooftop as he sat below. Warned by revelation, the Prophet left the scene without explanation, then sent an ultimatum: leave Madinah within ten days, or face death. Banu Nadir initially considered resistance after Abdullah ibn Ubayy promised military support, but when the moment came he provided none. The Prophet besieged their fortress for roughly 7–10 days; they surrendered unconditionally and were allowed to leave with whatever their camels could carry, excluding weapons — some tribespeople reportedly dismantled even their own doors and roof-beams rather than leave them for the Muslims. Most of Banu Nadir relocated to the Jewish stronghold of Khaybar, where — as this lesson goes on to show — they would soon incite a far larger war against Madinah.

Causes of the Battle of the Trench

By 5 AH, the exiled leaders of Banu Nadir, still smarting from their expulsion, travelled to Makkah and proposed an unprecedented alliance: unite every enemy of Madinah into one combined force, backed by promises of food and funding from Khaybar’s date harvest. The Quraysh, still stinging from Badr and Uhud, agreed, and recruited further tribes — the Ghatafan, Banu Asad, Banu Sulaym, and others — assembling what became the largest coalition (al-Ahzab, “the confederates”) ever to march against the Muslims: roughly 10,000 fighters, led by Abu Sufyan, against a Muslim defending force of only around 3,000.

Salman al-Farsi’s trench

Facing overwhelming numbers in open ground, the Prophet consulted his Companions on strategy. Salman al-Farsi, a Persian Companion familiar with Persian siege warfare unknown to the Arabs, proposed digging a defensive trench (khandaq) around the one exposed approach to Madinah — the rest of the city being naturally protected by volcanic rock and dense palm groves. The trench, roughly 2 km long and several metres deep and wide, was completed in under ten days of continuous labour by the whole Muslim community, the Prophet working alongside them despite severe food shortages — he reportedly tied a stone to his own stomach against hunger. This unfamiliar tactic gives the battle its two names: Khandaq (“the Trench”) and al-Ahzab (“the Confederates”).

Siege, stalemate, and betrayal from within

For roughly a month, the coalition camped outside the trench, unable to cross it in force; only a handful of horsemen — including the champion Amr ibn Abd Wud — managed to leap it, and Amr was killed in single combat by Ali ibn Abi Talib after the Prophet permitted him to fight. Muslim night patrols shouted “Allahu Akbar” along the trench line to exaggerate their numbers, and conditions grew desperate on both sides.

The gravest danger came not from outside the trench but from within Madinah itself. Banu Qurayza, the last of the city’s three major Jewish tribes and still formally bound by the Constitution of Madinah, was persuaded by the exiled Nadir leader Huyayy ibn Akhtab to abandon their treaty and open secret talks with the confederates — threatening the Muslims with a two-front catastrophe: roughly 10,000 enemies outside the trench, and a further force potentially attacking from inside the city. The Qur’an captures the terror of this moment directly:

[Remember] when they came at you from above you and from below you, and when eyes shifted [in fear], and hearts reached the throats, and you assumed about Allah [various] assumptions. Qur’an 33:10

Nu’aym’s deception and the sandstorm

A Ghatafan tribesman, Nu’aym ibn Mas’ud, secretly converted to Islam and, with the Prophet’s approval that “war is deception,” carried out a brilliant campaign of disinformation: he warned Banu Qurayza the confederates might abandon them and told them to demand hostages first, then told the confederates that Banu Qurayza intended to hand over hostages to the Muslims. This sowed enough mutual distrust to fracture the alliance’s coordination at exactly the moment it needed unity.

Then Allah sent a violent, freezing windstorm that tore through the confederates’ camp, extinguishing fires, flattening tents, and terrifying the animals — described directly in the Qur’an:

O you who have believed, remember the favour of Allah upon you when armies came to [attack] you and We sent upon them a wind and armies [of angels] you did not see. Qur’an 33:9

Demoralised, exhausted, and now distrustful of one another, the confederates abandoned the siege and dispersed. Muslim casualties throughout the siege were remarkably low — around 6–7 dead, including the respected Ansari leader Sa’d ibn Mu’adh, mortally wounded by an arrow.

The siege and judgement of Banu Qurayza

The moment the confederates withdrew, the Prophet, by revelation, marched directly on Banu Qurayza without pause. Their fortress was besieged for roughly 25 days; offered the option to fight, negotiate for terms like Banu Nadir’s, or convert, they instead sought unconditional negotiation and eventually surrendered on the Prophet’s demand for unconditional surrender.

At Banu Qurayza’s own request — since Sa’d ibn Mu’adh, though mortally wounded, was chief of their long-standing allied tribe, the Aws — the Prophet allowed Sa’d to arbitrate their fate. Sa’d, carried to the scene, ruled according to the law he understood to apply to a besieged people who had broken covenant: that the adult men should be executed, property distributed, and the women and children spared and resettled. The Prophet responded: “you have decreed upon them the judgement of Allah from above the seven heavens.” Estimates of the men executed vary between sources, roughly 400–700. This is presented in the seerah not as an act of religious persecution but as the outcome of an internally-agreed arbitration for wartime treason, judged by the standard of their own tribal ally rather than imposed unilaterally by the Muslims.

□ Where this comes from

Sa’d ibn Mu’adh’s ruling is described by early biographers as being made according to the law of the Torah/Deuteronomy governing a besieged city that had broken covenant with its defenders — a standard that Banu Qurayza’s own leaders would have recognised, and one they themselves requested by asking for an Aws arbitrator. Cambridge examiners generally expect you to state that this was an arbitrated judgement carried out at the tribe’s own request for treaty-breaking during an active siege, not an unprovoked attack.

□ Elaborate — wartime deception versus treaty betrayal

Nu’aym ibn Mas’ud’s deception campaign against the confederates is described approvingly by the Prophet as legitimate “war and deception.” In your own words, explain the difference between this kind of wartime strategy and betrayal of a signed treaty, such as what Banu Qurayza did — why is one considered acceptable and the other a serious breach?

PAST PAPER · J24 s12 · 0493/12 · Q14

(a) Describe the events of the Battle of the Trench (Khandaq). 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Causes: exiled Banu Nadir leaders helped unite Quraysh, Ghatafan, and other tribes into a coalition of roughly 10,000 against a Muslim force of about 3,000, in 5 AH.
  2. Salman al-Farsi proposed digging a trench around Madinah’s one exposed side, an unfamiliar tactic completed in under ten days.
  3. The siege stalemated; only a few horsemen, including Amr ibn Abd Wud, crossed the trench, and Amr was killed by Ali in single combat.
  4. Banu Qurayza broke their treaty and opened secret talks with the confederates, threatening Madinah with a two-front attack.
  5. Nu’aym ibn Mas’ud’s deception sowed distrust between the confederates and Banu Qurayza.
  6. A violent windstorm (Qur’an 33:9) scattered the confederates’ camp, ending the siege; the Prophet then besieged and defeated Banu Qurayza, whose fate was arbitrated by Sa’d ibn Mu’adh.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) What leadership skills can Muslims today adopt from this battle?” Model answer approach: consultation (shura) before deciding strategy; willingness to adopt new, unfamiliar ideas from outside one's own culture (Salman's trench); personally sharing hardship with followers; and using intelligence/psychological strategy (Nu'aym's mission) rather than relying on force alone.

Examiner note: Candidates frequently forget to mention Salman al-Farsi by name or the origin of the trench idea — examiners specifically reward naming both the individual and the fact that it was a foreign (Persian) military technique adopted through consultation, which is itself a leadership lesson.
