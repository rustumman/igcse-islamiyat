Treaty of Hudaybiyyah

3.4 Life in Madinah · Dhu al-Qa’dah, 6 AH · a “manifest victory” that looked like anything but

Fourteen hundred unarmed pilgrims set out for Umrah — and come home having agreed to terms that look, on the surface, like surrender. The Qur’an calls it a “manifest victory” anyway. This lesson is about learning to trust the long game.

□ Predict before you read

Before you read: imagine you have travelled for days in peace, unarmed, only to be turned away at the gates and forced to sign a treaty that seems to favour your rivals in almost every visible way. Would you call that a defeat? Read on to see why the Qur’an insists otherwise — and what changed within two years to prove it right.

The vision and the journey

In Dhu al-Qa’dah, 6 AH, the Prophet (pbuh) saw a vision of himself performing Umrah at the Ka’bah in safety. Taking this as a sign to act, he called the Muhajirun, Ansar, and surrounding tribes to join him on a peaceful pilgrimage — unarmed except for the traveller’s sword, and bringing seventy sacrificial camels garlanded to mark their religious purpose. Around 1,400 Muslims set out; many Bedouin tribes, fearing conflict, refused to join, a cowardice the Qur’an later criticises. Deliberately, the journey began at the start of the sacred months, when pre-Islamic custom obliged even hostile tribes to permit pilgrims safe passage.

The Quraysh block the way

Learning of the approach, the Quraysh — fearing the political humiliation of appearing unable to stop Muhammad from entering their city, whatever his intentions — sent Khalid ibn al-Walid to block the direct route with armed men. Advised by Abu Bakr to avoid confrontation, the Prophet led the pilgrims along a difficult alternate path and camped at the plain of Hudaybiyyah, just outside Makkah’s sanctuary boundary.

Failed emissaries and the killing rumour

A series of intermediaries tried to break the standoff. Budayl ibn Warqa’ of Khuza’ah conveyed the Muslims’ peaceful intent, but the Quraysh, driven by pride, refused to yield. Urwah ibn Mas’ud of Thaqif, sent by the Quraysh to assess the Muslims, was so struck by the Companions’ devotion to the Prophet — rushing to catch every drop of his ablution water — that he told the Quraysh: “I have never seen any king shown such respect by his people.” The Muslims then sent their own envoy, Uthman ibn Affan, chosen for his family ties to the Quraysh leadership, into Makkah to negotiate directly.

When Uthman’s return was delayed, a rumour spread that he had been killed. In response, the Prophet gathered the Companions beneath a tree at Hudaybiyyah and called for a pledge:

Certainly was Allah pleased with the believers when they pledged allegiance to you [O Muhammad] under the tree, and He knew what was in their hearts, so He sent down tranquillity upon them and rewarded them with an imminent conquest. Qur’an 48:18

This became known as the Bay’at ar-Ridwan (“the Pledge of the Tree” or “Pledge of Ridwan”): roughly 1,400 Companions pledged to fight to the death if necessary to avenge Uthman, with the Prophet symbolically clasping his own hand for Uthman’s, since he was still absent in Makkah. Shortly after, Uthman returned safely — the rumour had been false — but the pledge had already demonstrated the Companions’ total commitment.

Negotiating the treaty

The Quraysh finally sent their most capable negotiator, Suhayl ibn Amr, whose own son was a Muslim captive in Madinah — a fact that shaped his hard bargaining. The treaty eventually agreed contained several striking terms:

  • A ten-year truce between the Muslims and the Quraysh.
  • The Muslims must return home this year without performing Umrah, but could come back peacefully to perform it the following year.
  • Any Quraysh who joined the Muslims without a guardian’s consent must be returned to Makkah — but Muslims who left Islam to rejoin the Quraysh would not be sent back.
  • Tribes were free to ally with either side.

The imbalance of these terms was thrown into painful relief immediately: Abu Jandal, Suhayl’s own son, arrived in chains, having escaped his captors to beg for asylum with the Muslims — precisely the treaty’s first test case. Despite the Prophet’s pleas for an exception, Suhayl refused, and Abu Jandal was returned in visible distress. Even the wording of the document was contested: Suhayl objected to the Islamic opening “In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful,” insisting instead on the old formula “In Your name, O Allah,” and to the title “Muhammad, Messenger of Allah,” accepting only “Muhammad, son of Abdullah.” The Prophet, to Ali’s discomfort as scribe, agreed to both changes to preserve the agreement.

Umar’s objection

The apparent one-sidedness of the terms caused real anguish among the Companions. Umar ibn al-Khattab confronted the Prophet directly, questioning why Muslims should accept conditions that seemed to humiliate them. The Prophet’s answer rested on trust in divine command; when Umar pressed the same complaint to Abu Bakr, he received the sharper reply: “Watch out. He is the Messenger of Allah, and he will not disobey his Lord.” Umar later spent years performing extra good deeds, by his own account, to atone for having doubted the Prophet’s judgement that day.

A “manifest victory”

Despite the visible frustration, the Qur’an reframes the entire episode in Surah al-Fath, revealed on the return journey:

Indeed, We have given you, [O Muhammad], a clear conquest. Qur’an 48:1

The term fath mubin (“manifest/clear victory”) reflects a victory measured in outcomes, not appearances. By formally negotiating a treaty as an equal party, the Quraysh had implicitly recognised the Muslims as a legitimate political power for the first time. More importantly, the ten years of peace this treaty opened up — cut short after only about two, when the Quraysh themselves broke it — allowed tribes across Arabia to observe Islam directly rather than through the lens of war. According to the early biographer al-Zuhri, more people accepted Islam in the two years after Hudaybiyyah than in the whole of the previous nineteen years combined — the Muslim community grew from roughly 1,400 at Bay’at ar-Ridwan to around 10,000 by the time of the Conquest of Makkah.

□ Common mix-up to avoid

Do not describe Hudaybiyyah as a “defeat” or a treaty the Muslims “lost.” Examiners specifically reward candidates who can explain why the Qur’an itself (48:1) calls it a victory despite its unfavourable-looking terms — the reasoning about the two years of peaceful growth is the answer’s real substance, not just a fact to mention in passing.

□ Elaborate — trusting a leader’s judgement

Abu Bakr told Umar that the Prophet “will not disobey his Lord” when Umar objected to the treaty’s terms. In your own words, explain what this exchange teaches about the difference between judging a decision purely by its immediate appearance and trusting a leader’s judgement based on their track record and divine guidance.

PAST PAPER · N23 w11 · 0493/11 · Q14

(a) Describe the events at Hudaybiyya on the way to Makka. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Context: the Prophet’s vision prompted roughly 1,400 unarmed pilgrims to set out for Umrah in 6 AH; the Quraysh blocked their entry and the Muslims camped at Hudaybiyyah.
  2. Failed mediation attempts (Budayl, Urwah), then Uthman ibn Affan sent to negotiate; a rumour of his death led to the Bay’at ar-Ridwan under the tree (Qur’an 48:18).
  3. Suhayl ibn Amr negotiated the treaty: a 10-year truce, Muslims to return without performing Umrah that year, and a one-sided return clause for Quraysh converts.
  4. Abu Jandal’s forced return illustrated the treaty’s harsh terms in practice.
  5. Umar’s objection and Abu Bakr’s reply show the Companions’ internal struggle to accept the terms.
  6. Surah al-Fath was revealed, declaring the treaty a “manifest victory” (48:1).

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) What do you think is the most important lesson to be learnt from Hudaybiyya?” Model answer approach: candidates might argue for trust in Allah's plan even when outcomes look unfavourable (tawakkul); the value of patience over pride; or that peace can achieve more for a cause than continued conflict — any one argued clearly with reference to the treaty's actual long-term outcome (spread of Islam, growth to 10,000) will score well.

Examiner note: A strong answer must actually explain WHY the treaty is called a victory (link to the following two years’ growth), not simply assert that it was one — assertion without evidence loses AO2 marks.
