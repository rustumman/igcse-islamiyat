Justice and Keeping Promises

3.5 Qualities · the Black Stone dispute, and Abu Jandal at Hudaybiyyah

At thirty-five, before he was ever a prophet, he stopped a near-riot over who got to lift a sacred stone — by making sure no single tribe lifted it alone. Years later, as the leader of a state, he sent a chained, tortured, desperate convert back into the hands of his enemies, because a treaty he had signed said he must. Fairness and promise-keeping, in this lesson, are shown to cost him something real every time.

□ Predict before you read

Think of a time you kept a promise even though it turned out to be inconvenient, unpopular, or costly to keep. Write one sentence about what made you keep it anyway. Keep this in mind for the Abu Jandal episode below — one of the hardest promises anyone in the seerah is ever shown keeping.

The Black Stone dispute — fairness before he was ever a prophet

Around the age of thirty-five, years before the first revelation, the Ka’bah in Makkah was being rebuilt after flood damage, with the different clans of Quraysh dividing the reconstruction work between them. When it came time to place the sacred Black Stone (al-Hajar al-Aswad) back into its corner, a dispute broke out over which clan would have the honour of setting it. The rivalry between Banu Abd Manaf and Banu Makhzum escalated so seriously that the two sides made a blood pact to fight over it, and construction halted entirely for several days while tensions built toward real violence.

An elder eventually proposed a way out: whoever next entered the sanctuary would be asked to decide. That person turned out to be the young Muhammad (pbuh), not yet a prophet but already trusted enough by the whole of Makkah to be accepted as an arbiter. His solution avoided declaring any single winner at all: he spread out a cloak, placed the Black Stone on it himself, and asked a representative from every subtribe to take hold of an edge of the cloak. All of them lifted it together, and he then placed the stone into position with his own hands. No clan could claim sole honour, and no clan could claim to have been shut out — the fairest possible outcome achieved without anyone losing face or a single blow being struck.

Why the Black Stone episode matters for the exam

This event is worth remembering precisely because it happened before prophethood, showing that an instinct for fair, creative conflict resolution was part of his character from the start — not something that only appeared once he held religious authority. An answer that only mentions his justice as a prophet or ruler misses this earlier, arguably even more striking, piece of evidence.

Amanat left with him, even by opponents

As established in the Honesty and Trustworthiness lesson, Makkans — including some who came to oppose his message religiously — continued entrusting him with their valuables even during the years of open persecution, and widely-told tradition holds that he arranged for these deposits to reach their rightful owners even as he prepared to leave for the Hijrah, asking Ali ibn Abi Talib to remain behind in Makkah to see it done. Read together with justice and promise-keeping, this same episode makes a second point: honouring a trust (amanah) is not conditional on whether you like or agree with the person who gave it to you. He kept faith with people who did not keep faith with him.

Abu Jandal — a promise kept at real human cost

The clearest, hardest test of promise-keeping in the whole seerah comes from the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah. As the treaty’s terms were being finalised, Abu Jandal, the son of the Quraysh negotiator Suhayl ibn Amr, appeared at the Muslim camp — in chains, visibly bearing marks of torture, having been held captive in Makkah for converting to Islam. He cried out, “O Muslims, help me! O Muslims, save me!”

The problem was immediate and brutal: one of the treaty’s own terms stated that any man from the Quraysh who defected to the Muslims — even a Muslim convert — had to be returned. The Prophet (pbuh) pleaded with Suhayl repeatedly to make an exception for his own son; it is recorded that in the whole of the seerah, there is no other moment where the Prophet is shown pleading so many times over one request. Suhayl refused outright and threatened to abandon the entire treaty if the term were broken. Faced with a choice between the letter of a promise he had just signed and the anguish of a single convert in front of him, the Prophet (pbuh) upheld the treaty. He told the desperate Abu Jandal, “Be patient, O Abu Jandal, for Allah will make a way out for you,” and Abu Jandal was returned to the Quraysh.

Facing down his own Companions’ anger

This decision provoked real anger among the Muslims present, including Umar ibn al-Khattab, who struggled to accept terms that seemed to sacrifice a fellow Muslim for the sake of paperwork. The Prophet’s answer to that anger is one of the most direct statements of principle in the seerah: “I am the Messenger of Allah, and I will not disobey Allah, and He will help me.” He treated the treaty’s terms as binding regardless of how unjust or painful they felt in the moment — and, as later events at Hudaybiyyah bore out, being scrupulously literal about the agreed wording (for example, the treaty named only “men,” which meant women converts were not covered by the same return clause) later opened routes that vindicated the strict, careful reading rather than an emotional one.

Why Abu Jandal is the strongest example of promise-keeping

This episode matters more than almost any other in the syllabus because it shows that keeping a promise was not free for the Prophet (pbuh) — it cost him visible personal anguish, the safety of a desperate convert, and the patience of his own closest companions. A promise kept only when it is easy or popular is a weak example; a promise kept at real, painful cost, and defended against his own Companions’ objections, is the strongest possible evidence of the principle.

Fulfilling promises as a mark of faith

The wider seerah tradition consistently treats breaking a promise as a serious matter — it is widely narrated that breaking one’s word, along with lying and betraying a trust, is named among the signs of hypocrisy (nifaq). Read against that backdrop, the Prophet’s insistence on honouring even a painful treaty term is not simply personal stubbornness; it reflects a principle he held to be a matter of faith itself.

“Let every subtribe send its representative, and we will all put the Black Stone together.” the Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), resolving the Black Stone dispute at around age 35
“I am the Messenger of Allah, and I will not disobey Allah, and He will help me.” the Prophet Muhammad (pbuh), explaining his acceptance of the Hudaybiyyah treaty’s terms to Umar ibn al-Khattab

Justice (fair process for everyone)

  • The Black Stone: no single clan wins, no clan is shut out.
  • Deposits: honoured for opponents exactly as they would be for allies.
  • Fairness applied even before he held any religious or political authority.

Keeping promises (bound by his own word)

  • Abu Jandal returned to the Quraysh despite visible torture and his desperate pleas.
  • The Prophet pleaded repeatedly with Suhayl, but did not break the agreed term when refused.
  • He defended the decision against his own Companions’ anger, including Umar’s.

□ Elaborate — a modern “shared outcome” dispute

The Black Stone solution worked because no one had to lose. Write two or three sentences describing a modern dispute (between friends, in a classroom, or in a family) where a similar “everyone shares the outcome together” solution might work better than declaring a single winner.

□ Elaborate — was this compassion or a failure of it?

Abu Jandal’s story is genuinely difficult — a desperate man was sent back into danger because of a signed agreement. Write a short paragraph in your own words explaining why the Prophet’s choice here counts as an example of justice and promise-keeping rather than a failure of compassion. Consider both his private words of comfort to Abu Jandal and his public defence of the decision to Umar.

□ Elaborate — the easy-to-break promise

“Fulfilling promises is a mark of faith” is a principle this lesson draws from wider hadith tradition on hypocrisy. Apply it directly: describe one everyday promise (to a friend, a teacher, an employer) that is easy to break quietly without consequence — and explain, using this lesson’s reasoning, why a Muslim would still be expected to keep it.

AO1 · model answer points

  1. At around age 35, before prophethood, the Prophet resolved the dispute over placing the Black Stone by having every subtribe lift it together on a cloak, avoiding both bloodshed and favouritism.
  2. Makkans, including some who opposed his message, continued to trust him with valuables for safekeeping even during years of persecution.
  3. At the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah, Abu Jandal — tortured and in chains — begged for protection, but the treaty’s terms required his return to the Quraysh.
  4. The Prophet pleaded repeatedly with Suhayl ibn Amr to exempt his own son, but when refused, honoured the agreed terms rather than break the treaty.
  5. He comforted Abu Jandal directly, telling him Allah would make a way out for him, and defended the decision to an angry Umar by stating he would not disobey Allah.
  6. This scrupulous, literal honouring of the treaty’s exact wording later worked in the Muslims’ favour, since the treaty’s return clause named only men, not women.

AO2 — “Why is keeping promises important for Muslims?” / “How can Muslims show justice today?”

Model answer approach: keeping promises builds trust between people and reflects sincerity of faith, since breaking one’s word is named among the signs of hypocrisy; justice means treating people fairly regardless of personal feelings toward them, as shown by honouring deposits even from opponents; modern application includes keeping commitments at school or work even when inconvenient, and resolving disputes (between friends, in group projects) by finding solutions where no one is made to feel entirely defeated, as in the Black Stone example.
