Night Journey and Ascension

3.3 Life in Makkah — Life after Prophethood · a night beyond the seven heavens · al-Isra, al-Mi’raj, the five daily prayers

In a single night, the Prophet travelled further than any human being had before him — from Makkah to Jerusalem, and then up through the heavens themselves — receiving, at the very top of creation, the command that would shape every Muslim’s day ever since: the five daily prayers.

□ Predict before you read

After the grief of the Year of Sorrow and the humiliation at Ta’if, what kind of experience might Allah give the Prophet to restore his hope and confidence in his mission? Consider what a direct, personal, extraordinary sign might mean to someone at such a low point.

Roughly one year before the Hijrah — placed here because tradition holds that Khadijah died before the five daily prayers became obligatory, and the obligation of prayer was fixed on this very night — the Prophet experienced al-Isra’ wal-Mi’raj, the Night Journey and the Ascension. Coming directly after the grief of the Year of Sorrow and the humiliation at Ta’if, this event is often read as a moment of profound divine comfort and reassurance at the lowest point of the Makkan period.

Al-Isra’ — The Night Journey to Jerusalem

The journey began at Masjid al-Haram in Makkah. The Qur’an describes it directly:

“Exalted is He who took His Servant by night from al-Masjid al-Haram to al-Masjid al-Aqsa, whose surroundings We have blessed, to show him of Our signs. Indeed, He is the Hearing, the Seeing.” Surah al-Isra 17:1

The angel Jibril brought a creature called al-Buraq for the journey — described in the tradition as an animal “smaller than a mule and larger than a donkey,” pure white, and extraordinarily swift, moving with such speed that “it puts its hoof where the eye can see” (that is, covering vast distances almost instantaneously). The Prophet mounted al-Buraq, fitted with a harness and saddle, and travelled to Jerusalem, arriving at Masjid al-Aqsa.

On arrival, the Prophet tied al-Buraq to the same post traditionally used by earlier prophets, entered the mosque, and prayed two rak’at. He then led all the earlier prophets in prayer at that site — a detail of great theological significance, since it visibly established him as their leader (Imam) among the community of all the prophets, a point commonly tested for its symbolic meaning in exam questions.

Al-Mi’raj — The Ascension Through the Heavens

From Jerusalem, the Prophet ascended through the seven heavens, meeting a number of earlier prophets along the way. Tradition records these encounters, including brief exchanges of greetings and blessings at each level — among those met were Adam, Yahya (John) and Isa (Jesus) together, Yusuf (Joseph), Idris, Harun (Aaron), Musa (Moses), and finally Ibrahim (Abraham) at the seventh and highest level, closest to Paradise itself.

The Prophet’s meeting with Isa included a discussion of the signs of the Day of Judgment — Isa described his own future return, the emergence of the Dajjal, and the destruction of Gog and Magog, saying that when these events occur, the Day of Judgment will arrive suddenly, “like a pregnant woman who is just about to give birth.” Ibrahim, at the seventh heaven, conveyed his greetings to the Muslim community and described the soil of Paradise as beautiful but barren — its seedlings growing only from believers’ repeated declarations of “Subhan Allah,” “Alhamdulillah,” “La ilaha illa Allah,” and “Allahu Akbar,” a detail often used in exam answers to explain why remembrance of Allah (dhikr) is valued so highly in Islamic practice.

Sidrat al-Muntaha

The ascension finally brought the Prophet to Sidrat al-Muntaha, “the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary,” marking the furthest limit of creation reachable by any being. Its scale was described in the tradition as vast beyond ordinary comparison — its fruits as large as water jars, its leaves like the ears of elephants — and it displayed colours beyond what human perception could normally register, presented as one of Allah’s greatest signs shown to the Prophet that night.

The Command of Five Daily Prayers

At the peak of the ascension, Allah commanded fifty daily prayers upon the Muslim community. Descending, the Prophet passed Musa, who — drawing on his own experience of the difficulties his people, the Bani Isra’il, had faced in upholding religious obligations — repeatedly urged the Prophet to return and ask for a reduction, insisting that “your ummah will not be able to do this.” Through repeated negotiation, following Musa’s advice each time, the number was steadily reduced, until it settled at five daily prayers — while retaining the full reward of fifty, since, in the tradition’s words, “it is five, but it shall be rewarded with fifty.” This episode is one of the most commonly tested details in exam questions on this event, since it directly explains the origin of the five daily prayers (salah) as an obligation.

The Quraysh’s Reaction the Next Morning

When the Prophet related what had happened the following morning, the Quraysh responded with open disbelief and mockery. Some began clapping mockingly, others put their hands on their heads in disbelief, and others simply laughed and snickered at the claim.

One Qurayshi who had previously visited Jerusalem himself pressed the Prophet for proof, asking him to describe Masjid al-Aqsa in detail. As the Prophet began describing its features, he found himself momentarily unable to recall certain specific details, and — by his own account — became deeply anxious (“I became so worried and anxious that I wasn’t able to answer”). At that moment, tradition holds that Allah caused the image of Masjid al-Aqsa to appear before him, “rising up in front of me,” allowing him to answer every question with total precision. Even his questioner was forced to concede: “As for the descriptions of Bayt al-Maqdis, he is fully accurate.”

The Prophet went further, offering a second, independently verifiable proof: he described caravans he had passed on the road during the journey, including their exact expected time of arrival back in Makkah. When one of these caravans duly arrived exactly as described, Abu Jahl’s response was simply to dismiss it as “clear sorcery.”

Abu Bakr and the Title As-Siddiq

News of the Prophet’s claim reached Abu Bakr before the Prophet could tell him personally. Rather than doubt, Abu Bakr’s response became legendary in the seerah tradition. Challenged as to how he could possibly believe such an extraordinary claim, he answered:

“I believe him in something even more amazing than this: he claims that the revelation from above the seven heavens comes to him instantaneously.” Abu Bakr, on hearing of the Night Journey

Abu Bakr’s reasoning was that if he already accepted the far greater claim — that revelation from Allah, above all seven heavens, reached the Prophet directly — then a single night’s journey to Jerusalem and back was, by comparison, a far smaller matter to accept. His unwavering, immediate faith in this moment earned him the enduring title as-Siddiq, “the Testifier to Truth” — a title that has remained attached to his name in Islamic history ever since, and is frequently referenced in exam answers about the qualities of the earliest Companions.

□ Common mix-up to avoid

Al-Isra’ (the night journey to Jerusalem) and al-Mi’raj (the ascension through the heavens) are two connected but distinct stages of the same single night’s event. Exam answers should refer to them by name and describe both stages separately — journey to Jerusalem first, then ascension through the heavens — rather than treating “Isra wal-Mi’raj” as one undifferentiated trip.

□ Elaborate — fifty prayers, the reward of fifty

The number of daily prayers was reduced from fifty to five, but the reward remained equivalent to fifty. In your own words, explain what this detail might suggest about the balance Islam strikes between placing genuine obligations on believers and remaining considerate of human capacity and weakness.

PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · N21 W12 · Q14

(a) The night journey and ascension (al-isra wal-mi’raj) 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Roughly a year before the Hijrah, the Prophet was taken by night on al-Buraq from Masjid al-Haram in Makkah to Masjid al-Aqsa in Jerusalem (Surah al-Isra 17:1), where he prayed and led the earlier prophets in prayer.
  2. He then ascended through the seven heavens, meeting prophets including Adam, Yahya, Isa, Yusuf, Idris, Harun, Musa, and Ibrahim, before reaching Sidrat al-Muntaha, the furthest limit of creation.
  3. Fifty daily prayers were commanded; following Musa’s repeated advice that the Muslim community would not manage this, the number was reduced through negotiation to five, while retaining the reward of fifty.
  4. The next morning, Quraysh mocked the claim; a Qurayshi demanded a description of Masjid al-Aqsa, which the Prophet provided accurately with divine assistance, and a caravan he described arrived exactly as predicted, though Abu Jahl dismissed it as sorcery.
  5. Abu Bakr believed the account immediately and without hesitation, earning him the title as-Siddiq (“the Testifier to Truth”).

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) How did this event give the Prophet hope?” Model answer approach: connect the event directly to its timing — coming immediately after the Year of Sorrow (deaths of Khadijah and Abu Talib) and the humiliation at Ta’if, it served as a powerful reassurance of Allah’s continued support, honour, and closeness to the Prophet at his lowest point, and reaffirmed his status among all the prophets by leading them in prayer.

Examiner note: Weaker answers describe the Night Journey and Ascension as one undifferentiated event (“he went up to heaven”) without separating al-Isra (to Jerusalem) from al-Mi’raj (through the heavens), and often omit the specific detail of prayer being reduced from fifty to five, which is a key, frequently rewarded fact.
PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · J24 S11 · Q14

(a) The night journey and ascension 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. The Prophet travelled by night from Masjid al-Haram to Masjid al-Aqsa on al-Buraq, a swift white creature brought by Jibril, in fulfilment of Surah al-Isra 17:1.
  2. At Masjid al-Aqsa he prayed two rak’at and led the earlier prophets in prayer, establishing his position among them.
  3. He then ascended through the seven heavens, meeting various prophets at different levels, before reaching Sidrat al-Muntaha, beyond which no created being can pass.
  4. Allah commanded fifty daily prayers, reduced to five through Musa’s repeated advice to negotiate, while the reward remained that of fifty prayers.
  5. The Quraysh mocked the account the next morning, but the Prophet’s accurate description of Masjid al-Aqsa (aided by a vision of it appearing before him) and his correct prediction of an arriving caravan served as proof, while Abu Bakr’s immediate belief earned him the title as-Siddiq.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) How this connects Muslims with God” Model answer approach: explain that the five daily prayers, commanded directly during this event without any intermediary revelation process, form the primary regular means by which ordinary Muslims maintain a direct connection with Allah; link this to the idea that prayer, established on this extraordinary night, remains the everyday practical expression of that connection for every Muslim.

Examiner note: For part (b), candidates should connect the historical event specifically to salah as the ongoing practice linking Muslims to Allah — answers that discuss only general “closeness to God” without naming the five daily prayers miss the strongest available link and lose marks.
