Persecution of the Weak and the Prophet

3.3 Life in Makkah — Life after Prophethood · torture of the weak, attacks on the Prophet · Bilal, Sumayyah and Yasir, Ammar, Khabbab

When words failed to silence the new message, Quraysh turned to torture — targeting first the poorest and most defenceless believers, then the Prophet himself, in a campaign of cruelty that early Muslims would remember, and be tested by, for the rest of their lives.

□ Predict before you read

Why might the Quraysh have targeted enslaved and poor converts first, rather than immediately attacking wealthier or more protected Muslims like Abu Bakr? Think about who had tribal protection in Makkah’s clan system, and who did not.

Once Islam was preached openly, Quraysh opposition escalated from mockery into physical violence — and the earliest victims were those with the least protection. In Makkah’s clan-based society, a person’s safety depended heavily on their tribe’s willingness to defend them. Enslaved people and the very poor had no such protection, which made them the first targets of organised persecution.

Bilal ibn Rabah

Bilal ibn Rabah al-Habashi, an enslaved convert, belonged to Umayyah ibn Khalaf, one of the most bitter enemies of the new faith. Umayyah dragged Bilal through the streets of Makkah with a rope around his neck and, in the full heat of the desert sun, pinned him beneath massive heated rocks — stones so hot, it was said, that meat could be cooked on them. Despite this torment, Bilal refused to renounce his faith, repeating only one phrase over and over: “Ahad, Ahad” — “One, One,” affirming the oneness of Allah even as he was tortured for it. Bilal survived, was eventually freed (through Abu Bakr, as covered in earlier units), and went on to become the Prophet’s official muezzin, the one chosen to call the adhan — his voice transformed from a cry of suffering into the call to prayer heard by the whole Muslim community.

Sumayyah bint Khayyat and Yasir — the First Martyrs

Yasir and his wife Sumayyah bint Khayyat, along with their son Ammar, were enslaved converts belonging to a hostile Makkan master. Quraysh subjected the whole family to brutal torture in an attempt to force them to recant. Sumayyah was killed by Abu Jahl himself, making her the first martyr (shahida) of Islam. Yasir also died from his torture shortly after. Their deaths were witnessed by their teenage son Ammar, who was forced to watch his parents killed in front of him for refusing to abandon their faith — a detail that makes the family’s story one of the most solemn in the whole unit, and one commonly tested in exam questions on persecution.

Ammar ibn Yasir

Ammar ibn Yasir, only around fifteen years old at the time, was tortured alongside his parents. Overwhelmed by the brutality he had witnessed and endured, he eventually spoke words of disbelief under duress simply to make the torture stop. Distressed, he came to the Prophet fearing he had committed a grave sin. The Prophet reassured him, and a Qur’anic verse (16:106) was later understood to address situations exactly like his — permitting an outward statement of disbelief under compulsion, so long as the heart remains firm in faith. Ammar went on to become one of the most respected and long-serving Companions, eventually dying in battle decades later (37 AH, at the Battle of Siffin) as an old man still serving the Muslim community.

Khabbab ibn al-Aratt

Khabbab ibn al-Aratt belonged to a female slave-owner and was subjected to severe beatings. His owner had heated iron — the kind used for forging swords — pressed directly onto his back, leaving permanent scarring. Decades later, during the caliphate of Umar, Khabbab showed his scarred back as proof of what he had endured for his faith, and was honoured with a position and salary from the state treasury, much of which he shared generously with the needy. Even on his deathbed, tradition holds that Khabbab wept — not from fear of death or from pain, but from concern that the comfort and prosperity he had lived to see might reduce his reward in comparison to companions who had died earlier, before Islam became secure, without ever tasting such ease.

Direct Persecution of the Prophet Himself

The Prophet’s own status as a member of Banu Hashim gave him some protection — Quraysh could not kill him outright without risking a blood feud with his clan, protected as he was for a time by his uncle Abu Talib — but this did not stop repeated attempts at humiliation, mockery, and physical assault.

Mockery was constant: he was branded majnun (madman), sahir (sorcerer), and sha’ir (poet) — labels designed to dismiss his message without engaging with it. Beyond words, the Quraysh subjected him to real physical danger and emotional cruelty.

The Strangling at the Ka’bah

On one occasion, while the Prophet was praying at the Ka’bah, Uqbah ibn Abi Mu’ayt seized the Prophet’s own garment and wound it around his neck, choking him. Abu Bakr rushed to intervene, pulling the attacker away and confronting him directly:

“Are you going to kill a person just because he says, ‘My Lord is Allah’?!” Abu Bakr, defending the Prophet at the Ka’bah

Entrails Thrown During Prostration

In another notorious incident, while the Prophet was prostrating in prayer near the Ka’bah, Uqbah ibn Abi Mu’ayt again targeted him — this time dumping the entrails of a slaughtered camel onto his back while he remained in prostration, so that the weight prevented him from rising. His young daughter Fatimah rushed out to remove the filth from him. After completing his prayer, the Prophet invoked a specific supplication against seven named individuals responsible for the abuse — a supplication tradition holds was answered years later, when all seven men were killed at the Battle of Badr.

Abu Jahl’s Failed Attempt

On a separate occasion, Abu Jahl attempted to step on the Prophet’s neck while he prayed, but was reported to have suddenly retreated in fear, later explaining that he had seen something terrifying — a pit of fire, with wings hovering above it — a moment tradition presents as divine protection extended to the Prophet at a moment of direct physical danger.

His Reply to Abu Talib

As pressure from Quraysh mounted on his uncle Abu Talib to hand the Prophet over or force him to stop preaching, Abu Talib appealed to his nephew to ease the situation. The Prophet’s reply is one of the most quoted lines in the whole seerah, expressing his total resolve to continue his mission regardless of personal cost:

“…even if they placed the sun in my right hand and the moon in my left, [asking me] to abandon this course, until Allah has made it victorious, or I perish therein, I would not abandon it.” The Prophet’s reply to Abu Talib

This single statement is frequently used in exam answers to demonstrate the Prophet’s unwavering commitment to his message under the most severe personal pressure, and it marks the point at which further compromise with Quraysh became impossible.

Persecution of the weak (enslaved converts)

  • Torture aimed at forcing public renunciation of faith
  • Physical torment: heated rocks, beatings, branding with hot iron
  • No tribal protection to shield them
  • Resulted in the first martyrs of Islam (Sumayyah, Yasir)

Persecution of the Prophet himself

  • Combination of mockery, humiliation, and physical assault
  • Partial protection from his clan, Banu Hashim, under Abu Talib
  • Attacks carried out personally by Quraysh leaders (Uqbah, Abu Jahl)
  • Met with firm, unwavering verbal resolve rather than retaliation

□ Where this comes from

The accounts of Bilal, Yasir and Sumayyah, Ammar, Khabbab, and the direct assaults on the Prophet (the strangling, the entrails incident, Abu Jahl’s failed attempt) are drawn from the seerah tradition as recorded in the standard biographical sources. The reference to Ammar’s forced words under torture connects to Surah an-Nahl 16:106, understood by scholars as addressing statements made under compulsion.

□ Elaborate — the power of “Ahad, Ahad”

Bilal’s only words under torture were “Ahad, Ahad” — a single, repeated declaration of Allah’s oneness. In your own words, explain why this specific phrase, rather than any other statement of faith, might have carried particular power and meaning in that moment.

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · N22 W11 · Q14

(a) Persecution faced [by the Prophet] after he began preaching openly 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. The Prophet was mocked and given false labels — majnun (madman), sahir (sorcerer), sha’ir (poet) — to discredit his message.
  2. Uqbah ibn Abi Mu’ayt attempted to strangle him with his own garment while he prayed at the Ka’bah; Abu Bakr intervened to save him, confronting the attacker directly.
  3. Uqbah ibn Abi Mu’ayt also threw camel entrails on the Prophet while he was prostrating in prayer, preventing him from rising until his daughter Fatimah removed the filth.
  4. Abu Jahl attempted to physically harm the Prophet while he prayed but retreated, reportedly frightened by what he saw.
  5. Despite pressure — including from his own uncle Abu Talib to compromise — the Prophet refused to abandon his mission, famously declaring he would not stop even if offered the sun and moon.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) An example of his conduct under persecution Muslims can follow today” Model answer approach: choose one clear example (e.g. his firm but non-violent resolve in his reply to Abu Talib, or his continued patience and prayer even while being physically assaulted) and explain specifically how a Muslim facing hardship or mockery for their faith today could apply that same quality — remaining steadfast without retaliating, keeping trust in Allah, continuing worship despite difficulty.

Examiner note: Candidates often list only the mockery (“they called him names”) without describing a specific physical incident with named individuals (Uqbah ibn Abi Mu’ayt, Abu Jahl). For full marks, include at least one named attacker and one named incident, not just general statements about “persecution.”
PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · N23 W12 · Q14

(a) Persecution faced by early followers of Islam 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Bilal ibn Rabah, enslaved by Umayyah ibn Khalaf, was dragged through the streets and pinned under heated rocks in the desert sun, yet continued to repeat “Ahad, Ahad.”
  2. Sumayyah bint Khayyat was killed by Abu Jahl, becoming the first martyr of Islam; her husband Yasir also died from his torture, making them the first married martyrs.
  3. Their son Ammar ibn Yasir, around 15 years old, was tortured and forced to witness his parents’ deaths, later uttering words of disbelief under extreme duress before being reassured by the Prophet.
  4. Khabbab ibn al-Aratt was beaten by his owner and had hot iron pressed onto his back, leaving permanent scars he displayed decades later.
  5. These early followers had no tribal protection, unlike the Prophet, which is why enslaved and poor converts bore the earliest and most severe physical persecution.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) What their response teaches about facing difficulties” Model answer approach: draw out the theme of sabr (patience) and steadfastness under extreme pressure — that faith should not be abandoned for worldly ease or to avoid suffering, and that Muslims today should show similar patience, trust in Allah, and resilience when facing hardship of any kind, though rarely as severe.

Examiner note: A frequent error is confusing Sumayyah with Khadijah, or muddling which family member died first (Sumayyah, not Yasir, was killed directly by Abu Jahl). Precise names and the correct order of events earn the higher-level marks.
PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · N25 W11 · Q14

(a) Persecutions suffered by new followers of Islam in Makka 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Enslaved and poor converts were the first targeted, since they lacked the tribal protection wealthier Makkans enjoyed.
  2. Bilal ibn Rabah was tortured by his master Umayyah ibn Khalaf with heated rocks in the desert sun, yet remained steadfast, repeating “Ahad, Ahad.”
  3. Sumayyah bint Khayyat was killed by Abu Jahl — the first martyr of Islam — and her husband Yasir died from torture shortly after.
  4. Ammar ibn Yasir, still a teenager, was tortured and witnessed his parents’ deaths, and Khabbab ibn al-Aratt was branded with hot iron by his female owner.
  5. Despite the severity of the torture, none of these early believers permanently renounced their faith, and their steadfastness became an example for later generations of Muslims.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) Lessons about remaining steadfast in faith” Model answer approach: connect the examples directly to the concept of steadfastness (sabr and thabat) — Bilal’s repeated declaration of Allah’s oneness even mid-torture, and Khabbab’s example of gratitude and humility even in later comfort, both model unwavering commitment that a Muslim today can draw on in the face of smaller, everyday tests of faith.

Examiner note: Candidates sometimes only mention Bilal and forget the other three (Sumayyah, Yasir, Ammar, Khabbab); examiners look for a spread of named examples, not repeated detail on a single individual, to access full marks.
