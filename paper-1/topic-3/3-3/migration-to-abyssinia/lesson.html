Migration to Abyssinia

3.3 Life in Makkah — Life after Prophethood · two migrations to a Christian kingdom · the Negus, Ja’far’s speech, Surah Maryam

Facing torture with no end in sight, a small group of Muslims crossed the Red Sea to seek refuge under a Christian king renowned for his justice — the first migration in Islamic history, and a test of whether faith could find safety beyond the borders of Arabia.

□ Predict before you read

If you were advising the earliest Muslims on where to seek refuge from persecution, what qualities would you look for in a destination and its ruler? Consider distance, religion, existing trade links, and the character of the ruler.

By the fifth year of prophethood, persecution of Muslims in Makkah — covered in the previous lesson — had grown severe enough that the Prophet advised some of his followers to leave Arabia altogether. He directed them toward the Christian kingdom of Abyssinia (modern-day Ethiopia/Eritrea), ruled by a king known to the Arabs as the Najashi, or Negus (his personal name is recorded as As’hama ibn Abjar).

Why Abyssinia?

Several reasons made Abyssinia the natural choice for the Prophet’s advice, and are worth remembering as a set for exam answers: a just ruler — the Najashi was known to guarantee freedom of worship in his kingdom and would not persecute or interfere with the Muslims’ religious practice; relative accessibility — the journey required only around a day and a half on foot to reach the port of Jeddah, followed by a sea crossing of roughly five to seven hours, a manageable journey compared to alternatives further afield; existing familiarity — trade relationships already connected Arabia and Abyssinia, so it was not an entirely unknown land to the Makkans making the journey; and religious closeness — as a Christian kingdom, Abyssinia was seen as closer in belief to the Muslims than the polytheist Quraysh, sharing belief in one God, prophets, revelation, and the Hereafter, even while differing on matters of doctrine.

The First Migration

In Rajab of the fifth year of prophethood — only around eighteen months after the Prophet had begun preaching publicly — a first group set out for Abyssinia. This first group numbered around fifteen people: eleven men and four women. Among the most notable were Uthman ibn Affan and his wife Ruqayyah, the Prophet’s own daughter, who led the group, alongside other early converts such as Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf and Zubayr ibn al-Awwam.

That such a small, vulnerable community had to leave their homeland altogether — so soon after the public preaching had even begun — is itself evidence of just how severe persecution had already become for ordinary believers with no tribal protection.

The Second, Larger Migration

A second wave followed, considerably larger than the first: more than eighty Muslims, led by Ja’far ibn Abi Talib, the Prophet’s cousin and the older brother of Ali. This group represented a substantial portion of the entire Muslim community at the time — by some estimates, well over ten percent of Makkah’s total population had, between both migrations, left for Abyssinia. This scale alarmed the Quraysh considerably, since it demonstrated both the growing size of the Muslim community and its ability to organise and act collectively, even under pressure.

The Quraysh Delegation to the Negus

Unwilling to simply let the Muslims settle safely beyond their reach, Quraysh sent two delegates to Abyssinia to demand their return: ’Amr ibn al-’As (not yet a Muslim at this stage) and ’Abdullah ibn Abi Rabi’ah. The two men brought expensive gifts — including fine camel leather — distributing presents to the Negus’s ministers first, in the hope of winning their support before ever making their case to the king himself. They described the Muslim refugees to the Negus as “foolish youths” who had abandoned their ancestors’ religion without properly embracing Christianity either, framing them as a social and religious threat rather than as sincere believers.

Ja’far’s Speech to the Negus’s Court

Summoned to answer the Quraysh delegation’s accusations, Ja’far ibn Abi Talib entered the Negus’s court and — notably — did not prostrate before the king, explaining that Muslims prostrate to Allah alone. He then delivered a speech that is one of the most quoted passages in the whole seerah, describing the state of pre-Islamic Arabia (jahiliyyah, “ignorance”) before contrasting it with what the Prophet’s message had changed:

“…the strong amongst us would eat up and devour the weak…” Ja’far ibn Abi Talib, describing pre-Islamic Makkah to the Negus

Ja’far went on to describe how the Prophet had brought them out of this state through the worship of one God, honesty, kindness to relatives and neighbours, protection for the vulnerable, and an end to practices such as devouring orphans’ wealth and slandering chaste women.

When the Negus asked for a portion of what had been revealed to the Prophet, Ja’far chose to recite from Surah Maryam, the chapter describing the miraculous birth of Jesus (Isa) to the Virgin Mary (Maryam) — a deliberate and strategically wise choice given his Christian audience, since it demonstrated the shared reverence Islam holds for Jesus and his mother.

The Negus’s Verdict

Deeply moved by the recitation, the Negus is reported to have declared:

“I swear by God, this recitation, and the Messages of Moses and Jesus, have sprung forth from the same fountain.” The Negus, on hearing Ja’far’s recitation from Surah Maryam

He refused to hand the Muslims back to the Quraysh delegation, returned their gifts, and granted the Muslim refugees his continued and permanent protection. This decision is one of the clearest turning points in the persecution narrative: it demonstrated to the Muslims — and to the watching Quraysh — that Islam could find recognition and protection even from outside Arabia, and it establishes the Negus as a figure of considerable respect in the seerah, later remembered fondly by the Prophet himself.

First migration (Year 5 of prophethood)

  • Around 15 people: 11 men, 4 women
  • Led by Uthman ibn Affan and Ruqayyah
  • Smaller, more urgent, earlier response to persecution
  • Established Abyssinia as a place of refuge

Second migration (soon after)

  • More than 80 Muslims — a much larger group
  • Led by Ja’far ibn Abi Talib
  • Provoked a direct Quraysh diplomatic mission to reclaim them
  • Resulted in Ja’far’s famous speech and the Negus’s protection

□ Where this comes from

The details in this lesson — numbers of migrants, the Quraysh delegation of ’Amr ibn al-’As and ’Abdullah ibn Abi Rabi’ah, Ja’far’s speech and recitation from Surah Maryam, and the Negus’s response — reflect the seerah tradition as recorded in the standard biographical sources.

PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · N21 W12 · Q14

(a) The migration to Abyssinia by early Muslims 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Facing severe persecution in Makkah, the Prophet advised some followers to migrate to the Christian kingdom of Abyssinia, ruled by a just king known as the Negus (Najashi).
  2. The first migration, in the fifth year of prophethood, involved around fifteen people (eleven men, four women), led by Uthman ibn Affan and his wife Ruqayyah, the Prophet’s daughter.
  3. A second, larger migration followed, of more than eighty Muslims, led by Ja’far ibn Abi Talib.
  4. Alarmed, Quraysh sent ’Amr ibn al-’As and ’Abdullah ibn Abi Rabi’ah with gifts to persuade the Negus to return the Muslims, describing them as foolish youths who had abandoned their ancestral religion.
  5. Ja’far spoke to the Negus’s court, described the change Islam brought from pre-Islamic ignorance, and recited from Surah Maryam about Jesus and Mary; the Negus was moved, refused to hand the Muslims back, and returned the Quraysh’s gifts.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) Lessons from how Muslims were treated there” Model answer approach: highlight the Negus’s justice and hospitality toward people of a different faith, and draw the lesson that Muslims should likewise treat refugees and minorities of other faiths with fairness and protection, and that seeking safety from persecution through legitimate, peaceful means is an established prophetic example.

Examiner note: A common mistake is merging the two migrations into one event with one set of numbers. Examiners expect candidates to distinguish the smaller first migration (Uthman/Ruqayyah, ~15 people) from the larger second migration (Ja’far, ~80+ people) for full marks.
PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · N23 W11 · Q14

(a) The migration of early Muslims to Abyssinia 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Persecution of Muslims in Makkah, especially of the weak and enslaved, led the Prophet to advise emigration to Abyssinia, ruled by the Negus, known for his justice and tolerance.
  2. The first migration (Year 5 of prophethood) numbered around fifteen people, led by Uthman ibn Affan and Ruqayyah.
  3. A larger second migration of over eighty people followed, led by Ja’far ibn Abi Talib, alarming the Quraysh.
  4. Quraysh sent a delegation — ’Amr ibn al-’As and ’Abdullah ibn Abi Rabi’ah — with gifts to have the Muslims returned, accusing them of abandoning their ancestral religion.
  5. Ja’far’s speech to the Negus, and his recitation from Surah Maryam about Jesus and Mary, convinced the Negus to refuse the Quraysh’s demand and protect the Muslims permanently.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) Similarities to Muslim migrations today” Model answer approach: draw parallels between the early Muslims fleeing persecution to seek safety and modern refugees fleeing conflict or persecution to seek asylum in other countries; note similarities such as seeking a place of tolerance, leaving behind homes and possessions, and depending on the hospitality and justice of a host ruler or government.

Examiner note: Candidates should be careful to name the Negus/Najashi and at least one specific leader (Uthman/Ruqayyah or Ja’far) rather than writing generally about “some Muslims left for Africa” — vague answers without names or numbers lose marks against the level descriptors.
