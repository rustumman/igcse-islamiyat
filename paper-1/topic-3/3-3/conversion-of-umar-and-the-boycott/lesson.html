Conversion of Umar and the Boycott

3.3 Life in Makkah — Life after Prophethood · two conversions, one collective punishment · Hamza, Umar, the boycott of Shi’b Abi Talib

A man who set out one morning to kill the Prophet ended that same day as one of his most devoted followers — and his conversion, alongside his cousin Hamza’s, so alarmed the Quraysh that they responded with the harshest collective punishment yet: starving an entire clan into submission.

□ Predict before you read

Why might the conversion of two powerful, respected men — rather than dozens of poorer converts — have provoked such a severe reaction from Quraysh? Think about what tribal strength and reputation meant in Makkah’s society.

By this stage of the Makkan period, Islam had survived years of ridicule and violent persecution of its weaker members. Two dramatic conversions now changed the balance of power within Makkah itself — and provoked the Quraysh’s most severe collective response of the entire Makkan period: the boycott.

The Conversion of Hamza ibn Abdul Muttalib

Hamza ibn Abdul Muttalib, the Prophet’s uncle and a renowned archer and strong man, was not yet a Muslim when, one day, Abu Jahl publicly insulted and cursed the Prophet and his ancestors near the hill of Safa, with unusual severity. Women of Banu Hashim who witnessed the humiliation later taunted Hamza on his return from a hunting trip, asking why he had allowed his nephew to be dishonoured in this way. Enraged, Hamza marched straight to the Ka’bah — still carrying his bow — and struck Abu Jahl across the face, drawing blood, before declaring:

“And I too am a follower of his religion!” Hamza ibn Abdul Muttalib, at the Ka’bah

By his own account, this declaration came from anger rather than settled conviction — Hamza himself was surprised by his own words. That night, he prayed sincerely to Allah, asking for guidance if Islam was true, or death if it was false. The next morning he sought out the Prophet, who spoke with him, and Hamza’s conversion became genuine and lasting. His acceptance of Islam was highly significant: as the highest-ranking Qurayshi convert to date, and Abu Talib’s own brother, Hamza’s conversion considerably strengthened the Muslims’ standing within Makkah’s tribal hierarchy.

The Conversion of Umar ibn al-Khattab

Umar ibn al-Khattab had, until this point, been one of Islam’s fiercest opponents. The Prophet is reported to have prayed: “O Allah, bring glory to Islam through one of these two men who is more beloved to You: either Abu Jahl or Umar ibn al-Khattab.”

Motivated by Abu Jahl’s offer of a substantial reward — a hundred red-and-black camels and a hundred pouches of silver — Umar set out one day with his sword drawn, intending to kill the Prophet himself. On the way, he met Nu’aym ibn Abdillah al-Nahham, a secret convert, who diverted his anger by revealing an unexpected fact: Umar’s own sister, Fatimah bint al-Khattab, and her husband Sa’id ibn Zayd, had themselves secretly become Muslims.

Umar rushed instead to his sister’s house, where he heard the sound of Qur’anic recitation coming from inside. When he pounded on the door demanding entry, the household scrambled — Khabbab, who had been teaching them the recitation, hid, and Fatimah concealed the written pages. When Umar confronted them and demanded to know what he had heard, Fatimah at first denied everything. Umar then struck Sa’id ibn Zayd, but the blow landed instead on Fatimah. Seeing his sister bleeding and undeterred, both siblings openly and boldly declared their faith in front of him.

Shaken by his sister’s courage, Umar asked to see what she had been reciting. Fatimah insisted he first purify himself (perform ghusl), since he remained a mushrik (idol-worshipper) and was not yet fit to touch the Qur’an. After doing so, Umar read from Surah Ta-Ha, and, in the traditional account, “Islam then entered his heart.”

Umar was guided by Sa’id to Dar al-Arqam, the house where the Prophet and his followers gathered secretly. When the sentries at the door saw Umar approaching with his sword still drawn, they warned in alarm: “Ya Rasulullah, Umar is outside with a sword!” Hamza, already present, responded calmly:

“Let him in. For if Allah wants good, he will accept Islam; but if Allah wants other than this, the very sword he is holding will be the end of him.” Hamza ibn Abdul Muttalib, on Umar’s arrival at Dar al-Arqam

The Prophet himself seized Umar by his collar and warned him firmly of the consequences of continuing his hostility. Umar, already transformed by the morning’s events, responded:

“I have come to accept Islam, believe in Allah, believe in you, and testify in the Truth.” Umar ibn al-Khattab

Public Prayer at the Ka’bah

The Prophet’s response to Umar’s conversion was to proclaim “Allahu Akbar” so loudly that the whole gathering — and Makkah beyond it — instantly knew of Umar’s acceptance of Islam. With Hamza and Umar now openly Muslim, roughly forty Muslims were able, for the first time, to pray publicly and visibly at the Ka’bah, with no one daring to attack them. Umar was later given the title al-Faruq (“the one who distinguishes truth from falsehood”) for the clarity his conversion brought to the Muslim cause. The Prophet is recorded as saying of this moment: “When I converted, we all were able to pray in the Haram” — recognising the security Umar’s and Hamza’s strength now provided.

The Boycott of Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib

Alarmed by these conversions — and by the substantial migration of Muslims to Abyssinia already covered earlier in this unit — the Quraysh’s leadership determined that decisive action was needed. When Abu Talib refused to hand the Prophet over to be killed or silenced, Quraysh issued an ultimatum and then a formal, written boycott, hung as a public document inside the Ka’bah itself.

The boycott’s terms were sweeping: no buying or selling with members of Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib, no intermarriage, and no social contact of any kind — applied to the whole of both clans, whether they were Muslim or not, simply for standing by and protecting the Prophet. This collective punishment forced the two clans into self-imposed exile in a narrow valley or quarter of Makkah known as Shi’b Abi Talib, where they lived confined for approximately three years, from around the seventh to the tenth year of prophethood.

Conditions inside the boycotted quarter were severe. Cut off from normal trade, the clans survived on rainwater, what little vegetation they could find, and occasional secret provisions smuggled in by more sympathetic individuals such as Mut’im ibn Adi and Hakim ibn Hizam. According to tradition, Bilal later described just how desperate conditions became: “We began defecating like goats defecate. Our droppings were indistinguishable from the goats’.” The boycott was only partially relieved during the sacred months of pilgrimage, when tribal customs of truce briefly allowed trade to resume.

The End of the Boycott

Two developments finally brought the boycott to an end. First, a severe famine struck Makkah generally — which some in the tradition attributed to the Prophet’s own supplication. Second, and more directly, a small number of more sympathetic Quraysh figures worked to have the boycott document annulled. Hisham ibn Amr and Zuhayr ibn Abi Umayya led the effort, organising public opposition within the Quraysh’s assembly. Zuhayr is recorded as declaring: “For how long are we going to starve our own kith and kin to death? This is an evil thing!” — with Mut’im ibn Adi and Abu al-Bukhturi ibn Hisham joining him, each contradicting Abu Jahl’s insistence that Quraysh remained united behind the boycott.

The decisive moment came when the Prophet told Abu Talib that Allah had revealed to him that the boycott document itself had been eaten by termites (or insects), except for the words “In Your name, O Allah,” with which the document had opened. Abu Talib staked his credibility on this claim, going to the Ka’bah with non-Muslim Quraysh tribesmen present and demanding the sealed document be inspected. When it was opened, only that opening phrase remained intact, exactly as the Prophet had said. Astonished — and, in the tradition, inclined to attribute it to sorcery rather than accept its significance — the assembled Quraysh could not proceed with a document now visibly nullified, and the boycott collapsed. Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib returned to normal life in Makkah after roughly three years of confinement.

□ Common mix-up to avoid

Do not confuse the boycott of Banu Hashim/Banu al-Muttalib (a collective punishment of an entire clan for protecting the Prophet, lasting around 3 years) with the earlier persecution of individual weak converts (Bilal, Sumayyah, etc., covered in the previous lesson). The boycott is a later, larger-scale, clan-wide event triggered specifically by the conversions of Hamza and Umar and by the Abyssinian migrations.

□ Elaborate — tribal loyalty under pressure

The boycott applied to all of Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib, whether Muslim or not, simply because they stood by the Prophet. Explain, in your own words, what this detail reveals about the importance of tribal loyalty and protection in pre-Islamic Makkah’s social structure.

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · J25 S11 · Q14

(a) The boycott of Muslims at Shi’b Abi Talib 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Alarmed by the conversions of Hamza and Umar ibn al-Khattab, and by the growing migration of Muslims to Abyssinia, the Quraysh issued a written boycott against Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib, hung inside the Ka’bah.
  2. The boycott banned all trade, intermarriage, and social contact with members of both clans, whether Muslim or not, forcing them into the valley of Shi’b Abi Talib.
  3. The confinement lasted around three years, roughly from the seventh to the tenth year of prophethood, with conditions of severe hunger relieved only slightly during the sacred pilgrimage months.
  4. Sympathetic figures such as Hisham ibn Amr and Zuhayr ibn Abi Umayya worked within the Quraysh assembly to have the boycott annulled, opposing Abu Jahl’s insistence on unity behind it.
  5. The boycott ended when the document was found to have been eaten by termites except for the opening words “In Your name, O Allah,” as the Prophet had predicted, leaving the Quraysh unable to maintain their position.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) Was this the worst time in the Prophet’s life?” Model answer approach: present a balanced argument — the boycott caused prolonged, severe collective suffering (hunger, isolation) for three years, which could be argued as extremely severe; however, it could be compared with the Year of Sorrow (death of Khadijah and Abu Talib) or the events at Ta’if, which involved profound personal loss and physical violence directed at the Prophet himself. Candidates should reach a reasoned judgement rather than simply describing both without comparison.

Examiner note: Candidates often forget to mention that the boycott applied to non-Muslim members of the two clans as well — this detail (showing it was a tribal, not purely religious, punishment) is frequently rewarded. Also ensure the “termites” detail and the role of Hisham ibn Amr are included, not just “the boycott ended eventually.”
