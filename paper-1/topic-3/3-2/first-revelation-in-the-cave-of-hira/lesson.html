The First Revelation in the Cave of Hira

3.2 Call to Prophethood · Cave of Hira, Ramadan 610 CE · Jibreel, Khadija & Waraqah ibn Nawfal

One night near the end of Ramadan, in the year 610 CE, a forty-year-old merchant who had never learned to read was gripped by an angel inside a bare mountain crevice and commanded to recite — and prophethood began. This is the single most heavily examined event in the whole syllabus: get the sequence, the names, and the exact wording precisely right.

□ Predict before you read

Before you read on: the Prophet Muhammad (pbuh) could not read or write. Write down your own answer to this — why might it matter, as evidence rather than mere biography, that the very first words ever revealed came to a man unable to read them himself? You will meet the examiner’s version of this reasoning later in the lesson.

A night near the end of Ramadan

By the time he approached the age of forty, Muhammad (pbuh) had spent years building the habit of tahannuth described in the previous lesson — withdrawing alone to the Cave of Hira on Jabal al-Nur to reflect, away from the idols and noise of Makkah. It was during one of these retreats, in the last ten days of Ramadan, on what is reported to have been a Monday, in the year later reckoned as 610 CE, that everything changed.

Iqra — a command he could not obey

As he sat alone in the cave, the angel Jibreel appeared to him for the first time and commanded: “Iqra” — recite. Muhammad (pbuh), who had never learned to read, answered honestly that he could not. Jibreel then seized him and pressed him forcefully — hard enough, by the Prophet’s own later description, that he reached the very limit of what he could bear — before releasing him and repeating the same command. The Prophet gave the same honest answer again, and was pressed a second time. Only after the third command and the third forceful embrace did Jibreel finally recite the words himself, for the Prophet to take in.

The words themselves — Surah al-’Alaq 96:1–5

What Jibreel recited became the very first verses ever revealed, the opening of Surah al-’Alaq (Chapter 96): “Recite in the name of your Lord who created – Created man from a clinging substance. Recite, and your Lord is the most Generous - Who taught by the pen – Taught man that which he knew not.” (Qur’an 96:1–5, Sahih International)

Read carefully what these opening words actually establish: before any law, ritual or narrative, the very first thing revealed names God as Creator, and as the one who taught by the pen — the origin of human knowledge and writing itself. A book that would go on to shape a civilisation of scholars and scribes opened with a command to recite, addressed to a man who could not read a single letter.

Why an unlettered Prophet matters

This detail — that Muhammad (pbuh) was ummi, unable to read or write — is not incidental biography; it does real theological work. If the Prophet could not read, he could not have composed the Qur’an’s content himself by drawing on earlier texts, nor could he have polished its language through study. For Muslims, this is part of the case that the Qur’an’s content came from God rather than from any human authorship — a point worth stating explicitly in an exam answer about the significance, not just the events, of the first revelation.

Trembling home to Khadija

The experience left the Prophet shaken to his core, his heart pounding. He hurried home to his wife Khadija bint Khuwaylid and cried, “Cover me! Cover me!” — convinced, in his fear and confusion, that something terrible had happened to him. Khadija wrapped him in a cloak until his trembling passed, and once he was able to speak, he told her everything.

“You are good to your kin” — Khadija’s reassurance

Khadija did not hesitate or ask for time to think. She reassured her husband at once, reasoning not from a guess but from years of firsthand knowledge of exactly who he was: “No, by Allah! Allah will never humiliate you. You are good to your kin, you are truthful in speech, you take on the burden of others, you give money to those who have little, and you are hospitable to your guests.” (Khadija bint Khuwaylid, as narrated by ’A’ishah, Sahih al-Bukhari)

Notice the shape of her reasoning: she does not simply comfort him — she builds an argument. A man of that character, she is saying, is not the kind of man God would abandon or disgrace.

Waraqah confirms it — and warns what is coming

Still needing more than a spouse’s reassurance, Khadija took her husband to her elderly cousin, Waraqah ibn Nawfal — the scholar of earlier scripture introduced in the previous lesson, already primed by years of studying the Gospel to recognise what an untrained observer could not. When the Prophet described what had happened in the cave, Waraqah recognised it immediately: “This is the same Keeper of Secrets that came to Moses.” (Waraqah ibn Nawfal)

He then went further, and his next words turned a moment of religious confirmation into a warning: “I wish I were a young man so that I could help you when your nation ridicules you, persecutes you, and expels you.” (Waraqah ibn Nawfal)

When the Prophet asked whether his own people really would expel him, Waraqah did not soften the answer: “Anyone who came with something similar to what you have brought was treated with hostility.” (Waraqah ibn Nawfal)

Waraqah died not long afterward, before the persecution he had foreseen fully began — but his identification of the experience, and his unflinching prediction of what following it would cost, both matter as evidence: here was an independent, scripturally trained outsider with nothing to gain from flattering the Prophet, recognising the pattern of true prophecy and immediately forecasting the very hardship that an ambitious impostor would never have wished on himself.

Reading the night as evidence, not just narrative

Three people reacted to the same experience in three distinct ways, and together those reactions form part of the Muslim case that the revelation was genuine: the Prophet himself responded with fear and uncertainty, not the confident excitement of a man inventing a claim to power; Khadija reasoned from years of lived knowledge of his honesty; and Waraqah, an outsider trained in earlier scripture, confirmed the experience against an independent standard and predicted its costly consequences. An exam answer that keeps these three strands distinct — rather than compressing them into “everyone believed him” — is doing exactly the kind of AO1 work examiners are looking to reward.

□ Where this comes from

The narrative of the Cave of Hira, the “Iqra” command repeated three times, the revelation of Surah al-’Alaq 96:1–5, Khadija’s reassurance in her own words, and Waraqah ibn Nawfal’s confirmation and warning are drawn from Yasir Qadhi’s Seerah lecture series, episode 010 (“Zayd b. Harithah, Beginning of…”), arqadhi.blogspot.com — which presents this material as part of the same core hadith tradition recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari’s Book of the Beginning of Revelation, Hadith 3, narrated by ’A’ishah. The Qur’anic quotation of Surah al-’Alaq 96:1–5 is quoted verbatim from the Sahih International translation, retrieved via the quran.ai canonical text service — treat this wording as exam-ready.

□ Same event, different question — how Topic 2 tests this night

This exact event — the Cave of Hira, “Iqra,” Khadija, Waraqah — is also tested under Topic 2 of this syllabus (The history and importance of the Qur’an), where questions ask about “the Prophet’s experience of receiving the first revelation” (e.g. J22 s12, J23 s11) or “the different ways revelation was received” (e.g. J21 s11, J23 s12). The facts are the same; what changes is the angle. Topic 2 questions want the mechanics of revelation — how it physically and emotionally felt to the Prophet, and how that first experience compares to other forms revelation later took across his life. Topic 3 questions — like the ones in this Exam Lab — want this same night told as one link in the continuous life-story of the Prophet, in sequence with what came before (birth, marriage) and after (the pause, the second revelation, the start of preaching). Do not answer a Topic 3 “life story” question with a Topic 2 “mechanics of revelation” answer, or vice versa — examiners mark for fit to the question asked, not just factual accuracy.

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · J21 S11 & J22 S11 · Q14

(a) Give an account of the Prophet’s life from birth until revelation. 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. This question covers the Prophet’s entire life story from birth up to the first revelation — the birth-to-marriage portion (his birth, orphaning, upbringing by Abd al-Muttalib and Abu Talib, work as a merchant, marriage to Khadija) belongs to Unit 3.1 and should open the answer; this lesson supplies the closing portion.
  2. By around age 40, the Prophet had developed the habit of tahannuth, withdrawing to the Cave of Hira on Jabal al-Nur for solitary reflection.
  3. In the last ten days of Ramadan, 610 CE, the angel Jibreel appeared to him there for the first time.
  4. Jibreel commanded “Iqra” (recite); the Prophet, unable to read, said so, and was pressed tightly three times before Jibreel recited the opening verses of Surah al-’Alaq (96:1–5) for him.
  5. Terrified and trembling, he returned home and asked Khadija to cover him.
  6. Khadija reassured him, reasoning from his honesty, kindness to relatives, generosity to the poor and hospitality to guests.
  7. She took him to her cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal, a scholar of earlier scripture.
  8. Waraqah identified the visitor as the same angel sent to Musa, confirmed the experience was genuine prophecy, and predicted that the Prophet’s own people would treat him with hostility.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) Explain why the Prophet’s inability to read and write is considered significant.” Model answer approach: because he was ummi (unlettered), he could not have composed the Qur’an’s content himself by drawing on earlier texts or polishing its language through study; this is presented as evidence that the words came from God rather than from human authorship; it also rules out the accusation that he simply copied or reworded scripture he had read; fittingly, the very first revealed verses are themselves about reading, writing and the origin of knowledge.

Examiner note: Weak answers to this question either skip straight to the revelation without the birth-to-marriage context from Unit 3.1, or stop at “he received the Qur’an” without naming Jibreel, the “Iqra” x3 detail, Khadija, and Waraqah by name. Full marks require the complete named sequence, not a vague summary.
