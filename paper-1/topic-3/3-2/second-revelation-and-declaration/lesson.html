The Second Revelation and the Declaration of Prophethood

3.2 Call to Prophethood · the fatrat al-wahy & Surah al-Muddaththir · nubuwwah to risalah

After the overwhelming night in the Cave of Hira, revelation went silent — and the silence itself became a trial. When the angel finally returned, the words he brought did not describe an experience anymore; they gave an order. “Arise and warn” turned a private, terrifying encounter into a lifelong mission.

□ Predict before you read

Before you read on: the first revelation (Surah al-’Alaq) never actually told the Prophet to do anything — it simply happened to him. Guess: what kind of command would need to come next before he could begin actively preaching to anyone? Keep your guess in mind; the second revelation answers exactly this.

Silence after Hira — the fatrat al-wahy

After the extraordinary night described in the previous lesson, revelation did not continue. A gap followed — remembered as the fatrat al-wahy, “the pause in revelation” — during which no further verses came at all. Scholars have disagreed sharply on how long this pause lasted: some reports put it at two or three years, which most scholars have judged far too long to fit the timeline; Ibn Abbas reported it as forty days; Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri described it as “around a month,” roughly forty days or a month and ten days. Whatever its exact length, the pause was long enough to matter deeply to the Prophet himself.

“I was worried for myself” — living through the silence

The Prophet did not experience this gap calmly. In his own words, reflecting on this period, he said he “was worried for myself” — fearing, in his uncertainty, that what had happened to him might not have been genuine after all. He responded by returning again and again to the mountain, wandering the valleys and hills around Makkah, visiting Jabal al-Nur daily, hoping to see Jibreel once more. Each time, there was nothing.

The second encounter

Eventually, while he was out — by most accounts descending the mountain — the Prophet heard his name called. Looking up, he saw “the angel I had seen at Hira”, this time seated on a throne suspended between heaven and earth. One report describes him falling to his knees from the shock of the sight. Overwhelmed once again, he hurried straight home, saying, “Wrap me up.”

The revelation comes at home, not on the mountain

It is worth being precise here, because this detail is easy to get wrong in an exam answer: the actual verses of the second revelation were not spoken to him on the mountainside. According to the fuller account, Jibreel followed him home, and it was there, in Khadija’s house, wrapped in his garment, that the words came.

Surah al-Muddaththir 74:1–7 — “Arise and warn”

What was revealed was the opening of Surah al-Muddaththir (“The One Wrapped Up”): “O you who covers himself [with a garment], Arise and warn And your Lord glorify And your clothing purify And uncleanliness avoid And do not confer favor to acquire more But for your Lord be patient.” (Qur’an 74:1–7, Sahih International)

The contrast with the first revelation is the whole point of this lesson. Surah al-’Alaq described an experience — it gave the Prophet words to recite about God as Creator and Teacher, but no instruction about what to do next. Surah al-Muddaththir gives a direct command: arise, and warn. Purify yourself, keep clear of idols, do not seek worldly reward for the message, and be patient with whatever follows.

From nubuwwah to risalah

This shift has a precise technical name in Islamic scholarship: the movement from nubuwwah (prophethood — simply being chosen to receive revelation) to risalah (messengership — being commanded to actively convey that revelation to others). The night in the Cave of Hira made Muhammad (pbuh) a nabi; the command in Surah al-Muddaththir made him a rasul, with a mission rather than only an experience. For this reason, this second revelation is often described as the true declaration of prophethood — the moment the private encounter turned into a public responsibility.

What this opens the door to

“Arise and warn” is where the instruction begins, but the campaign of preaching itself — the earliest converts, the private, cautious first years of dawah, and the eventual public declaration on Mount Safa — belongs to the next unit in this syllabus (Unit 3.3). What matters for this lesson is the hinge point itself: a man who had merely received something extraordinary in a cave was now, by direct command, someone who had to act on it.

How reception of the Prophet changed

It is worth noting, looking back over both this lesson and the last, how differently the Prophet was treated by Makkan society before and after revelation began. Before Hira, he had been known and respected across Makkah’s clans as al-Amin, “the trustworthy” — a reputation solid enough that rival clans had once accepted his arbitration over who would replace the sacred Black Stone at the Ka’bah. Once revelation began and, in time, the call to abandon idol-worship became public, that same respected reputation did not disappear overnight — but it stopped protecting him from hostility. Waraqah’s warning in the previous lesson, that “anyone who came with something similar to what you have brought was treated with hostility,” proved exactly right: the clans of Makkah, including men who had personally trusted the Prophet’s honesty for years, became his fiercest opponents once his message directly threatened the religious and economic order built around the Ka’bah’s idols.

□ Where this comes from

The details of the fatrat al-wahy (including the differing reports on its length from Ibn Abbas and Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri), the Prophet’s own words of distress during the pause, the second encounter with the angel seated on a throne, and the clarification that the revelation itself came at home rather than on the mountain, are drawn from Yasir Qadhi’s Seerah lecture series, episode 011 (“Second Revelation”), arqadhi.blogspot.com. The Qur’anic quotation of Surah al-Muddaththir 74:1–7 is quoted verbatim from the Sahih International translation, retrieved via the quran.ai canonical text service — treat this wording as exam-ready. The framing of “arise and warn” as the shift from nubuwwah to risalah, and the note on the private preaching phase that follows, draw on the same lecture series’ episode 012 (“Declaration of Prophethood”), which covers the earliest converts and the later public declaration in more detail than this lesson requires — that fuller material belongs to Unit 3.3.

□ Common mix-up to avoid

Students frequently place the revelation of Surah al-Muddaththir on the mountainside, at the moment the Prophet sees the angel on the throne. Be precise: he saw the vision on the mountain, but fled home in fear before the verses themselves were revealed — the actual recitation happened afterward, at home, wrapped in his garment. Getting this sequence right (vision on the mountain → flight home → revelation at home) is the kind of specific detail that separates a strong AO1 answer from a vague one.

PAST PAPER · 0493/12 · N22 W12 · Q14

(a) How did the clans of Makka treat the Prophet before and after he received revelation? 10 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Before revelation, the Prophet was widely known and respected across Makkah’s clans as al-Amin (“the trustworthy”), a reputation strong enough that rival clans accepted his arbitration in placing the Black Stone at the Ka’bah.
  2. The first revelation (Surah al-’Alaq, 96:1–5) came privately in the Cave of Hira and was not yet public knowledge among the clans.
  3. Waraqah ibn Nawfal, upon hearing of the first revelation, predicted that the Prophet’s own people would ridicule, persecute and expel him once the message became known.
  4. After the fatrat al-wahy, the second revelation (Surah al-Muddaththir, 74:1–7) commanded the Prophet to “arise and warn,” beginning the shift from private experience to active mission.
  5. As the message of monotheism began reaching the clans (initially through cautious, private preaching), it directly threatened the religious and economic order built around idol-worship at the Ka’bah.
  6. Clans and individuals who had previously trusted the Prophet’s honesty as al-Amin became hostile once his message called for abandoning the idols they profited from and worshipped.
  7. Waraqah’s warning proved accurate: the shift from respected, trusted merchant to opposed prophet illustrates how reception changed once revelation, and the call built on it, became known.

Part (b) · AO2 — 4 marks

“(b) Explain why the Prophet’s known reputation for honesty (al-Amin) did not protect him from opposition once he began preaching.” Model answer approach: opposition was rooted in the economic and religious threat his message posed to idol-worship at the Ka’bah, not in doubts about his personal honesty; many Makkans could believe he was truthful and still reject the message because accepting it meant abandoning ancestral religion and the trade benefits idol-worship brought to Makkah; this shows that early hostility targeted the content of the message rather than the character of the messenger.

Examiner note: This question is frequently answered as a general character sketch of the Prophet rather than a focused “before and after” comparison. Structure the answer explicitly around the contrast — trusted arbitrator and honest trader before revelation, opposed and persecuted prophet after it became public — rather than listing facts about his life without linking them to that change in treatment.
