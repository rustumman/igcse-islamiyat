Zayd ibn Harithah and the Seekers of Truth

3.2 Call to Prophethood · Zayd ibn Harithah, the hunafa & tahannuth · background to the first revelation

Before a single verse of the Qur’an was ever revealed, two quiet threads were already being woven into the Prophet’s household — a stolen boy who chose loyalty over freedom, and a handful of Makkans who had already given up on the idols. Neither thread is usually the first thing students think to write about “the call to prophethood,” which is exactly why examiners like to test it.

□ Predict before you read

Before you read on: the Prophet Muhammad (pbuh) did not receive his first revelation in a vacuum — his household and his social circle already contained people unusual for pre-Islamic Makkah. Guess: what kind of person might already have been primed to recognise a genuine prophetic experience when it happened, rather than dismiss it as madness or trickery? Keep your guess in mind — the lesson names exactly such a person.

A boy stolen from his family

Zayd ibn Harithah did not begin his life anywhere near Makkah, and he did not begin it enslaved. He was born into the Qahtani (Yemeni) branch of the Arab tribes, the son of Harithah. As a boy of around seven or eight, he was with his mother, Su’da bint Tha’laba, when raiders from her own tribe — seeking revenge in an unrelated tribal conflict against his father’s people — seized him. He was carried off and sold at the Ukkadh marketplace, one of pre-Islamic Arabia’s great trading fairs, for four hundred dirhams, to a Makkan named Hakim ibn Hizam — who happened to be the nephew of Khadija bint Khuwaylid, the woman the Prophet would later marry.

A gift that became a family

Through that family connection, Zayd passed into Khadija’s household. When Khadija married Muhammad (pbuh), she gave Zayd to her husband as part of her household — not knowing, of course, that she was placing into the Prophet’s care a boy who would go on to be remembered as one of the most devoted companions in Islamic history.

Freedom offered, freedom refused

Years later, Zayd’s father Harithah and an uncle traced him to Makkah and came to buy his freedom and take him home. The Prophet’s response was remarkable for a slave-owning society: he refused to simply hand Zayd over as property. Instead, he gave Zayd himself the choice, telling the father plainly: “It is up to him. If he chooses you, I will send him back with no ransom. But if he chooses me, I can never turn away.”

Zayd — by now a young man of about twenty-five — did not hesitate. He answered at once: “I can never choose anyone over you, for you are to me more than a father and an uncle combined.” In front of his own father, Zayd chose to stay.

A name changed by revelation

Moved by this loyalty, the Prophet went to the Hijr Isma’il, the semi-circular area beside the Ka’bah, and declared publicly: “Zayd is a free man, and I have adopted him as my son.” From that day, according to Abdullah ibn Umar, Zayd was known as Zayd ibn Muhammad rather than Zayd ibn Harithah — until, years later, revelation itself corrected the practice. Surah al-Ahzab was revealed instructing: “Call them by [the names of] their fathers; it is more just in the sight of Allah. But if you do not know their fathers - then they are [still] your brothers in religion and those entrusted to you…” (Qur’an 33:5, Sahih International)

After this verse, he was once again known as Zayd ibn Harithah — his adoption remained a bond of love and loyalty, but the Qur’an insisted that lineage itself not be rewritten.

The hunafa: seekers of truth before Islam

Zayd’s story shows one strand of the household the Prophet grew up in. A separate, equally important strand runs through pre-Islamic Makkah itself. Not every Makkan was content with the idol-worship that dominated the Ka’bah and the city’s religious life. A small number of individuals — remembered in the sources as the hunafa (singular: hanif, meaning someone who inclines away from idolatry toward the worship of one God) — had already concluded, independently, that the religion of their forefathers was false, without yet knowing what the true alternative was.

Waraqah ibn Nawfal: a scholar among the seekers

Among this small group was Waraqah ibn Nawfal, an older cousin of Khadija. Waraqah had gone further than most of the hunafa: he had actually converted to Christianity, and had studied earlier scripture directly — he was literate in Hebrew and had studied the Gospel in its earlier language. This detail matters enormously for what comes next in this unit: when the Prophet’s first revelation experience needed an outside, informed opinion, it was this same Waraqah — already primed by years of studying real revealed scripture — who Khadija turned to, and who was able to recognise immediately what an untrained observer could not have.

The Prophet’s own retreat: tahannuth in the Cave of Hira

The household Zayd belonged to, and the wider Makkan circle that included seekers like Waraqah, form the backdrop to a habit the Prophet himself had been building for years by the time he approached the age of forty: tahannuth — periods of solitary retreat and reflection, away from the marketplace, the idols, and the noise of the city. His chosen spot was a modest opening in the rock on Jabal al-Nur (“the Mountain of Light”) just outside Makkah — the Cave of Hira, described in the earliest accounts as barely more than a crevice, with room enough for one person, its mouth naturally facing the direction of the Ka’bah. He would take food and water and stay there for many days at a stretch, returning to the city only to replenish supplies and check on his family, before climbing back up.

Why this backdrop matters

None of this — Zayd’s loyalty, Waraqah’s scriptural learning, the Prophet’s own increasing withdrawal to Hira — was revelation itself. But together they explain why, when the extraordinary finally happened in that cave, the people closest to the Prophet did not simply panic or disbelieve him. A household built on demonstrated loyalty and a social circle that included a genuine scholar of earlier scripture meant that the ground was, in a sense, already prepared to recognise the real thing.

□ A note on the hunafa

The lecture identifies Waraqah ibn Nawfal as one of a small number of known hunafa in Makkah before Islam — individuals who had rejected idol-worship without yet receiving revealed guidance of their own. This lesson names only Waraqah, since he is the hanif whose role is directly confirmed by the source material for this unit; do not invent names of the others in an exam answer unless you have a separately confirmed source for them.
