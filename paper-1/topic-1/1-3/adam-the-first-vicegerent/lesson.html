Adam — The First Vicegerent

1.3 Allah’s Messengers · Paper 1, Topic 1

Before humanity existed at all, the angels asked Allah a pointed question about the very idea of creating them — and Allah’s answer, “I know that which you do not know,” sets up one of the Qur’an’s richest stories about knowledge, pride, and forgiveness.

□ Predict before you read

Before reading on: if you were an angel who only ever obeyed and glorified Allah, and Allah told you He was about to create a being capable of mischief and bloodshed, what would you want to ask? Compare your question with what the angels actually said.

Surah al-Baqarah, verses 30–37, tells the story of Adam’s creation, the test of knowledge he passes that the angels cannot, Iblis’s refusal to honour him, the trial in Paradise, and Adam’s repentance.

“I will place a khalifah on the earth”

Allah informs the angels: “I am going to place a khalifah (successive generations/a vicegerent) on the earth.” The angels ask: “Will You place therein those who will cause corruption and shed blood, while we glorify You with praise and declare Your holiness?” Ibn Kathir explains this was not a challenge to Allah’s decision but a genuine question seeking to understand the wisdom behind it. Allah’s reply — “I know that which you do not know” — points to what the angels could not yet see: that this new creation would include righteous people, prophets, and devoted worshippers.

Adam is taught the names

Allah teaches Adam the names of all things, then asks the angels to name them. They admit: “Glory be to You; we have no knowledge except what You have taught us.” Adam, however, is able to answer. Ibn Kathir reads this as demonstrating Adam’s — and by extension humanity’s — capacity for knowledge as the reason for his honoured status, not physical strength or constant worship.

The prostration, and Iblis’s refusal

Allah then commands the angels to prostrate to Adam (a gesture of honour and greeting, not worship). All obey except Iblis, who refuses out of arrogance, saying, “I am better than him. You created me from fire and him from clay.” Ibn Kathir treats this as the origin of the sin of pride — Iblis’s error was not disbelief in Allah’s existence, but arrogant refusal to submit to Allah’s command and comparison of his own worth to another of Allah’s creations.

Paradise, the test, and the slip

Adam and his wife are placed in Paradise and told they may eat freely from it, except from one tree. Shaytan deceives them into approaching it, causing them both to slip and be sent out of that state.

Words of repentance and forgiveness

Adam receives words from his Lord — traditionally understood as “Rabbana zalamna anfusana wa in lam taghfir lana wa tarhamna la nakunanna minal-khasirin” (“Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves, and if You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will surely be among the losers”) — and Allah accepts his repentance, “for He is the Oft-Returning, the Most Merciful.” Ibn Kathir highlights this as proof that sincere repentance, however serious the sin, opens the door to Allah’s forgiveness.

“They said: ’Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves, and if You do not forgive us and have mercy upon us, we will surely be among the losers.’ Then Adam received words [of inspiration] from his Lord, and He accepted his repentance. Indeed, it is He who is the Accepting of repentance, the Merciful.” Qur’an 2:37 (words also recorded at 7:23)

The five key moments of this passage

1

The angels’ question

Allah announces a khalifah on earth; the angels ask about corruption and bloodshed; Allah answers, “I know what you do not know.”

2

The test of knowledge

Adam names what the angels cannot, showing his distinguishing capacity for knowledge.

3

The prostration and Iblis’s refusal

All angels obey except Iblis, who refuses out of pride, comparing fire to clay.

4

The trial in Paradise

Adam and his wife are deceived by Shaytan into approaching the forbidden tree.

5

Repentance and forgiveness

Adam turns to Allah in sincere repentance and is forgiven.

□ Where this comes from

Based on Tafsir Ibn Kathir’s commentary on Surah al-Baqarah 2:30–37, including his reading of the angels’ question as genuine inquiry (not objection), his treatment of Iblis’s refusal as the origin of the sin of pride, and his explanation of Adam’s words of repentance as a model for sincere tawbah.

□ Elaborate

Iblis and Adam both disobeyed Allah in this story — Iblis by refusing to prostrate, Adam by approaching the forbidden tree — yet the Qur’an treats them very differently afterwards. What is the key difference between their two responses to having disobeyed, and why do you think that difference matters so much in Islamic teaching about sin?

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · Paper 1, Q1

Choose any two of the following passages from the Qur’an. (a) 4 marks · (b) 4 marks

This passage has been offered as one of the three options in: Oct/Nov 2017, May/June 2020, May/June 2023.

Choose any two of the following passages from the Qur’an. (a) Briefly describe the main theme(s) in each passage. [4] (b) Briefly explain the importance of these themes in a Muslim’s life today. [4]

AO1 · part (a) model answer points

  1. Allah’s announcement of a khalifah on earth, and the angels’ question about corruption and bloodshed.
  2. Adam’s knowledge of the names, distinguishing him and showing the value Allah places on knowledge.
  3. Iblis’s arrogant refusal to prostrate, and Adam’s sincere repentance and forgiveness after the trial in Paradise.

AO2 · part (b) — importance today

Importance today — establishes human beings as khalifah (stewards) of the earth, giving a sense of responsibility for how the world is treated; models sincere repentance (tawbah) as the correct response to sin, in contrast with Iblis’s prideful refusal; teaches that knowledge, not just worship or strength, is part of what gives humanity its honoured status.

Examiner note: Candidates often retell the whole story chronologically without clearly stating the underlying theme(s) — examiners want named themes (khilafah/stewardship, the danger of pride, the power of sincere repentance) explicitly identified, not just narrated events.
