Ibrahim’s Search for the Truth

1.3 Allah’s Messengers · Paper 1, Topic 1

Long before he became known as the father of monotheism, Ibrahim (as) reasoned his way to Allah step by step — testing star, moon, and sun in turn, and rejecting each one the moment it set.

Surah al-An’am, verses 75–79, describes Ibrahim’s (Abraham’s) reasoned journey toward recognising the one true Creator, set against a community that worshipped celestial bodies and idols.

Shown the dominion of the heavens and earth

The passage opens with Allah showing Ibrahim “the kingdom of the heavens and the earth” — Ibn Kathir reads this as strengthening Ibrahim’s insight (or certainty, yaqin) so that he could see clearly through the false beliefs of his people.

The star, the moon, the sun

Ibrahim’s people worshipped celestial bodies. Using their own logic against them, Ibrahim looked at a star and said, “This is my Lord” — but when it set, he said, “I do not like things that set.” He then saw the moon rising and said, “This is my Lord” — but when it too set, he said, “If my Lord does not guide me, I will surely be among the astray people.” Finally he saw the sun, larger and brighter, and said, “This is my Lord, this is greater” — but when the sun set as well, he declared to his people:

The declaration of monotheism

“O my people, I am free of what you associate [with Allah]. I have turned my face towards Him Who created the heavens and the earth, being a hanif (a pure monotheist), and I am not of the polytheists.”

Ibn Kathir explains Ibrahim’s reasoning throughout: anything that disappears, sets, or ceases cannot be worthy of worship as “Lord,” because Ibrahim knew “his Lord is Eternal and never ceases.” Each celestial body, however impressive, was created and temporary — disqualifying it from being divine.

A pedagogical argument, not personal confusion

Ibn Kathir stresses this was not genuine confusion on Ibrahim’s part (since he had already been shown the dominion of the heavens and earth) but a deliberate teaching method — leading his people, step by step, using their own assumptions, to see the logical flaw in worshipping anything that rises and sets.

“So when the night covered him, he saw a star. He said, ’This is my Lord.’ But when it set, he said, ’I do not like things that set.’... So when he saw the sun rising, he said, ’This is my Lord; this is greater.’ But when it set, he said, ’O my people, I am free of what you associate. □ Where this comes from

Based on Tafsir Ibn Kathir’s commentary on Surah al-An’am 6:75–79, which explains Ibrahim’s method as deliberate pedagogical argument (not genuine doubt) and grounds his rejection of star, moon, and sun in the reasoning that anything which sets or ceases cannot be the eternal Lord.

□ Elaborate

Ibrahim uses the exact same test — “does it set?” — on all three objects (star, moon, sun) before rejecting each in turn. Why might using one consistent, repeatable test be more convincing to his audience than simply asserting from the start that all three were false gods?

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · Paper 1, Q1

Choose any two of the following passages from the Qur’an. (a) 4 marks · (b) 4 marks

This passage has been offered as one of the three options in: Oct/Nov 2017, May/June 2019, Oct/Nov 2022, May/June 2025.

Choose any two of the following passages from the Qur’an. (a) Briefly describe the main theme(s) in each passage. [4] (b) Briefly explain the importance of these themes in a Muslim’s life today. [4]

AO1 · part (a) model answer points

  1. Ibrahim being shown the dominion of the heavens and earth to strengthen his certainty.
  2. His reasoned rejection of the star, moon, and sun in turn, because each one sets.
  3. His declaration of pure monotheism (hanif) — turning his face to the Creator of the heavens and earth alone.

AO2 · part (b) — importance today

Importance today — models using reason and observation to arrive at and defend Tawheed, not just accepting belief passively; Ibrahim (as) is honoured across Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, so this passage is often used in interfaith contexts to explain the shared root of monotheism; encourages Muslims to actively question and test what deserves worship, rather than following inherited custom without thought.

Examiner note: Candidates sometimes only mention “Ibrahim rejected idols” without walking through the specific star/moon/sun sequence and reasoning (“things that set cannot be Lord”) — examiners reward the specific logical steps, not just the conclusion.
