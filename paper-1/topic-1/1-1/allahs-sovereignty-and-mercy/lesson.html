Allah’s Sovereignty, Majesty, and Mercy

1.1 Allah in Himself · Paper 1, Topic 1

The same verses that describe the heavens as almost tearing apart in awe of Allah’s majesty also describe the angels constantly begging His forgiveness for people on earth — power and mercy held together in a single breath.

Surah ash-Shura, verses 4–5, pair two ideas that might seem to pull in opposite directions: Allah’s overwhelming greatness, and His mercy toward the very creation that so often disobeys Him.

“To Him belongs all that is in the heavens and the earth”

Ibn Kathir explains that verse 4 affirms Allah’s complete sovereignty — “everything exists under His dominion and control.” The verse follows this immediately with two of Allah’s names: Al-’Aliyy (the Most High) and Al-’Azim (the Most Great), underscoring His supreme authority and majesty over everything He owns.

The heavens “almost torn apart” from above

Verse 5 pictures the heavens as “nearly rent asunder from above them.” Ibn Kathir records that scholars read this as an expression of the heavens’ awe before Allah’s power and majesty — even the vast, seemingly indestructible sky is depicted as trembling at what it witnesses of Allah’s greatness.

The angels ask forgiveness for those on earth

Despite — or rather alongside — this overwhelming majesty, the verse continues: the angels glorify their Lord’s praise and ask forgiveness for those on the earth. Ibn Kathir stresses that this is a reminder of Allah’s mercy even as creation trembles before His might: the angels are not asking forgiveness for themselves (they do not sin) but interceding, by Allah’s permission, on behalf of human beings.

Allah is truly the Forgiving, the Merciful

Ibn Kathir notes the passage concludes by reminding believers that Allah is Al-Ghafoor (the All-Forgiving) and Ar-Rahim (the Most Merciful) — and that the Prophet’s own role is only to warn and convey the message; the actual management of every affair, including forgiveness, belongs to Allah alone.

“To Him belongs all that is in the heavens and all that is on the earth. And He is the Most High, the Most Great. The heavens are almost torn apart from above them, and the angels glorify the praises of their Lord and ask forgiveness for those on the earth. Verily, Allah — He is the Oft-Forgiving, the Most Merciful.” Qur’an 42:4–5

□ Where this comes from

Based on Tafsir Ibn Kathir’s commentary on Surah ash-Shura 42:4–5, which reads verse 4 as an affirmation of Allah’s ownership and majesty, and explains the angels’ intercession for people on earth as evidence of Allah’s mercy even alongside His overwhelming greatness.

□ Elaborate

This passage places Allah’s supreme power (verse 4, the heavens trembling) directly next to His mercy (the angels asking forgiveness for humanity). Why do you think the Qur’an so often pairs these two ideas together, rather than presenting Allah’s power and His mercy in separate, unconnected verses?

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · Paper 1, Q1

Choose any two of the following passages from the Qur’an. (a) 4 marks · (b) 4 marks

This passage has been offered as one of the three options in: May/June 2021, May/June 2025.

Choose any two of the following passages from the Qur’an. (a) Briefly describe the main theme(s) in each passage. [4] (b) Briefly explain the importance of these themes in a Muslim’s life today. [4]

AO1 · part (a) model answer points

  1. Allah’s complete ownership and sovereignty over everything in the heavens and earth.
  2. Allah’s majesty, described through the image of the heavens almost being torn apart in awe.
  3. Allah’s mercy — the angels constantly ask forgiveness for people on earth.

AO2 · part (b) — importance today

Importance today — reassures Muslims that however small they feel next to Allah’s majesty, His mercy and forgiveness are just as real and constant (symbolised by the angels’ intercession); encourages hope alongside awe, rather than fear alone, when a Muslim sins and seeks forgiveness.

Examiner note: This is one of the least-tested passages in the last decade (only 2 sessions), so candidates sometimes prepare it thinly. Because it appears less often, examiners note that when it IS set, answers are often weaker and more generic than for high-frequency passages like Ayat al-Kursi — full preparation on this one pays off precisely because it’s under-revised by most candidates.
