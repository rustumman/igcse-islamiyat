Seeking Refuge — Surah an-Nas

1.2 Allah’s Relationship with the Created World · Surah an-Nas 114:1–6

The very last surah of the Qur’an is a short, practical prayer — asking Allah, under three of His names at once, for protection from an enemy no one can see.

Surah an-Nas (114), the final surah of the Qur’an, teaches Muslims to seek refuge in Allah from a specific, named danger: whispered temptation toward evil.

قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ النَّاسِ · مَلِكِ النَّاسِ · إِلَـٰهِ النَّاسِ · مِن شَرِّ الْوَسْوَاسِ الْخَنَّاسِ · الَّذِي يُوَسْوِسُ فِي صُدُورِ النَّاسِ · مِنَ الْجِنَّةِ وَالنَّاسِ “Say: I seek refuge with the Lord of mankind, the King of mankind, the God of mankind, from the evil of the whisperer who withdraws — who whispers in the breasts of mankind — from among the jinn and mankind.” Qur’an 114:1–6 — Arabic given for reference only; the exam assesses the English translation, not Arabic recall

Three names, one request

The surah opens: “Say: I seek refuge with the Lord of mankind, the King of mankind, the God of mankind.” Ibn Kathir explains that these three names — Rabb (Lord/Sustainer), Malik (King/Sovereign), Ilah (God/the One worshipped) — together form the strongest possible basis for seeking protection: the One asked for refuge is simultaneously the One who sustains, the One who rules, and the One who alone deserves worship.

The whisperer who withdraws

The danger named is “the evil of the whisperer who withdraws” (al-waswas al-khannas). Ibn Kathir identifies this as Shaytan, characterised by his method: he whispers, and “when he is obeyed, he withdraws” — but crucially, when a person remembers Allah, Shaytan retreats from the heart. This describes an ongoing, repeated struggle, not a single event.

Whispering into the hearts

The surah specifies this whisperer works “in the breasts of mankind” — targeting the heart and inner thoughts directly, rather than acting through visible force.

From among jinn and mankind

The final verse notes the source of this whispering can be “of the jinn and mankind” — meaning temptation toward evil can come not only from unseen sources (Shaytan) but also from other human beings who encourage wrongdoing.

A hadith on personal struggle

Ibn Kathir records a hadith in which the Prophet (pbuh) explained that every person has an assigned devil-companion (qarin) — but that his own qarin, by Allah’s special aid, had submitted to Islam and only commanded him to do good, illustrating that this whispering, though universal, can be overcome.

Al-Falaq (113) · usually paired with An-Nas

  • Seeks refuge from external harms: the darkness of night, witchcraft, envy
  • Uses one name: “Lord of daybreak”

An-Nas (114)

  • Seeks refuge from an internal, psychological harm: whispered temptation
  • Uses three names together: Lord, King, God of mankind

□ Where this comes from

Based on Tafsir Ibn Kathir’s commentary on Surah an-Nas, including his identification of the “whisperer” as Shaytan and the hadith on every person having an assigned qarin (devil-companion).

□ Elaborate

An-Nas asks for protection using three different names for Allah (Lord, King, God) rather than just one. What do you think each specific name adds to the request — why might “Lord of mankind” alone not be considered enough, requiring “King” and “God” as well?

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · PAPER 1, Q1 · OCT/NOV 2016, MAY/JUNE 2017, MAY/JUNE 2018, OCT/NOV 2018, OCT/NOV 2020, OCT/NOV 2022, MAY/JUNE 2023

Choose any two of the following passages from the Qur’an. (a) Briefly describe the main theme(s) in each passage. 4 marks (b) Briefly explain the importance of these themes in a Muslim’s life today. 4 marks

AO1 · model answer points

  1. Seeking refuge in Allah under three names: Lord, King, and God of mankind.
  2. The specific danger named: the whisperer (Shaytan) who withdraws when Allah is remembered.
  3. The whispering can come from both jinn and mankind, and targets the heart directly.

Part (b) · AO2 — importance today

Widely recited by Muslims for daily protection (e.g. before sleep, when anxious, or facing temptation); reassures Muslims that the struggle against temptation is universal and can be overcome, as shown by the hadith on the Prophet’s own qarin; reminds Muslims that not all harmful influence is supernatural — some comes from other people, encouraging discernment about who one keeps company with.

Mark scheme wording · verbatim (CIE 0493/11 MS, Oct/Nov 2016 & Oct/Nov 2022)

(a) “Sovereignty: God establishes Himself as Master (Rabb), King (Malik) and true God (Ilah). God’s refuge: Only He can help in times of need, in this case from jinn and humans… Control: He created everything so controls everything, even mischief makers. Warning: It’s a warning of those who whisper evil/bad ideas to humans, and then disappear and leave them on their own.” (b) “Through these verses Muslims get to know the kind of evils/mischief they have to be wary of, e.g. jinn, humans or internal whisperings such as jealousy… Reciting this sura with the other ‘qul’s’ is a source of protection.”

Preparation note (study tip, not from an official examiner report): This is one of the two most-tested passages in the topic (7 out of 20 sessions, tied with Al-Alaq). Candidates commonly conflate An-Nas with Al-Falaq (113) — the mark scheme specifically credits the three names (Lord, King, God) and the “whisperer” detail unique to An-Nas, not the content of Al-Falaq.
