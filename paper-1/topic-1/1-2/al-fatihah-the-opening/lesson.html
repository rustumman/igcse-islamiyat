Al-Fatihah — The Opening of Every Prayer

1.2 Allah’s Relationship with the Created World · Paper 1, Topic 1

Every Muslim recites this seven-verse surah at least seventeen times a day in the five daily prayers — and the Prophet (pbuh) said that without it, a prayer simply does not count.

□ Predict before you read

Before reading on: if a surah has to be recited in every single unit of every daily prayer, what kind of content would you expect it to contain — rules and laws, a story, or something else entirely? Write a guess, then check it against Al-Fatihah’s actual content below.

Surah al-Fatihah (“The Opening”) is the first surah of the Qur’an and, despite being only seven verses long, is treated by Muslims as summarising the entire relationship between the worshipper and Allah.

Bismillah — beginning in Allah’s name

The surah opens with “In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.” Ibn Kathir notes scholarly discussion over whether this line is a separate verse of every surah or not, but its content is clear: Ar-Rahman (Most Gracious) describes mercy that reaches all of creation, while Ar-Rahim (Most Merciful) specifically describes the mercy reserved for believers. Ibn Kathir also notes that “Ar-Rahman” is a name exclusive to Allah — when the false prophet Musaylimah tried to claim it for himself, he became permanently known in history as “the Liar.”

Praise, Lordship, and ownership of the Day of Judgement

“Praise be to Allah, Lord of all the worlds” (Rabb al-’Alamin) establishes Allah as Sustainer and Master of everything that exists, not only human beings. The surah then names Him Maliki Yawm ad-Din — Owner/Sovereign of the Day of Judgement — reminding the reader that accountability is certain and that ownership of that Day belongs to Allah alone.

“You alone we worship, You alone we ask for help”

Ibn Kathir explains that this central verse contains two linked declarations: first, that worship belongs to Allah exclusively (a declaration of innocence from shirk), and second, that all real power and help come only from Him (a rejection of relying on one’s own strength or on others). He notes a deliberate shift here from talking *about* Allah in the third person to addressing Him directly — “after the servant praised and thanked Allah, he stands before Him, addressing Him directly.”

The Straight Path

The surah closes by asking Allah to “guide us to the Straight Path” — and defines that path by contrast: the path of those Allah has favoured, not the path of those who earned His anger, nor of those who went astray. Classical tafsir (recorded by Ibn Kathir, citing a hadith in Jami’ at-Tirmidhi) explains “those who earned anger” as people who had knowledge of the truth but did not act on it, and “those who went astray” as people who worship without correct knowledge — traditionally identified with the Jews and Christians respectively, though the principle (knowledge without action; devotion without knowledge) applies more broadly.

Divine reciprocity in prayer

Ibn Kathir cites a hadith in Sahih Muslim in which Allah says the prayer (Al-Fatihah) is divided between Him and His servant: when the servant says “You alone we worship, You alone we ask for help,” Allah says, “This is between Me and My servant, and My servant shall have what he asked.”

“Guide us to the Straight Path — the path of those on whom You have bestowed Your favour, not of those who have earned Your anger, nor of those who have gone astray.” Qur’an 1:6–7

Why Al-Fatihah is called “Umm al-Kitab” (the Mother of the Book)

1

It is compulsory in every unit (rak’ah) of every prayer

a prayer without it is not valid, according to hadith.

2

It summarises the whole Qur’an’s message in seven verses

praise of Allah, His mercy, His sovereignty over Judgement Day, exclusive worship, and a request for guidance.

3

It moves from information about Allah to direct address and personal request

modelling exactly how a Muslim should approach Allah in every prayer.

□ Where this comes from

Based on Tafsir Ibn Kathir’s commentary on Surah al-Fatihah, including his discussion of Ar-Rahman/Ar-Rahim, the hadith in Sahih Muslim on the surah being divided between Allah and His servant, and the identification of “those who earned anger” / “those who went astray” recorded via a hadith in Jami’ at-Tirmidhi.

□ Elaborate

Al-Fatihah asks Allah to keep the worshipper away from two different wrong paths — one belonging to people who had knowledge but didn’t act on it, and one belonging to people who acted with sincerity but without correct knowledge. Which of these two errors do you think is easier to fall into by accident, and why might the Qur’an warn against both, rather than just one?

PAST PAPER · 0493/11 · Paper 1, Q1

Choose any two of the following passages from the Qur’an. (a) 4 marks · (b) 4 marks

This passage has been offered as one of the three options in: May/June 2016, Oct/Nov 2016, Oct/Nov 2021, Oct/Nov 2023.

Choose any two of the following passages from the Qur’an. (a) Briefly describe the main theme(s) in each passage. [4] (b) Briefly explain the importance of these themes in a Muslim’s life today. [4]

AO1 · part (a) model answer points

  1. Praise and thanks to Allah as Lord and Sustainer of all creation (Rabb al-’Alamin).
  2. Allah’s mercy (Ar-Rahman, Ar-Rahim) and His sovereignty over the Day of Judgement.
  3. Exclusive worship of Allah and reliance on Him alone; the request for guidance to the Straight Path.

AO2 · part (b) — importance today

Importance today — recited in every unit of every daily prayer, making it the most-repeated passage in a Muslim’s life; shapes how Muslims begin any act of worship (with praise, then request); the request for the “Straight Path” is a daily reminder to seek guidance and avoid both empty ritual and misguided sincerity.

Examiner note: Candidates frequently list the surah’s content but forget to mention its unique liturgical role (recited in every rak’ah) when explaining “importance in a Muslim’s life today” — this is one of the strongest and most specific points available for this passage.
