## LESSON: Generosity
Lesson slug: generosity

### Lede
*Prisoners of war fed better than their guards. A hundred camels handed, one after another, to men who had been fighting Islam months earlier. A companion who gave away literally everything, keeping nothing back but Allah and His Messenger. This lesson follows the Prophet's generosity from the aftermath of his first battle to the very last campaign of his life — and the community he trained to give the same way.*

### Content

#### Badr: generosity even toward the men who fought him
After the Muslims' unexpected victory at the **Battle of Badr**, the community found itself holding around seventy prisoners of war — men who had marched from Makkah specifically to fight them. What happened next set a pattern that reappears throughout the rest of the Prophet's life. Ransom was set according to what each prisoner could actually afford — the Prophet (pbuh) reportedly knew the men well enough personally to judge roughly what each family could pay, so wealthier captives paid more and poorer ones paid less. Prisoners who had no money and no literacy skills to offer were released without any ransom at all.

#### Freedom in exchange for teaching, not money
For prisoners who could read and write but had no money, the Prophet (pbuh) offered a striking alternative: freedom in exchange for teaching ten children of the **Ansar** how to read and write. This detail is worth remembering precisely for the exam — it turns a wartime ransom system into an investment in the whole community's future literacy, using the skills of former enemies rather than simply extracting money from them.

#### Instructions to treat prisoners with kindness
Beyond the terms of ransom itself, the Prophet (pbuh) directly instructed the Muslims guarding these prisoners: *"I command you to treat them with kindness."* This was not just a formal command — the prisoner **Abu Aziz** later reported that his Ansari guards would give him bread and meat while keeping only dates and water for themselves. Even as prisoners of a battle these men had started, they were, in practice, fed better than the Muslims guarding them. One captive, **Abu Azzah al-Jumahi**, appealed on the grounds of his poverty and the dependants relying on him, and the Prophet freed him outright, asking in return only that he never fight against the Muslims again.

#### Hunayn: winning hearts with wealth, not conquering them by force
Years later, after the **Battle of Hunayn**, the Muslims found themselves with a very large amount of captured wealth — livestock and goods taken from the defeated Hawazin and Thaqif tribes. The Prophet (pbuh) chose to distribute a striking share of it to people who had only recently converted, or had not yet formally converted at all, as gifts intended to soften hearts still uncertain about Islam — a category the tradition calls **al-mu'allafatu qulubuhum**, "those whose hearts are to be reconciled." **Abu Sufyan ibn Harb** and **Safwan ibn Umayyah**, both former opponents, each received one hundred camels; **Uyaynah ibn Hisn** of the Ghatafan and **al-Aqra' ibn Habis** of the Tamim received the same. Safwan, who had not even completed his own conversion at the time, later described the effect on him directly: *"The Prophet was most despised to me. But he continued to give... until he became most beloved to me."*

#### The Ansar's honest complaint — and the Prophet's answer
Not everyone in the Muslim camp was pleased. Some of the younger **Ansar**, the Madinan Muslims who had sheltered the Prophet and fought at his side for years, murmured that they were asked to fight but overlooked when it came to sharing the spoils. When word of this reached the Prophet (pbuh) through **Sa'd ibn Ubadah**, he gathered the Ansar alone and addressed the complaint directly rather than ignoring it. He told them plainly: *"If all of mankind went one direction and the Ansar in another, I would go with the Ansar."* He then explained his reasoning for the unequal gifts: *"I give to some people because I fear for their greed... I don't give to others because I trust what Allah has given in their hearts."* Finally, he reframed what the Ansar had actually received: *"Are you not happy that people go back with sheep and camels... but you go back home with the Messenger of Allah?"* The Ansar wept and answered, *"We are content with Islam and you, ya Rasulullah!"*

#### Reading Hunayn correctly
This episode is important for the exam because it shows generosity used with real strategic purpose, not randomly — winning over people whose loyalty to Islam was still fragile, while trusting the strong faith of the Ansar to need no material reward. It also shows the Prophet (pbuh) handling a legitimate grievance from his own closest supporters with honesty and respect rather than dismissing it.

#### Tabuk: a community that gives, following his own example
By the time of the **Battle of Tabuk**, the Prophet's own habit of generosity had visibly shaped the wider Muslim community. He encouraged donations toward the difficult expedition, promising that whoever financed the hard-pressed army — the **Jaysh al-Usrah**, "Army of Hardship" — would be granted Paradise. **Uthman ibn Affan** responded with an enormous donation of wealth and gold, prompting the Prophet to remark, *"Wallahi, whatever Uthman does after today, it will not harm him."* **Abu Bakr** then gave his contribution, and when asked what he had kept back for his own family, answered simply: *"I left them Allah and His Messenger"* — meaning he had given everything, holding nothing in reserve. It is also recorded that in the six months before Tabuk, the Prophet (pbuh) himself had already distributed the entirety of the wealth captured at Hunayn and Ta'if, keeping nothing back for himself or the treasury — consistent with a broader, well-known description of his habit never to keep money overnight once it came into his hands.
