## LESSON: Pledges of Aqabah and the Hijrah
Lesson slug: pledges-of-aqabah-and-the-hijrah

### Lede
*After thirteen years of rejection in his own city, the Prophet finally found a community willing not just to believe, but to protect him with their own lives — and the journey that followed, hidden in a cave with pursuers standing at its mouth, became the turning point of Islamic history itself.*

### Content
For years, the Prophet had used the annual pilgrimage season at Makkah's fairs to approach visiting tribes, presenting Islam and asking for their protection and support. Most rejected him outright — among those approached without success were **Kindah**, **Banu Kalb**, **Banu Hanifa**, and **Banu Amir ibn Sa'sa'a**. This persistent, patient outreach — repeated year after year despite constant failure — is itself a detail worth remembering for exam answers on the Prophet's perseverance.

#### The Six Men of Khazraj

In the **tenth year of prophethood**, not long after Abu Talib's death, the Prophet encountered a small group from the **Khazraj** tribe of **Yathrib** (later Madinah) at **Aqabah**. Learning they were from Yathrib, and aware that the city had a significant Jewish population already familiar with the concept of an awaited prophet, he asked if they were "the neighbours of the Jews." He then sat with them, explained the teachings of Islam, recited the Qur'an, and taught them **tawhid** (the oneness of Allah) — despite their small numbers and modest social standing. Among these six men were figures who would go on to become prominent, including **As'ad ibn Zurarah**. All six accepted Islam and returned to Yathrib, where they spread the message until, in time, "everybody in the city of Yathrib had heard of the New Message."

#### The First Pledge of Aqabah

The following year — the **eleventh/twelfth year of prophethood** — a larger group of **twelve men** came from Yathrib to meet the Prophet at Aqabah during the pilgrimage season, this time including members of the **Aws** tribe as well as Khazraj, showing the message had begun to spread across both of Yathrib's major tribes. This meeting is known as the **First Pledge of Aqabah**.

The pledge made here is often called the **"Pledge of Women"** (bay'at an-nisa'), because its terms were purely moral and personal rather than military — the same terms later required of women pledging allegiance to the Prophet. The twelve men pledged:

1. **Not to associate partners with Allah** (to uphold tawhid).
2. **Not to steal.**
3. **Not to commit adultery.**
4. **Not to kill their children** (referring to the pre-Islamic practice of infanticide).
5. **Not to slander** or spread false accusations.
6. **To obey the Prophet in all that is good** (ma'ruf).

Crucially, this first pledge involved **no commitment to armed defence or fighting** on the Prophet's behalf — it was a pledge of personal moral conduct and loyalty, not yet a military alliance. After the pledge, the Prophet sent **Mus'ab ibn Umayr**, one of his own Makkan companions, back with the group to Yathrib, to act as the first official teacher and representative of Islam there.

#### Mus'ab ibn Umayr's Mission

Mus'ab's mission in Yathrib proved remarkably successful. Within just a few weeks of his arrival, **around forty people** had converted. Over the course of roughly a year, Mus'ab is credited with converting between **250 and 300 people** in total — a number the tradition describes as rivalling the entire size of the Muslim community remaining in Makkah itself. This rapid growth transformed Yathrib from a city with a handful of interested converts into a genuine stronghold of new Muslims, setting the stage for a far more significant pledge the following year.

#### The Second Pledge of Aqabah

In the **twelfth/thirteenth year of prophethood**, roughly one year before the Hijrah, a much larger delegation came for the pilgrimage season: **around seventy-three men and two women**, by far the largest group yet to formally commit to the Prophet. This meeting, held again at Aqabah, is known as the **Second Pledge of Aqabah**, or the **"Pledge of War"** (bay'at al-harb), because — unlike the first pledge — it explicitly included a commitment to fight in the Prophet's defence.

The terms of this pledge, as recorded in the tradition, were considerably more demanding than the first:

> "You must give the bay'ah that you-hear-and-you-obey in times of difficulty and ease, and that you spend [of your money] in the Way of Allah, and that you command the good and forbid the evil, and that you speak the truth no matter what the consequences, and that you help me once I come to Yathrib just like you help your own family."
> — The Prophet, at the Second Pledge of Aqabah

When those present asked what reward they would receive in return for this level of commitment, the Prophet's answer was direct and simple: **"Al-Jannah"** — Paradise. From among the seventy-three men, the Prophet selected **twelve leaders (nuqaba')** to represent and organise the wider community of new Muslims in Yathrib, mirroring the structure of the twelve tribal leaders of the Israelites in earlier prophetic history.

This pledge marked the decisive turning point that made the Hijrah possible: for the first time, the Prophet had a community willing to defend him with force if necessary, not merely to believe privately or offer moral support.

#### Quraysh's Alarm and the Early Emigrations

Once the Prophet permitted it, Muslims in Makkah began emigrating to Yathrib **in small groups, quietly, by ones and twos**, to avoid drawing Quraysh attention and interference.

Not every departure went smoothly. **Abu Salama ibn Abd al-Asad** was the first to attempt an open, public emigration; Quraysh forcibly separated him from his wife **Umm Salama** and their young son, keeping the family apart for around **sixteen months** before Umm Salama was finally able to join him, assisted along the entire journey on foot by a sympathetic non-Muslim, **Uthman ibn Talha**. **Suhayb ar-Rumi** faced obstruction on his own departure and was forced to negotiate his freedom by surrendering his wealth to Quraysh, arriving in Yathrib "with just the clothes on his back" — a sacrifice the Prophet later connected to the Qur'anic description of those who "sell themselves for the sake of Allah." **Umar ibn al-Khattab**, by contrast, coordinated his own emigration alongside **Hisham ibn al-As** and **Ayyash ibn Abi Rabi'ah**; when Ayyash was later tricked by his own brothers into returning to Makkah and imprisoned there, he was eventually rescued, along with Hisham, by **al-Walid ibn al-Walid**.

As emigration continued and the Muslim community in Yathrib grew, the Quraysh became increasingly alarmed at the prospect of losing control over the Prophet and his followers entirely, and of a rival, organised, and armed community establishing itself only days' travel from Makkah. According to the wider seerah tradition, this alarm led Quraysh's leadership to convene at their council house, **Dar an-Nadwa**, where they are said to have settled on a final, drastic plan: to select one man from each clan of Quraysh to strike the Prophet together, so that responsibility for his killing — and the resulting blood-guilt — would be shared across every clan, making retaliation by Banu Hashim against any single clan impossible.

#### Ali's Decoy and the Escape

Warned of the plot, the Prophet arranged for **Ali ibn Abi Talib** — who had lived in his household since childhood — to remain behind, sleeping in the Prophet's own bed, wrapped in his cloak, so that anyone watching the house would believe the Prophet himself was still inside. This bought crucial time. The Prophet then slipped away from Makkah, meeting **Abu Bakr**, who had prepared provisions and two camels in advance for the journey.

#### The Cave of Thawr

Rather than heading directly north toward Yathrib — the route Quraysh search parties would expect — the Prophet and Abu Bakr travelled instead to **Ghar Thawr**, a cave to the **south** of Makkah, roughly two and a half to three hours' journey away, planning to hide there for **three days and nights** until the pursuit had died down.

Quraysh search parties, including **Abu Jahl** and **al-Walid ibn al-Mughira**, reportedly came right to the mouth of the cave during the search. Inside, Abu Bakr grew fearful that they would be discovered, but the Prophet reassured him with words later confirmed in the Qur'an itself:

> "...when they were in the cave and he [Muhammad] said to his companion, 'Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us.' And Allah sent down His tranquility upon him and supported him with soldiers you did not see..."
> — Surah at-Tawbah 9:40

Tradition also records — though with varying strength of narration — that a spider had spun a web across the cave's entrance and that a dove had nested nearby, details taken by many as further protective signs suggesting the cave had not been recently disturbed, though these particular narrations are noted as carrying weaker chains of transmission than the core Qur'anic account itself.

#### The Journey to Yathrib

After three days, a hired guide, **Abdullah ibn Uraiqit** — notably a non-Muslim at the time, chosen specifically for his expert knowledge of the desert routes — led the Prophet and Abu Bakr toward Yathrib by an unconventional **southern route**, deliberately avoiding the main roads Quraysh would be watching, before turning north toward their destination.

##### Suraqah ibn Malik's Pursuit

Drawn by the bounty Quraysh had placed on the Prophet's capture, **Suraqah ibn Malik**, a tribal leader, tracked and nearly caught up with the travelling party. Each time he drew close, however, his horse stumbled and threw him. After his horse fell a third time, Suraqah — reading this as a sign beyond his control — abandoned the pursuit and instead called out for safe passage and protection, which the Prophet granted, having it recorded in writing. Suraqah later accepted Islam himself, and is remembered in tradition as eventually receiving the royal bracelets of the Persian king Khosrow (Kisra) after later Islamic conquests, fulfilling a prophecy the Prophet is said to have made to him on this very night.

#### Arrival and Welcome

The Prophet's arrival in the outskirts of Yathrib, after the dangers of the journey, marked the effective end of the Makkan period covered across this entire unit and the beginning of the Madinan period of Islamic history. The city itself would soon be renamed **Madinat an-Nabi** ("the City of the Prophet"), later simply **al-Madinah**, in recognition of this arrival. This moment — the Hijrah — is of such foundational importance to Islamic history that it was later chosen by the second caliph, Umar ibn al-Khattab, as the starting point of the Islamic (Hijri) calendar itself, rather than the Prophet's birth or the first revelation.
