## LESSON: Humility and Simplicity
Lesson slug: humility-and-simplicity

### Lede
*By the last years of his life, the Prophet (pbuh) led a growing state stretching across Arabia — and still lived in a small room of unfired brick beside the mosque, mended his own sandals, and asked people not to stand up for him. Power did not change how he lived. That contrast is the whole lesson.*

### Predict Box
Before you read on: think of someone with real power or fame today. Now imagine they still cooked their own meals, fixed their own shoes, and got visibly uncomfortable if a room stood up when they walked in. Write down whether you think that combination is common — and why it might matter that the Prophet (pbuh) is remembered this way.

### Content

#### The Ahl as-Suffa — care for the poorest, at his own doorstep
Attached to the mosque in Madinah was a covered platform known as the **Suffa**. It became home to a group of Muslims — often poor emigrants who had left everything behind in Makkah, with no family, property, or shelter of their own in the new city — who became known as the **Ahl as-Suffa**, "the People of the Bench." Rather than treating them as a burden to be managed at a distance, the Prophet (pbuh) took a direct, personal interest in their welfare: sharing what food was available, checking on them, and involving the wider community in supporting them. This detail matters for the exam because it shows humility expressed not as a personality trait in the abstract, but as concrete, ongoing responsibility toward the poorest people around him — at a time when, as head of the new Muslim community in Madinah, he had every means to distance himself from that work.

#### Mending his own sandals, sewing his own clothes
It is narrated, describing his everyday household conduct, that the Prophet (pbuh) mended his own sandals and sewed his own clothes when they needed repair, rather than assigning the task to a servant — doing, as the tradition puts it, what any ordinary man would do in his own house. Despite being the leader of the Muslim community, he did not treat manual, humble tasks as beneath him. This detail is small on its own, but it is exactly the kind of concrete, specific fact that an exam answer on humility should reach for instead of a vague claim like "he was humble."

#### Disliking exaggerated respect
The Prophet (pbuh) is reported to have actively disliked it when people stood up for him out of exaggerated deference, in the way subjects might rise for a king or emperor. He discouraged this kind of formal, hierarchical display of status, wanting to be approached and treated as one of the people rather than set apart by ceremony. Read this alongside the mending-sandals detail: both point to the same underlying refusal to let his position change his everyday relationships with people.

#### Eating with the poor and his own household without distinction
Consistent with the same pattern, the Prophet (pbuh) is described as sitting and eating together with the poor, with servants, and with ordinary members of his household, without setting himself apart at a separate table or with different food. He also helped with everyday household tasks himself rather than leaving all of it to others. None of this was performance for an audience — it describes the routine, private texture of how he actually lived day to day.

#### A leader's house of unfired brick
Even in his final years, after leading the Muslims through the establishment of a state covering much of Arabia, the Prophet (pbuh) continued to live in simple rooms attached to the side of the mosque in Madinah, built from unfired mud brick with roofing of palm-leaf stalks. He did not commission a palace, a fortified residence, or any of the trappings that would have been entirely available to a leader of his standing by that point. For the exam, this is the detail that most sharply proves the point of the whole lesson: simplicity was not a a stage he passed through on the way to greater status — it was a deliberate, sustained choice, held onto right through to the peak of his authority.

#### Why humility mattered as a form of leadership
Taken together, these details describe a specific *kind* of humility: not shyness or a lack of confidence, but a leader deliberately refusing the usual signs of status — separate treatment, formal deference, a grand residence — while wielding real authority. This is precisely the pairing examiners want candidates to draw out: humility (modesty of manner) and simplicity (modesty of lifestyle) as two sides of the same consistent choice.

### Exam Lab
Past paper reference: N24 w11 · 0493/11 · Q14 (simplicity half)
Sessions seen: November 2024, Session 11 (Window 11)
Question: (a) How did the Prophet (pbuh) show honesty and simplicity? [10] — this lesson supplies the simplicity half of the AO1 content; see the Honesty and Trustworthiness lesson for the honesty half.
AO1 model answer points:
1. Despite leading a growing state, the Prophet (pbuh) continued living in simple rooms of unfired brick with palm-leaf roofing attached to the mosque in Madinah, rather than a grand residence.
2. He mended his own sandals and sewed his own clothes rather than always relying on servants for such everyday tasks.
3. He personally cared for the Ahl as-Suffa, the poor emigrants sheltering at the mosque, rather than delegating their welfare entirely to others.
4. He ate together with the poor, with servants, and with his household without setting himself apart with separate or superior food.
5. He disliked people standing up for him out of exaggerated respect, preferring to be treated as an ordinary member of the community rather than as a figure of formal status.
AO2 part (b), if any: (b) Can Muslims live as simply as the Prophet (pbuh) today? [4] — Model answer approach: yes, in principle — Muslims can avoid excessive luxury, live within their means, and avoid ostentatious display of wealth, following the spirit of the Prophet's example; however, some acknowledge this is harder in a modern consumer-driven society with different expectations around housing, technology, and social pressure; simplicity does not require literal poverty, but rather deliberate restraint and avoiding attachment to material status; small daily choices (not wasting food, doing tasks yourself rather than assuming others should serve you, treating people equally regardless of their wealth) can reflect the same principle even in modern circumstances.
Examiner note: Because this question bundles honesty and simplicity together, an answer that spends most of its length on one quality and only briefly touches the other will lose marks for imbalance — plan for roughly equal coverage of both halves.
