## LESSON: Preaching in Secret and in Public
Lesson slug: preaching-in-secret-and-in-public

### Lede
*For three years, Islam spread through Makkah as an open secret — a quiet current of belief passed between trusted friends and family, until a command from Allah turned whispers into a public declaration that the whole city could no longer ignore.*

### Predict Box
Before you read: why might a new Prophet choose to preach privately at first, rather than announcing his message to the whole of Makkah immediately? Think about the risks of open opposition versus the risks of moving too slowly.

### Content
After the first revelation on Mount Hira (Surah al-'Alaq, 96:1-5), there was a pause in revelation before the command to warn people finally came. What followed was not silence, but a **private phase of dawah (calling people to Islam)** that lasted around three years. The Prophet Muhammad (pbuh) did not hide in secret in the sense of denying his message — the conversions were, in effect, an "open secret" — but he deliberately avoided public confrontation with the Quraysh, choosing instead to approach individuals he trusted.

**Khadijah bint Khuwaylid**, his wife, was the very first person to believe in him, offering him comfort and certainty on the night of the first revelation itself. She was followed by others close to the Prophet's household and circle: **Ali ibn Abi Talib** (his young cousin, raised in his home), **Zayd ibn Harithah** (his freed slave and adopted son), and **Abu Bakr as-Siddiq**, his close friend, whose acceptance carried particular weight because of his standing and character among the Quraysh. Through Abu Bakr's own network of trusted friends, several more prominent men of Makkah accepted Islam in this early period.

Many of the earliest converts, however, came from the poorer and enslaved classes of Makkah — people such as **Ammar ibn Yasir** and **Bilal ibn Rabah**, who had less to lose socially but far more to risk physically, as later lessons in this unit show. By the end of this private phase, roughly **forty people** had accepted Islam — a small number set against the population of Makkah, but the beginning of a community. Of the many thousands of Companions who would eventually follow the Prophet, only a fraction of names have survived in the historical record, yet the tradition holds that from this earliest group came many of Islam's future leaders.

#### The Command to Warn His Nearest Kin

The private phase ended with a specific revelation instructing the Prophet to widen his warning to his own tribe:

> "And warn, [O Muhammad], your closest kindred."
> — Surah ash-Shu'ara 26:214

Acting on this command, the Prophet invited members of his clan, **Banu Hashim** — more than forty adult relatives — to a meal at his house. Ali prepared food and soup for the gathering, and after the Prophet blessed it, the small amount of food satisfied everyone present. Before the Prophet could deliver his message, however, his uncle **Abu Lahab** interrupted and the gathering broke up without the announcement being made.

Days later, the Prophet reconvened the same relatives and addressed them directly: "I am coming as a Messenger from Allah... if you leave your idolatry and turn to Him, then Allah will give you all the good of this world and give you the Jannah in the Next." Of everyone present, only the young **Ali** stood and pledged his support; the rest remained silent or unmoved. This gathering at Banu Hashim marks the transition point of the unit — from calling individuals in private to addressing the Prophet's own tribe as a collective, setting the stage for the wider public declaration.

#### From Private Calling to Public Proclamation

The final and decisive command came in Surah al-Hijr:

> "Then declare what you are commanded and turn away from the polytheists (mushrikun)."
> — Surah al-Hijr 15:94

This verse ended the private phase permanently. The Prophet was now commanded to proclaim his message openly to the whole of Makkah, not merely to his own household and close circle — a far riskier undertaking, since it meant confronting the tribal and religious establishment of the city directly for the first time.

#### The Safa Hill Address

Acting on this command, the Prophet climbed the hill of **Safa**, close to the Ka'bah, and called out to the clans of Quraysh by name, gathering a crowd curious at the unusual summons. He asked them: if he told them an army was massing on the other side of the hill, ready to attack, would they believe him? The Quraysh answered without hesitation that they would — they had never known him to lie, and had long called him **al-Amin**, "the trustworthy one." The Prophet then declared that he was warning them of a punishment far more serious: the punishment of Allah, if they did not abandon idol-worship. This was the moment Islam moved from private conviction to public challenge — witnessed by the very clan leaders who would soon organise against it.

#### Abu Lahab's Curse and Surah al-Masad

Among those present was the Prophet's own uncle, **Abu Lahab**, who responded with open contempt and cursed him publicly rather than answering the warning. He was known to disparage the Prophet to pilgrims visiting Makkah, at times dismissing his own nephew as having "gone crazy." Abu Lahab's hostility, and that of his wife **Umm Jamil**, who carried thorns to scatter in the Prophet's path, became so notorious that Allah revealed an entire short surah condemning them by name:

> "May the hands of Abu Lahab be ruined, and ruined is he. His wealth will not avail him or that which he gained. He will [enter to] burn in a Fire of [blazing] flame, and his wife [as well] — the carrier of firewood. Around her neck is a rope of [twisted] fiber."
> — Surah al-Masad 111:1-5

This is significant for two reasons often tested in exams: it shows that opposition to the Prophet came from within his own family, not only from outsiders; and it is one of the clearest examples in the Qur'an of a named, individual condemnation, revealed during the Prophet's own lifetime — a challenge Abu Lahab could easily have disproved simply by outwardly accepting Islam, which he never did.

#### From Mockery to Organised Opposition

Once the message went public, the Quraysh's response hardened. What began as scattered mockery grew into a **coordinated, tribal-led campaign** against the Prophet and his followers, generally recognised as involving several linked tactics:

1. **Ridicule and mockery** — belittling the Prophet and his followers in public gatherings and poetry.
2. **False accusations** — labelling him a madman, a sorcerer, or a poet to discredit his message without engaging its content.
3. **Disrupting recitation** — making noise and commotion whenever the Qur'an was recited publicly, to drown it out.
4. **Demanding miracles** — challenging the Prophet to produce supernatural proof on demand.
5. **Bribery and offers** — approaching the Prophet with offers of wealth, status, or marriage alliances in exchange for abandoning his message.
6. **Physical violence** — beating and assaulting those who recited the Qur'an or preached publicly.
7. **Coordinated leadership** — Quraysh's chief men, especially **Abu Jahl**, directing and organising the community's response rather than leaving it to individuals.

This shift — from a family-level dispute to a citywide, organised campaign of opposition — is the hinge on which the rest of Unit 3.3 turns: the persecution of the weak, the assault on the Prophet himself, and the eventual migrations to Abyssinia all flow from this moment when Islam became public and Quraysh could no longer ignore it.

### Timeline
- **Year 1 of Prophethood** — First revelation — Surah al-'Alaq (96:1-5) revealed on Mount Hira; Khadijah is the first to believe.
- **Years 1-3 of Prophethood** — Private dawah — Close family and friends convert quietly; around 40 people accept Islam, mostly from trusted circles and the poorer classes.
- **Year 3 of Prophethood (approx.)** — Warning to nearest kin — Surah ash-Shu'ara 26:214 revealed; the Prophet invites Banu Hashim to a meal and calls them to Islam; only Ali responds.
- **Year 3-4 of Prophethood** — Command to proclaim openly — Surah al-Hijr 15:94 revealed, ending the private phase.
- **Shortly after** — Safa hill address — The Prophet publicly warns all of Quraysh; Abu Lahab curses him in response.
- **Following this** — Surah al-Masad revealed — Condemns Abu Lahab and his wife by name for their hostility.
- **From this point** — Organised Quraysh opposition begins — Mockery, false accusations, disrupted recitation, bribery attempts, and violence become systematic rather than isolated.

Question: (a) How did the Prophet begin openly calling Makkans to Islam, and how did they react? [10]
AO1 model answer points:
1. After private preaching to family and close friends for around three years, the Prophet was commanded in Surah al-Hijr (15:94) to "declare what you are commanded" — to proclaim Islam openly rather than privately.
2. He climbed the hill of Safa and called the clans of Quraysh together, asking whether they would believe him if he warned of an army approaching — they confirmed they would, since he was known as al-Amin (the trustworthy). He then warned them of Allah's punishment if they did not give up idol worship.
3. Most Quraysh responded with disbelief, ridicule and mockery rather than acceptance; some accused him of being a magician, a poet, or mad.
4. His own uncle, Abu Lahab, publicly cursed him and rejected the message on the spot, later becoming a byword for hostility to Islam — condemned by name in Surah al-Masad.
5. Opposition quickly became organised and led by senior Quraysh figures such as Abu Jahl, moving from scattered mockery to a deliberate, coordinated campaign (false accusations, disrupting Qur'an recitation, offers of bribery, and eventually violence).
AO2 part (b), if any: Lessons from his reaction to be applied today [4] — Model-answer approach: identify qualities the Prophet showed (patience, courage in speaking the truth publicly despite guaranteed opposition, continuing to call people with wisdom rather than anger, not repaying insult with insult) and explain how a Muslim today could apply each one when facing mockery or rejection for their faith — e.g. remaining calm and dignified under ridicule, not abandoning one's principles for social ease, continuing to invite others patiently.
Examiner note: Candidates frequently muddle the Banu Hashim feast with the Safa hill address, or give only "he preached openly" without naming the Safa hill event or the specific reactions (mockery, Abu Lahab's curse). For full marks, name the location (Safa), describe the content of his address, and give at least two distinct reactions from Quraysh.

### Exam Lab
Past paper reference: N22 w12 · 0493/12 · 14
Sessions seen: November 2022 (Windows 12)
Question: (a) How did the clans of Makka treat the Prophet before and after revelation? [10]
AO1 model answer points:
1. Before revelation, the Prophet was respected and trusted by the clans of Makkah, known as al-Amin ("the trustworthy") for his honesty in trade and dealings.
2. In the earliest years after revelation, while his calling remained private, there was no open confrontation — Quraysh were largely unaware of, or indifferent to, a small circle of believers.
3. Once the Prophet began preaching openly (after Surah al-Hijr 15:94) and delivered the Safa hill address, attitudes shifted sharply: mockery, false accusations (madman, sorcerer, poet), and public hostility replaced the earlier respect.
4. Family loyalty broke down too — his own uncle Abu Lahab rejected and cursed him publicly, even though pre-Islamic Arabian custom placed a high value on protecting one's kin.
5. Opposition became increasingly organised across clans, led by figures such as Abu Jahl, showing a complete reversal from the trust he had previously enjoyed.
Examiner note: This question rewards a clear "before / after" contrast structure. Weaker answers describe only the "after" persecution without first establishing what changed from his earlier reputation as al-Amin.
