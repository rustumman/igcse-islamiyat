## LESSON: Marriage to Khadijah
Lesson slug: marriage-to-khadijah

### Lede
*A wealthy businesswoman, a hesitant young trader who couldn't quite believe she meant him, and a marriage that would give the future Prophet his first, staunchest supporter for the next twenty-five years — few episodes in the seerah reward close reading of detail as much as this one.*

### Predict Box

### Content
#### A Reputation Built on Honesty
By his mid-twenties, Muhammad had built a quiet but widely recognised reputation in Makkah — not for wealth or status, which as an orphan he had little of, but for honesty and good character. This reputation is often summarised through the two titles Makkans are said to have given him around this period: **al-Sadiq** ("the Truthful") and **al-Amin** ("the Trustworthy"). One small story captures exactly why: on one occasion he sent someone else to collect wages owed to him rather than collecting them in person, and it was this act that first drew the attention of Khadijah's own sister, who remarked, "I have not seen any man who is more shy, honourable, noble, and chaste in his interactions, than Muhammad" — the first time, tradition records, that Khadijah herself heard of him.

#### Khadijah bint Khuwaylid and the Journey to Syria
**Khadijah bint Khuwaylid** was, by this point, the wealthiest woman in Makkah, a successful and independent merchant who ran her trading business through **mudarabah** arrangements — hiring capable men to carry her goods to distant markets in return for a share of the profit, rather than trading herself. Despite Muhammad's inexperience in trade compared with Makkah's established merchants, Khadijah chose him specifically because of his honesty, and sent her servant **Maysarah** to accompany him on the journey north to **Bosra**, a marketplace town roughly 100 kilometres beyond Damascus in Syria.

The trip was a clear success. Maysarah returned to Khadijah full of praise for the young trader's "care and concern" and his "honesty in his dealings," and the venture itself yielded "double or triple the profit anyone else made before" for Khadijah. Between the profit and Maysarah's report of his character, Khadijah had every reason to take a closer interest in the man who had led it.

#### The Proposal
According to the sources, it was Khadijah, not Muhammad, who set the marriage in motion. She sent an elderly servant, **Nafisa**, to sound him out. Nafisa asked him directly, "Why don't you get married?" When Muhammad expressed doubt that marriage was within his means, she pressed further: "What if Khadijah wanted to marry you?" His recorded reply captures his genuine surprise at the idea: he fell silent, then said, **"Why would she want me?"**

#### The Marriage
The marriage went ahead with the full formality Makkan custom required. Khadijah's uncle, **Amr ibn Asad**, acted as her *wali* (marriage guardian), while Muhammad's own uncle, **Abu Talib**, delivered the wedding address (*khutbah*), praising his nephew's "manners, nobility, and lineage." The agreed **mahr** (dowry) was **twelve uqiyyah of silver** — roughly the equivalent of $400 in later estimates. Muhammad was **twenty-five years old**. Khadijah's age is traditionally given as **forty**, though this is disputed among scholars — al-Bayhaqi, Ibn Kathir and Ibn Ishaq are among those who have suggested she was closer to **twenty-eight**, an age that would fit more naturally with the six children the couple went on to have.

Those children were: **al-Qasim** (who died in infancy), **Zaynab**, **Ruqayyah**, **Umm Kulthum**, **Fatimah**, and **Abdullah** (who also died in infancy) — four daughters who survived to adulthood and two sons who did not. Fatimah in particular would go on to play a major role in later Islamic history as the wife of Ali ibn Abi Talib.

#### A Marriage of Equals
Khadijah was Muhammad's only wife for the entirety of their **twenty-five years** of marriage, until her death — a fact worth noting given how his marital life is often mischaracterised in exam answers that assume plural marriage from the start. She would go on to be the very first person to believe in his message when revelation began, using her own wealth and status to support and protect him at the moment he needed it most (covered in Unit 3.2). This marriage was, by every account preserved in the sources, a genuinely successful and loving one, built on the trust her own servant Maysarah had first reported back to her.

### Timeline
- **Mid-20s** — Reputation for honesty (al-Sadiq, al-Amin) — Muhammad becomes known across Makkah for truthfulness and trustworthy character.
- **Age ~24–25** — The Syria trading journey — Khadijah hires him, with her servant Maysarah, to trade her goods in Bosra; the venture is highly profitable.
- **Shortly after** — The proposal — Khadijah sends her servant Nafisa to propose marriage on her behalf.
- **Age 25** — Marriage to Khadijah — Amr ibn Asad acts as wali; Abu Talib delivers the khutbah; mahr of 12 uqiyyah of silver is agreed.
- **Over the next 25 years** — Six children — Al-Qasim, Zaynab, Ruqayyah, Umm Kulthum, Fatimah, and Abdullah; both sons die in infancy.

### Source Box
Label: Common mix-up to avoid
Text: Do not confuse Khadijah's servant **Maysarah**, who accompanied Muhammad on the Syria trading journey and reported back on his conduct, with **Nafisa**, the separate servant Khadijah sent afterwards to raise the idea of marriage. Two different servants, two different roles — trade first, then the marriage proposal.

Question: (a) How did the clans of Makka treat the Prophet before and after he received revelation? [10]
AO1 model answer points:
1. Before revelation, Muhammad was widely respected across Makkah's clans and known by the titles al-Sadiq ("the Truthful") and al-Amin ("the Trustworthy") for his honest dealings.
2. This trust led directly to Khadijah, a respected and wealthy Makkan, hiring him to trade on her behalf and later marrying him — itself a mark of the esteem in which he was held.
3. He was trusted to help resolve the dispute over the Black Stone during the rebuilding of the Kaabah, showing the clans' confidence in his fairness (see the Rebuilding the Kaabah lesson in this unit).
4. After he received revelation and began preaching, this same trust largely collapsed — most Quraysh clans turned to open hostility, mockery, and eventually persecution of him and his followers (covered fully in later units on the Makkan period).
5. Even so, some Makkans — including members of his own family such as his uncle Abu Talib — continued to protect him, showing the treatment was not uniformly hostile.
AO2 part (b), if any: Why was it significant that the Quraysh still trusted him with their belongings [even after prophethood, despite rejecting his message]? [4] — model approach: it shows the clans' hostility was to his message, not his personal character; his reputation for honesty was strong enough to survive their religious rejection of him, which itself is evidence for his integrity and a point Muslims can use to show his trustworthiness was beyond dispute even to his enemies.
Examiner note: This lesson supplies the "before revelation" half of this question in full detail (titles, the Khadijah trade venture, the Black Stone episode). The "after revelation" half belongs to later units on the Makkan persecution — make sure your revision links both halves together for the full 10 marks.
