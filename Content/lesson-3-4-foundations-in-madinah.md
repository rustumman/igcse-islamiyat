### Content
When the Prophet (pbuh) completed the Hijrah in 622 CE, arriving first at **Quba** on the outskirts of Madinah, he did not go straight to his own lodging. He stayed several days at Quba, and it was here that the very first mosque in Islamic history, **Masjid Quba**, was begun — the Prophet laid the first stone, followed by Abu Bakr and Ali, then the Ansar completed it. The Qur'an is widely understood to describe this mosque in Surah at-Tawbah as one "founded on righteousness [taqwa] from the first day" (9:108).

#### Masjid an-Nabawi and the household of the Prophet
Moving into central Madinah, the Prophet's priority was building the second and far more central mosque, **Masjid an-Nabawi** (the Prophet's Mosque). He personally carried building materials alongside his Companions. Attached to the mosque were built simple rooms for his household — the very first were for **Sawda** and **Aisha**, with further apartments added later for his other wives as the community grew. This physical arrangement — mosque and household side by side — reflected how completely public and private life were bound together in the new city: the mosque functioned not only as a place of prayer but as a courthouse, a classroom, a guesthouse for the poor, and a base for military planning.

The **adhan** (call to prayer) was also instituted in this period. When the Companions debated how to summon people to prayer, the Ansari Companion **Abdullah ibn Zayd** reported a dream in which a man taught him the exact wording of the call. The Prophet confirmed it as a true dream and told **Bilal ibn Rabah** — the freed Abyssinian Companion — "Stand up, O Bilal, for you have the loudest voice," making Bilal Islam's first **muezzin**.

#### The Muhajirun, the Ansar, and the Mu'akhah
The **Muhajirun** (Emigrants from Makkah) had left behind homes, trade, and income. The **Ansar** ("Helpers," the Muslims of Madinah, chiefly from the Aws and Khazraj tribes) had offered them refuge. To weld these two groups into a single community, the Prophet instituted the **Mu'akhah** — a formal pact of brotherhood pairing each Muhajir with an Ansari host. This went beyond hospitality: in the earliest period Muhajirun and their paired Ansari brother could even inherit from one another, a rule later adjusted once ties of blood were re-established as the normal basis of inheritance after Badr. The Mu'akhah broke sharply with pre-Islamic custom, where tribal blood-lineage was everything; here, brotherhood in faith stood in its place.

#### The Constitution (Charter) of Madinah
Perhaps the single most important document of this period is the so-called **Constitution of Madinah** (also called the Charter of Madinah), preserved by the early biographer Ibn Ishaq. It is considered one of the earliest written constitutions in history and set out the terms on which the very different communities of Madinah — Muslims from Quraysh and Yathrib, roughly a dozen named Jewish tribes, and the remaining pagan Arabs — would coexist under one political order.

Its main provisions can be grouped as follows:

- **The Muslims as one ummah.** Muslims of Quraysh and of Yathrib, whatever their tribal origin, were declared "one ummah (community) to the exclusion of all other people" — loyalty was now to faith, not blood.
- **The Jewish tribes as allied communities.** Named Jewish tribes — including **Banu Qaynuqa**, **Banu Nadir**, and **Banu Qurayza** — were recognised as communities alongside the believers, retaining internal legal autonomy (their disputes judged by their own law) and freedom of religious practice, in return for mutual defence: "the Jews will spend [towards war] along with the believers as long as they are at war."
- **A single external authority.** Any serious dispute among the parties to the document was to be referred to "Allah and Muhammad" — establishing the Prophet (pbuh) as the effective head of state and final arbiter for the whole of Madinah, Muslim and non-Muslim alike.
- **Mutual protection and internal peace.** No group could shelter or assist an enemy of Madinah against another party to the constitution; Madinah's inner area was declared a sanctuary (haram); and the document bound everyone to defend the city jointly if attacked.

The Constitution mattered because it turned the Prophet (pbuh) from a religious leader of one community into the acknowledged political head of an entire city-state, and because it shows that the earliest Islamic polity was explicitly pluralist: non-Muslims kept their law, their religion, and a share in government, on condition of loyalty to the collective defence of Madinah. When three of the named Jewish tribes later broke these terms during moments of crisis, the consequences that followed (covered later in this unit) were framed by the Prophet's biographers as punishment for breach of a political contract, not for religious difference.

#### The change of qiblah
For about **sixteen months** after the Hijrah, the Prophet (pbuh) and the Muslims continued to pray facing **Bayt al-Maqdis** (Jerusalem), as they had in Makkah. This meant that in Madinah he had to pray with his back toward the Ka'bah, something that troubled him deeply. He would look up to the sky in supplication, hoping for a new command. Allah then revealed:

> "We have certainly seen the turning of your face, [O Muhammad], toward the heaven, and We will surely turn you to a qiblah with which you will be pleased. So turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Haram."
> — Qur'an 2:144

The direction of prayer changed to the **Ka'bah in Makkah**, in year 2 AH. This was the **first instance of naskh (abrogation)** in Islamic law — an earlier ruling replaced by a later one — and the Qur'an addressed the principle directly: "Whenever We abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We bring forth [one] better than it or similar to it" (2:106). Reactions varied: some Jewish critics in Madinah mocked the change as proof of inconsistency; some pagans took it as evidence the Prophet was not fit to lead prayer toward their sanctuary; sincere Muslims accepted the command as a test of obedience, exactly as the Qur'an says: "We did not make the qiblah which you used to face except that We might make evident who would follow the Messenger from who would turn back on his heels" (2:143).

#### Permission to fight
Throughout the thirteen Makkan years, Muslims had been commanded to endure persecution without retaliating. Only once safely established with a state of their own in Madinah did Allah reveal permission for self-defence:

> "Permission [to fight] has been given to those who are being fought, because they were wronged. And indeed, Allah is competent to give them victory — [they are] those who have been evicted from their homes without right, only because they say, 'Our Lord is Allah.'"
> — Qur'an 22:39–40

This verse marks a hinge point in the seerah: from here on, armed conflict becomes a live possibility, setting up everything that follows in this unit, from Badr onward.
