## LESSON: Battle of Uhud
Lesson slug: battle-of-uhud

### Lede
*A battle the Muslims were winning is nearly lost to fifty archers leaving their post for spoils. Uhud is the seerah's hardest lesson in obedience — and the Qur'an spends sixty verses making sure no one ever forgets it.*

### Predict Box
Before you read: at Badr, the smaller Muslim army won decisively. A year later at Uhud, the Muslims actually had the battle won at one point — and still ended up defeated. What single mistake do you think could turn a winning position into a defeat? Keep your guess in mind as you read about the archers on the hillside.

### Content
Makkah could not let Badr go unanswered. Over the following year, **Abu Sufyan** systematically collected the profits Quraysh merchants had invested in the lost caravan to fund a revenge expedition. By **3 AH (625 CE)**, the Quraysh marched on Madinah with a force of roughly **3,000 men**, 200 horses, and 700 sets of armour — including around two dozen women, among them **Hind bint Utbah**, whose husband's father Utbah had died at Badr, brought along to recite poetry and stiffen the men's resolve.

#### The Muslim response and the hypocrite's withdrawal
The Prophet mustered around **1,000 fighters** — nearly all Muslim men of fighting age at the time. On the march out to the hill of **Uhud**, just outside Madinah, the hypocrite leader **Abdullah ibn Ubayy** withdrew with about **300 of his followers**, claiming "if we had known there would be fighting, we would have gone with you" — a claim the Qur'an exposes as false pretence. This reduced the Muslim force to around **700**, facing an army over four times its size.

#### The archers on the hillside
The Prophet positioned the army with Mount Uhud at its back for natural protection, leaving one flank exposed to cavalry. To guard this gap, he placed **fifty archers** on a hillside — later known as **Jabal ar-Rumat** ("the Archers' Hill") — under **Abdullah ibn Jubayr**, with an order remembered precisely for how absolute it was:

> "Protect us with your arrows, for their horses will never come forth in response to arrows... hold your position, even if you see the birds eating our bodies, and do not leave regardless of whether we are the victors or the losers."

#### Initial Muslim success, and the fatal mistake
The battle opened well for the Muslims. **Ali ibn Abi Talib** defeated the Quraysh champion Talha ibn Abi Talha in single combat, and the Muslim charge broke the Quraysh lines so completely that even the women at the rear fled up the mountainside. All ten successive standard-bearers of the Banu Abd al-Dar clan — a position of high honour — were killed, most by Hamzah, Ali, and Abu Dujana. It looked, for a moment, exactly like Badr again.

But believing the battle already won, most of the fifty archers left their post on the hillside to gather the abandoned spoils of the fleeing Quraysh — directly against the Prophet's explicit order. **Khalid ibn al-Walid** (still a pagan commander at this point, not yet a Muslim) spotted the now-undefended gap and led a cavalry charge around the hill, attacking the Muslims from the rear. The battle was thrown into chaos.

#### Chaos, the rumour, and the Prophet's own wounds
In the confusion, **Mus'ab ibn Umayr** — who resembled the Prophet and wore a similar cloak — was killed, and the enemy soldier Abdullah ibn Qami'a shouted that he had killed Muhammad (pbuh). The false rumour of the Prophet's death spread rapidly through both armies, briefly shattering Muslim morale before word reached the ranks that he was alive.

The Prophet himself was wounded: struck in the face by thrown stones, hit by an arrow that lodged in his cheek and dislodged a tooth, and hit again by a sword blow that drove links of his helmet into his cheekbone. A small circle of Companions — including **Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas**, **Talha ibn Ubaydillah**, and others — fought to protect him at close range, some suffering serious wounds themselves shielding him with their own bodies.

#### The death of Hamzah
Among the Muslim dead was **Hamzah ibn Abdul Muttalib**, the Prophet's uncle and one of Islam's most celebrated warriors. He was killed by **Wahshi**, an enslaved man promised his freedom by his owner Jubayr ibn Mut'im in revenge for a relative Hamzah had killed earlier in the battle; Wahshi speared him from behind while he was engaged with another fighter. In an act of raw vengeance for her own father's death at Badr, **Hind bint Utbah** then mutilated Hamzah's body. The Prophet wept upon finding him and, in his grief, spoke of retaliation in kind — but Allah revealed a corrective verse instructing measured justice (16:126), and the Prophet subsequently prohibited the mutilation of bodies outright, a rule that has stood in Islamic law ever since.

#### Withdrawal and the cost
Eventually the Quraysh, having inflicted heavy losses but unable to press home a final assault on Madinah itself, withdrew toward Makkah. Estimates of Muslim dead range from **65 to about 70 martyrs**; the Quraysh lost around **22**. The next day, the Prophet led a deliberately visible pursuit of 70 volunteers to **Hamra al-Asad**, discouraging any Quraysh thought of turning back for a second attack.

#### The Qur'an's commentary
Roughly sixty verses of **Surah Aal-Imran** reflect directly on Uhud — making it, alongside Badr, one of the most extensively commented-on battles in the entire Qur'an. The central theological point is unmistakable:

> "Muhammad is not but a messenger. [Other] messengers have passed on before him. So if he was to die or be killed, would you turn back on your heels [to unbelief]?"
> — Qur'an 3:144

Two further verses assign responsibility for the setback directly to the archers' disobedience, not to any weakness in Allah's promise:

> "Allah had certainly fulfilled His promise to you when you were killing them by His permission, until [the time] when you lost courage and fell to disputing about the order and disobeyed after He had shown you that which you love."
> — Qur'an 3:152

> "Why [is it that] when a disaster struck you, although you had struck the enemy with one twice as great [at Badr], you said, 'From where is this?' Say, 'It is from yourselves.'"
> — Qur'an 3:165

### Timeline
- **Ramadan 2 AH – 3 AH** — Abu Sufyan raises funds from Quraysh merchants to finance a revenge campaign for Badr.
- **3 AH (625 CE)** — A Quraysh army of about 3,000 marches from Makkah to Madinah under Abu Sufyan.
- **On the march out** — Abdullah ibn Ubayy withdraws with about 300 men, reducing the Muslim force to roughly 700.
- **Battle begins at Uhud** — 50 archers are positioned on Jabal ar-Rumat with strict orders never to leave, whatever the outcome.
- **Initial phase** — The Muslim charge breaks Quraysh lines; all ten Banu Abd al-Dar standard-bearers are killed.
- **Turning point** — Most archers abandon their post for spoils; Khalid ibn al-Walid attacks from the exposed rear.
- **Chaos** — A false rumour of the Prophet's death spreads; he is wounded (a broken tooth, a facial wound).
- **Hamzah's martyrdom** — Killed by Wahshi; his body mutilated by Hind bint Utbah.
- **Withdrawal** — The Quraysh retreat toward Makkah without pressing on to Madinah; roughly 70 Muslims and 22 Quraysh are dead.
- **Aftermath** — The Prophet leads a visible pursuit to Hamra al-Asad the following day, deterring any second attack.

- Around 14–15 Muslim martyrs

Right title: Battle of Uhud (3 AH)

- Around 65–70 Muslim martyrs, including Hamzah

### Source Box
Label: Common mix-up to avoid
Text: Uhud was not a "loss" in the sense of the Muslims being driven from the field entirely, nor was it a Quraysh conquest of Madinah — the Quraysh withdrew and never pressed their advantage. Examiners want you to describe it precisely as a costly setback caused by disobeying an order, distinct from an outright military catastrophe, and distinct from the earlier decisive victory at Badr.

### Elaborate Box
Text: The Prophet's order to the archers was absolute: hold the position "even if you see the birds eating our bodies," regardless of whether the battle looked won or lost. Explain, in your own words, why this kind of unconditional obedience to a leader's strategic instruction matters in warfare — and what the human cost was at Uhud when it was not followed.

### Exam Lab
Past paper reference: J21 s12 · 0493/12 · Q14
Sessions seen: June 2021
Question: (a) Describe the events of the Battle of Uhud (625). [10] (b) What lessons can Muslims learn from the outcome of this battle? [4]
AO1 model answer points:
1. Context: Quraysh revenge force of about 3,000 under Abu Sufyan marched on Madinah in 3 AH; the Muslim force of about 1,000 was reduced to 700 after Abdullah ibn Ubayy's withdrawal with 300 men.
2. The Prophet stationed 50 archers on a hillside with strict orders never to abandon their position.
3. Initial Muslim success: Ali's single combat victory, the Quraysh retreat, and the killing of all ten Banu Abd al-Dar standard-bearers.
4. Most archers left their post for spoils; Khalid ibn al-Walid attacked from the now-exposed rear, reversing the battle.
5. A false rumour of the Prophet's death spread; he was wounded (broken tooth, facial wounds); Hamzah was martyred and his body mutilated by Hind bint Utbah.
6. The Quraysh withdrew without pressing the advantage; around 70 Muslims and 22 Quraysh died; the Prophet pursued visibly to Hamra al-Asad the next day.
AO2 part (b), if any: What lessons can Muslims learn from the outcome? [4] — model approach: obedience to leadership and instructions even when victory seems assured (the archers' disobedience directly caused the reversal); the danger of greed for worldly gain over following orders; that setbacks test faith but do not disprove it (Qur'an 3:144); and that Allah's help is conditional on human effort and discipline, not automatic.
Examiner note: Weaker answers describe Uhud as a simple "Muslim defeat" without explaining why — the cause (the archers leaving their post) is the single most examinable fact of this battle and must be explicitly stated, with the consequence (Khalid's flanking attack) linked to it.

AO1 model answer points:
1. Same core narrative: causes, numbers, the archers' order, initial success, the archers' departure, Khalid's rear attack, the Prophet's injuries, Hamzah's martyrdom, and the eventual Quraysh withdrawal.

Examiner note: This is a "state and justify" style question — a bare list of possible lessons without picking one and explaining why it is most important will lose marks. Depth on one point beats breadth across several.
