## LESSON: Conquest of Makkah
Lesson slug: conquest-of-makkah

### Lede
*Ten thousand Muslims march on the city that exiled, boycotted, and tried to kill their Prophet for over a decade. He enters with his head bowed so low it nearly touches his saddle — and then forgives almost everyone who ever harmed him. This is the climax of the seerah.*

### Predict Box

### Content
#### The truce is broken
The Treaty of Hudaybiyyah (6 AH) had promised ten years of peace, and allowed tribes to ally with either side. In **8 AH**, the Quraysh-allied tribe **Banu Bakr**, supplied with weapons by the Quraysh themselves, launched a night raid on the **Khuza'ah**, a tribe allied to the Muslims, killing over twenty people at a watering place called al-Watir — a direct breach of the truce. Khuza'ah's chief, **Amr ibn Salim**, travelled to Madinah with forty delegates and appealed to the Prophet through a poem recalling an ancient alliance between their tribes. The Prophet answered decisively: "You shall be helped... may Allah never help me if I do not help the Banu Ka'b [Khuza'ah]."

The Prophet gave the Quraysh **three options**: pay blood-money for the Khuza'ah dead, disavow their alliance with Banu Bakr, or consider the treaty void. **Abu Sufyan** travelled personally to Madinah to try to salvage the situation but was rebuffed even by his own daughter **Umm Habibah**, now the Prophet's wife, who refused to let him sit on the Prophet's bedding as a polytheist. The Quraysh's failure to make amends meant war.

#### The march on Makkah
The Prophet mobilised with extraordinary secrecy — even Abu Bakr and Aisha were not initially told the destination — sending a decoy scouting party north to mislead the Quraysh before revealing the true target the night before departure. The army that assembled was the **largest yet: roughly 10,000**, up from just 300 at Badr eight years earlier, departing Madinah on the 10th of Ramadan and reaching the outskirts of Makkah by the 19th or 20th.

A serious security breach nearly compromised the operation: the Companion **Hatib ibn Abi Balta'ah**, worried for family left unprotected in Makkah, sent a letter warning the Quraysh of the approaching army. Informed by revelation, the Prophet intercepted the letter before it could do any harm and confronted Hatib, who explained his fear for his family rather than any disloyalty. Despite Umar's demand for execution, the Prophet forgave him outright, reminding Umar that Hatib was a veteran of Badr — a moment later addressed directly in Surah al-Mumtahanah (60:1).

As the army neared Makkah, **Abu Sufyan** — Quraysh's own leader, still hesitating — was found by the Prophet's uncle **al-Abbas** and, on witnessing the sheer scale of the approaching force, accepted Islam that same night, admitting: "had there been any other gods besides Allah, I would have seen them help me."

#### Entering the city
The Prophet's instructions were clear: avoid fighting except where actively resisted, aiming for the smallest possible bloodshed. Entry was achieved with almost no combat — a single small skirmish, in which perhaps 13–20 Quraysh were killed, was the only significant resistance. No general battle, no war booty taken, and no land confiscated followed — a deliberate restraint reflecting the sanctity of the Haram (sacred sanctuary) itself. Riding into the city, the Prophet bowed his head so low in humility and gratitude that it nearly touched his camel's saddle, reciting words of praise rather than triumph.

#### Cleansing the Ka'bah
At the Ka'bah, the Prophet performed tawaf and then personally destroyed the **360 idols** surrounding the sanctuary, pointing at each with his staff as it fell, reciting:

> "Truth has come, and falsehood has departed. Indeed is falsehood, [by nature], ever bound to depart."
> — Qur'an 17:81

#### The general amnesty
Standing at the door of the Ka'bah before the assembled Quraysh, the Prophet abolished the pre-Islamic hierarchies of tribe and lineage in a single sentence, declaring all claims of superiority void except the custodianship of the Ka'bah and the provision of water to pilgrims. He then asked the Quraysh directly what they expected of him. When they answered by praising his nobility, he replied with words that echo the Prophet Yusuf's mercy toward his own brothers in the Qur'an:

> "Go, for you are free. There is no blame upon you today."

This sweeping amnesty extended to virtually the entire city that had persecuted the Muslims for over a decade. Only a small handful — reports suggest between six and nine individuals — were excluded, guilty of the gravest offences such as incitement to murder or malicious propaganda against the Prophet and his family. Even most of these were, in the end, also pardoned: **Ikrimah ibn Abi Jahl** converted after a storm at sea convinced him of Allah's sole power; the poetess who had mocked the Prophet was forgiven after seeking pardon; even **Wahshi**, Hamzah's killer at Uhud, was pardoned a year later, though the Prophet asked never to see his face again. Of those originally named, only around four were ultimately executed, for crimes considered beyond pardon, such as incitement to murder.

This act — total military victory followed by near-total forgiveness of a city that had tortured, boycotted, and driven out its own people for over a decade — is among the most exam-significant moments in the entire seerah, frequently the basis for AO2 questions on the Prophet's mercy and character.

#### Aftermath: adhan on the Ka'bah, pledges, and further conversions
**Bilal** was invited to give the adhan from the roof of the Ka'bah itself — a powerful symbolic moment, the formerly enslaved and tortured Companion now calling the whole of Makkah to prayer from its holiest site. The Prophet then took pledges of allegiance at Mount Safa, the very location where his public preaching had begun two decades earlier, closing a twenty-year circle. Women too gave a separate pledge, per Qur'an 60:12, promising not to associate partners with Allah, steal, or commit other wrongs — during which **Hind bint Utbah**, who had mutilated Hamzah's body at Uhud, questioned why women faced different conditions than men, and was herself among those who accepted Islam.

The Prophet remained in Makkah for around **nineteen days**, during which further significant conversions and rulings occurred, including the declaration that "there is no Hijrah [emigration obligation] after the conquest" — since Makkah was no longer hostile territory requiring flight. Surah **an-Nasr** ("Help/Victory," 110), revealed around this time, was understood by Companions such as Ibn Abbas as a subtle sign that the Prophet's earthly mission, and life, was nearing its completion:

> "When the victory of Allah has come and the conquest, and you see the people entering into the religion of Allah in multitudes, then exalt [Him] with praise of your Lord and ask forgiveness of Him."
> — Qur'an 110:1–3

Question: (a) Describe the conquest of Makka in 630. [10] (b) Explain the significance of the conquest for the development of Islam. [4]
AO1 model answer points:
1. Causes: Banu Bakr's attack on Khuza'ah broke the Hudaybiyyah truce; the Prophet's three-option ultimatum to the Quraysh was refused.
2. The march: roughly 10,000 troops departed in secrecy; Hatib's letter was intercepted and forgiven; Abu Sufyan accepted Islam on the eve of entry.
3. Entry: almost bloodless, with only a small skirmish; the Prophet entered with his head bowed in humility.
4. At the Ka'bah: tawaf performed; 360 idols destroyed while reciting Qur'an 17:81.
5. The general amnesty: "Go, for you are free" declared to the Quraysh; only a small handful excluded, most later pardoned too.
6. Aftermath: Bilal's adhan from the Ka'bah; pledges at Mount Safa; roughly 19 days spent in Makkah before further rulings and conversions.
AO2 part (b), if any: Significance for the development of Islam. [4] — model approach: the conquest removed the last major obstacle to Islam's spread in Arabia; it demonstrated the power of mercy over vengeance, encouraging widespread voluntary conversion; it secured control of the Ka'bah, the spiritual centre of Arabia, purified of idolatry; and it triggered the subsequent "Year of Delegations" as tribes across Arabia recognised Islam's dominance.
Examiner note: Candidates frequently describe the conquest as a violent military event — the single most important fact examiners look for is that it was achieved with minimal bloodshed and followed by an almost total amnesty, which is the actual basis of most (b)-style significance questions on this topic.
