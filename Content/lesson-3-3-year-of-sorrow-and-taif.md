## LESSON: Year of Sorrow and Ta'if
Lesson slug: year-of-sorrow-and-taif

### Lede
*In the space of weeks, the Prophet lost the two people who had sheltered him most — his protector and his wife — and when he walked to a neighbouring city hoping for a fresh start, he was driven out bleeding by children throwing stones. This is the darkest stretch of the Makkan period.*

### Content
The **tenth year of prophethood** is remembered in Islamic tradition as **'Aam al-Huzn — the Year of Sorrow**. It brought two devastating losses in rapid succession, followed by one of the most painful public humiliations the Prophet ever endured, at Ta'if.

#### The Death of Abu Talib

**Abu Talib**, the Prophet's uncle, died in **Shawwal of the tenth year of prophethood** — only around five to six weeks after Banu Hashim had returned to normal life following the three-year boycott. As his uncle lay dying, the Prophet urged him: **"say 'la ilaha illa Allah' — one kalimah that I will be able to present as an argument on your behalf [before Allah]."**

Abu Jahl and Abdullah ibn Abi Umayya, however, remained at Abu Talib's bedside and repeatedly pressed him with the question of whether he would abandon the religion of his ancestors, preventing him from ever reciting the shahada. Tradition preserves a moment of private sympathy from Abu Talib toward his nephew's message, in which he is reported to have said: **"Indeed, Muhammad's religion is the best of all religions, and were it not for the fact that people would criticise me, I would have accepted it."** Yet he died still refusing outward conversion, out of fear of social censure and the judgment of his peers. Following his death, a revelation addressed the limits of seeking forgiveness for those who die outside the faith: **"It is not [proper] for the Prophet and those who believe to ask Allah's forgiveness for the mushrikun"** (Surah at-Tawbah 9:113).

Abu Talib's death mattered enormously beyond personal grief: for years, his position as head of Banu Hashim had provided the Prophet with **clan protection** that no individual Quraysh leader dared violate outright. With Abu Talib gone, that protection was suddenly uncertain — a vulnerability that would shape everything that followed in this lesson.

#### The Death of Khadijah

Barely more than a month later — **less than forty days after Abu Talib's death**, on the **10th of Ramadan** — **Khadijah**, the Prophet's wife of twenty-five years and the first person ever to believe in him, also died. The Prophet personally buried her. Those close to him observed that he **"did not see the Prophet (pbuh) smile for months on end"** afterward — a detail that captures, more than any other, the depth of personal loss behind the formal title "Year of Sorrow."

Khadijah's death compounded Abu Talib's in a second way: where Abu Talib had provided tribal protection, Khadijah had provided the Prophet's closest personal companionship, comfort, and unwavering emotional and material support since the very earliest days of revelation. Losing both pillars within weeks of each other left the Prophet more exposed — both socially and personally — than at any previous point in his mission.

#### Intensified Persecution

With Abu Talib's protection gone, the Quraysh's hostility, previously restrained to some degree by fear of Banu Hashim's retaliation, escalated sharply. The tradition records that persecution against the Prophet **"increased... like never before,"** with previously concealed hostility now expressed openly. One notable incident from this period involved **Uqbah ibn Abi Mu'ayt** and several accomplices throwing a dead animal carcass onto the Prophet during prayer — an echo of the entrails incident from earlier in the Makkan period, but now occurring without the deterrent of Abu Talib's living protection.

#### The Journey to Ta'if

Facing this deteriorating situation in Makkah, the Prophet set out on foot for the neighbouring city of **Ta'if**, accompanied by his adopted son and close companion **Zayd ibn Harithah**. Ta'if was a logical choice: it was the nearest major city to Makkah, the two cities had long-standing peace treaties and close relationships, and one of Ta'if's chieftains was even distantly related to the Prophet by marriage. The Prophet travelled on foot deliberately, rather than drawing attention with an animal escort — a detail the tradition uses to illustrate that trusting in Allah does not mean acting recklessly or without a sound plan.

##### Rejection by the Leaders of Thaqif

In Ta'if, the Prophet approached the three brothers who led the dominant tribe of **Thaqif** — **Abd Yalil, Mas'ud, and Habib**, sons of Amr — and presented Islam to them directly. Their responses were dismissive and contemptuous. One mocked him by saying he might as well tear down the very curtains of the Ka'bah if the Prophet's claim were true; another asked sarcastically whether Allah could not have "found anyone better" to send as a prophet; the third refused to even engage seriously, saying that if the Prophet truly were a prophet he was too holy to argue with, and if he were lying, too far beneath his dignity to bother responding to at all. The Prophet, still hoping to avoid further conflict, asked only that they keep his visit discreet from the Quraysh — a request the Thaqif leaders ignored.

##### The Stoning

After remaining in Ta'if for about a week, quietly attempting to speak with ordinary people rather than only the leadership, the Thaqif chiefs sent out a mob — largely street children and slaves — to drive the Prophet out by force. He was pelted with stones until, by tradition, **his sandals became soaked with blood**. Zayd ibn Harithah suffered injuries across his whole body attempting to shield him. The two were finally driven out at a place called **Qarn al-Manazil**, several kilometres outside Ta'if.

##### Shelter in the Orchard, and Addas

Exhausted and bleeding, the Prophet took shelter beneath a tree beside a walled orchard belonging to **Utbah and Shaybah**, sons of Rabi'ah — distant relatives who, watching from a distance, felt a mixture of pity and tribal pride that a kinsman of theirs had been treated so brutally, even though they themselves were not Muslim. They sent their servant, **Addas**, a Christian originally from Nineveh, out to the Prophet with a bowl of grapes.

As the Prophet ate, he said **"Bismillah"** ("in the name of Allah") — a phrase Addas had never heard used before a meal, and it caught his attention. When the Prophet learned Addas was from Nineveh, he responded: **"The city of Yunus ibn Matta [Jonah, son of Matthew]?"** Astonished, Addas asked how the Prophet could possibly know of Yunus, since — in his words — **"nobody in this whole land has ever heard of him."** The Prophet answered: **"How do I not know Yunus? He is my brother; and I am his brother. We are both prophets of Allah."** Addas immediately believed and, in the custom of honouring elders, kissed the Prophet's feet, later telling his masters that **"there is no one on earth who is better than he is, for he told me things only a prophet could know."**

##### The Offer of the Angel of the Mountains

Alone beneath the tree, the Prophet offered a private prayer of profound anguish, preserved in the biographical tradition:

> "O Allah, to You I complain of my weakness, and my lack of resources, and my lowliness before man... As long as You are not angry with me, I don't care [about the difficulty] — except for the fact that Your protection from tribulations is easier for me. And there is no power or change except with You and through You."
> — The Prophet's supplication outside Ta'if

According to the tradition, **Jibril (Gabriel)** then appeared and told him: **"Your Lord has heard what your people have said to you and their rejection of you. And He has sent me with the Angel of the Mountain to put at your disposal."** The **Angel of the Mountains** then offered the Prophet a devastating option: **"O Muhammad, say what you want; I am at your disposal. If you want, I can squeeze this city [Ta'if] in between the two mountains [surrounding it]."**

Despite everything he had just suffered, the Prophet refused, replying:

> "No. Rather, I hope Allah will extract from their progeny those who will eventually worship Him without associating partners with Him."
> — The Prophet, declining the offer of the Angel of the Mountains

This moment — choosing hope for future generations of believers from Ta'if over immediate and total vengeance against those who had just injured him — is treated in the tradition as one of the clearest demonstrations of the Prophet's character as **"a mercy to the worlds,"** and is one of the most frequently cited episodes in exam questions asking for evidence of his mercy or forbearance.

##### The Return to Makkah and Mut'im ibn Adi's Protection

Returning toward Makkah, the Prophet faced a precarious situation: Abu Talib was dead, and he could not safely re-enter the city without a clan willing to extend him protection. He sent messengers to several Quraysh leaders asking for this protection; two declined. **Mut'im ibn Adi**, chief of Banu Nawfal — the same man who had earlier helped smuggle provisions into the boycotted quarter — agreed. He instructed his sons to arm themselves and escort the Prophet safely into Makkah, publicly declaring at the city's edge: **"O people of Makkah! I have given my protection to Muhammad."** Though Mut'im himself never became Muslim, the Prophet held him in high regard for the rest of his life; years later, after the Battle of Badr, the Prophet remarked that had Mut'im still been alive and asked him for even one favour, he would have freed all the prisoners of war without ransom. The Prophet remained under Mut'im's protection for roughly a year and a half to two years, until circumstances changed again with the Pledges of Aqabah.

### Source Box
Label: Common mix-up to avoid
Text: Students sometimes state that Abu Talib "accepted Islam" on his deathbed out of sympathy. The tradition is precise on this point: he expressed private sympathy for the message but died without formally reciting the shahada, prevented by Abu Jahl and Abdullah ibn Abi Umayya. Keep the distinction between sympathy and formal conversion clear in exam answers.
