## LESSON: Forgiveness and Patience
Lesson slug: forgiveness-and-patience

### Lede
*A man stoned until his sandals filled with blood, offered the power to erase the town that did it — and he says no. A conqueror entering the city that exiled, boycotted and tried to kill him for over a decade — and he says "you are free." This lesson is not about a single episode: it is about the same restraint, tested at wildly different scales, across an entire lifetime.*

### Content

#### Why this unit is different
Every other unit in this topic follows the Prophet's life in order — childhood, revelation, Makkah, Madinah. This unit does not. **Forgiveness and patience (sabr)** is a thread that runs through the *whole* seerah, so this lesson deliberately pulls together moments from very different years and places — a lonely road outside Ta'if, and the conquered streets of Makkah itself — because it is the same trait being tested, not the same event being retold. If you have studied the Ta'if journey or the Conquest of Makkah in full elsewhere in this course, you already know the background; here, look specifically at what each moment reveals about his character.

#### Ta'if: stoned by strangers, and the Angel of the Mountains
After the deaths of his wife **Khadijah** and his uncle **Abu Talib** — his two greatest protectors — the Prophet (pbuh) travelled alone with **Zayd ibn Harithah** to the nearby town of **Ta'if**, hoping its leaders might listen to Islam where Makkah had refused. Three brothers who led the town rejected him with open mockery. When word spread, the townspeople turned on him directly: they sent a mob of local people to pelt him with stones and drive him from the town. He was hurt so badly that his sandals filled with blood; Zayd, trying to shield him, was struck and injured from head to toe.

Wounded and exhausted, the Prophet (pbuh) finally reached the shelter of an orchard wall outside the town, belonging to two brothers, **Utbah and Shaybah**, who were not yet Muslim but sent him grapes out of simple human kindness. It was here, in what may be the lowest moment of his entire life, that he made this supplication:

> "O Allah, to You I complain of my weakness, and my lack of resources, and my lowliness before man."

At exactly this point, the angel **Jibreel** appeared, accompanied by the **Angel of the Mountains**, who offered him a choice with real, immediate power behind it: *"If you want, I can squeeze this city in between the two mountains."* One word from the Prophet, and Ta'if would have been physically crushed. He refused, and gave a reason that looked forward rather than back:

> "No. Rather, I hope Allah will extract from their progeny those who will eventually worship Him without associating partners with Him."

#### Reading Ta'if correctly
Notice exactly what is being tested here. This is not forgiveness of an old insult after tempers have cooled — it is a real offer of total, permanent revenge, made to a man who was bleeding, physically at his weakest, and had every human justification to accept it. He turned it down not out of passivity, but because he was thinking generations ahead: not the guilty adults in front of him, but the unborn descendants who might one day become believers. This is the pairing the syllabus wants you to hold together — **patience** (sabr) under the persecution itself, and **forgiveness** in his response to the power to end it.

#### The Conquest of Makkah: forgiving the city that tried to destroy him
Years later, after the Hijrah, after the Quraysh's own violation of the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah forced his hand, the Prophet (pbuh) returned to Makkah at the head of an army large enough to take the city by force. This was the same Makkah that had boycotted his family, tortured his early followers, and driven him out at the cost of his life's safety. By any standard of the time, a conquering leader was entitled to punish his former persecutors severely.

Instead, as the Prophet entered the city, he moved to guarantee safety rather than settle scores. He declared publicly that whoever remained inside the Sacred Mosque, whoever stayed inside their own home, and whoever sheltered in the house of **Abu Sufyan** — the very man who had led military campaigns against the Muslims for years — would be safe. Standing before the people of Makkah once the city was secured, he delivered the words that became the defining statement of the conquest:

> "Go forth, you are free today."

#### What made this remarkable
This was not weakness or indifference — the Prophet (pbuh) had both the military power and every historical precedent of the time to justify mass retribution. He chose an amnesty instead, covering people who had personally fought against him, exiled him, and killed his companions. Read alongside Ta'if, the pattern becomes unmistakable: whether the wrong was done by strangers in a small town or by his own tribe over more than a decade, at the exact moment he held the power to punish, he chose restraint.

### Compare
Left title: Ta'if (persecution by strangers)
Left points:
- Physical violence at its most direct — stoned until bleeding
- The offer of revenge came with supernatural, total power (a mountain crushing a town)
- His reasoning looked to unborn future generations, not present guilt
- A private, near-death low point with almost no witnesses

Right title: Conquest of Makkah (persecution by his own tribe)
Right points:
- Over a decade of sustained persecution: boycott, exile, war
- The power to punish was entirely conventional — a victorious army in a conquered city
- His declaration was public, addressed to the whole city at once
- Even known enemies (Abu Sufyan) were explicitly named as protected

### Exam Lab
Past paper reference: J21 s12 · 0493/12 · Q14
Sessions seen: May/June 2021, Session 12
Question: (a) How did the Prophet (pbuh) show patience and forgiveness? Give examples. [10]
AO1 model answer points:
1. At Ta'if, after being stoned by the townspeople until he bled, the Prophet showed patience in his supplication rather than anger — asking God to note his weakness rather than cursing his attackers.
2. When the Angel of the Mountains offered to crush Ta'if between the two mountains, he refused, showing forgiveness by hoping for future believers among their descendants instead of demanding punishment.
3. He endured years of persecution, mockery, and the boycott of his family in Makkah with patience rather than retaliation, continuing to preach.
4. At the Conquest of Makkah, despite having the military power to punish the Quraysh who had persecuted the Muslims for over a decade, he declared "Go forth, you are free today."
5. He specifically guaranteed the safety of the household of Abu Sufyan, a former enemy commander, alongside all who stayed in their homes or in the Sacred Mosque.
6. He did not order revenge killings against the Quraysh leaders who had fought and killed Muslims, choosing amnesty over retribution.
AO2 part (b), if any: (b) Why should Muslims show patience in everyday life? [4] — Model answer approach: patience helps Muslims control anger and avoid actions they would regret; it follows the Prophet's own example, so imitating it is itself an act of following the Sunnah; it strengthens character and self-discipline, which the Qur'an and hadith link to closeness to God; it prevents minor conflicts (with family, at school, at work) from escalating into lasting harm or broken relationships.
Examiner note: Many students describe Ta'if or the Conquest of Makkah in vague terms ("he was very patient") without specific, checkable detail. Full marks require named facts: the Angel of the Mountains' exact offer and his refusal, or the "Go forth, you are free today" declaration and the naming of Abu Sufyan's house — general statements without such detail lose marks even if broadly accurate.

### Exam Lab
Past paper reference: J23 s12 · 0493/12 · Q14
Sessions seen: June 2023, Session 12
Question: (a) Describe the Prophet's (pbuh) patience and self-restraint while he was in Makka. [10]
AO1 model answer points:
1. The Makkan period covers persecution before the Ta'if journey: mockery, physical harassment of the Prophet and his followers, and the social and economic boycott of his family, all endured without retaliation.
2. On the journey to Ta'if, he sought support peacefully rather than through force, even after being rejected by the town's leaders.
3. When the mob of Ta'if stoned him until his sandals filled with blood, he did not strike back or curse them, but turned to prayer.
4. Offered total power to destroy the town via the Angel of the Mountains, he restrained himself and refused, choosing hope over vengeance.
5. His self-restraint is shown by his stated reasoning — hoping for future believers among the very people who had just injured him — rather than any desire for retribution.
6. This pattern of restraint under provocation continued for years until circumstances (such as the Hijrah) changed his situation, without him ever authorising indiscriminate revenge against Makkah during this period.
AO2 part (b), if any: (b) How can Muslims demonstrate patience and self-restraint today? [4] — Model answer approach: by not reacting to insults or provocation with anger or retaliation; by continuing to behave well even when treated unfairly by others (at school, work, or online); by using prayer and reflection to manage frustration rather than lashing out; by remembering that self-restraint under provocation is itself following the Prophet's example.
Examiner note: This question restricts the answer to the Makkan period specifically — the Conquest of Makkah happens later, in Madinan-period history, so including it here is off-topic and will not earn marks. Keep the Ta'if and pre-Hijrah persecution material distinct from the post-Hijrah Conquest material used in the other exam questions in this lesson.
